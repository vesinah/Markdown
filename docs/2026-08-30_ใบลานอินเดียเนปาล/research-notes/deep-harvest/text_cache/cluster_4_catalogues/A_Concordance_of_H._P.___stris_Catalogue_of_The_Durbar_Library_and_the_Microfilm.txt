the sceptical, but the references to the private papers of S. W. R. D. Bandaranaike and J. R.
Jayewardene would be more difficult to check, and it is greatly to the credit of those who made them

properly thought-out policy on anything seems to be justified. He was in such difficulties at the time
of his assassination that it is unlikely his government could have lasted much longer. His death, on
the other hand, beatified him.
This is assuredly not to deny that (as Dhanapala has indicated) many of his aspirations were good.
He did much to improve the opportunities for the Lankan citizen to vote for the government of his
choice. The 1956 elections themselves were among the most democratic ever held in Lanka. But
Bandaranaike's words in 1945, quoted by N. E. Weerasooria - "When in any democratic institution
you get too much interference from an elected body, by the Legislature or its Executive, with the
purely administrative machinery, then every kind of difficulty arises "-are a sad memorial to
unfulfilled intentions; the position of an M.P. in Lanka today still involves most undesirable powers
of influencing the administration. So also the instructions often given by Bandaranaike to the police
— with the best of motives at the time — to refrain from interference with political demonstrations
became most undesirable precedents. Certainly since 1970, elections have always brought increasing
violence with them.
The author's considered opinion of his subject seems to be summed up in an original fashion in
the remarkable photograph on the jacket cover, the source of which is not disclosed: it is merely said
to be "from the author's collection".

Sastri's catalogue offers detailed descriptions of many of these manuscripts but it is not always
systematic. Manuscripts are often referred to in two places, but without cross-references. Reinhold
Griinendahl's concordance remedies all that by listing all the manuscripts described by Sastri and
giving cross-references to the numbers by the National Archives, page references in the National
Archives' own multi-volumed catalogue, the Brhatsucipattra, reel numbers used on the NGMPP's
microfilms, as well as other citations by Sastri himself. An appendix lists all the manuscripts
mentioned in Sastri's article "New Manuscripts in Nepal" and where to find them in the catalogue.
An index lists the Archives' own running numbers in order with cross-references. There remain,
of course, the separate manuscript and name indices of Sastri's original two volumes. Sastri's
prefaces survey the manuscripts under subject headings (Vedas, grammar, medicine, dramas Indian
and Nepalese, anthologies, lexicons, fine arts, politics, erotics, epics, Puranas, Tantras Saivite and
Buddhist, and so on). Also included is Bendall's paper "The history of Nepal and surrounding
kingdoms (1000-1600 A.D.) ", originally published in the Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal in 1903
and included in Sastri's catalogue as a "Historical Introduction". Now superseded by the work of
Petech, Slusser, K. P. Malla, Dhanavajra Vajracarya and other Nepalese historians, it has a place in
Nepalese historiography since Bendall it was who discovered, and here announced, the existence of
the indispensable historical source, the Gopalarajavams'avalT.
It is now possible to locate all other important references, and in particular NGMPP reel numbers,
of a manuscript one has seen mentioned elsewhere. The editor Griinendahl has performed a great
service to the community of scholars (though his painstaking scholarship evidently did not extend
to having a native speaker weed certain quaint Germanisms out of his English!). It will be a long
time before there is a descriptive catalogue of the entire microfilm collection of the NGMPP (over
100,000 items). In the meantime this volume covering around 850 important manuscripts will be an
indispensable guide.
DAVID N. GELLNER
ESSAYS IN COMPARATIVE ALTAIC LINGUISTICS. By DENIS SlNOR. (Indiana University Uralic and Altaic
Series, Vol. 143.) pp. x, 454. Bloomington, Indiana, Indiana University Research Institute for Inner
Asian Studies, 1990.
What may be termed the "philological factor" has always rated higher in Inner Asian studies than
in almost any other comparable field of enquiry. The boundary between Altaic studies on the one
hand, and Inner Asian history on the other, has remained ill-defined, evidence perhaps, of the fields'
conceptual immaturity - or, conversely, of the robust common sense of their practitioners. The
legacy of this situation, however, has not always been a fortunate one: the twin banes of linguists
without history, and historians without language, have not been unknown in a field where the
possession of one discipline is little use without a more than working knowledge of the other.
The title given by their author to this gathering together of no fewer than thirty-five of his
contributions to Inner Asian studies leads the historically-minded reviewer to fear the worst, finding
himself in a world where historical reality disappears in a blizzard of Poppean asterisks. But - as we
might have expected - the title belies both the author and his approach to the subject. As Professor
Sinor, in a characteristically modest Preface and Apologia, observes (although those acquainted with
his output during the past half-century will not need reminding), he considers himself a historian and
not a linguist, and his work as a contribution to the clarification of a historical process.
