integrated with cellulose nanowhiskers

Palm leaf manuscripts (PLMs), as invaluable organic cultural heritage, are prone to embrittlement.
Clove oil (CO) has traditionally been used as a toughening agent, but its high volatility limits durability.
Herein, we develop a novel material comprising CO-controlled release nanocapsules, prepared via
Pickering emulsion templating and solvent evaporation, using cellulose nanowhiskers (CNW) as
stabilizers and polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) as the shell. This strategy reduces CO volatilization
by more than 11-fold. Applied to simulated PLMs, the material causes no visible alteration while
markedly enhancing tensile strength, fracture toughness, and elongation at break by 43.5%, 101.9%,
and 57.8%, respectively. Furthermore, aging tests show that the novel material maintains enhanced
mechanical properties of PLMs for 4 days, while CO alone loses them after 1 day. Characterizations
demonstrate that the controlled CO release, together with CNW reinforcement, provides both
enhanced performance and durability. This study presents a promising strategy for the preservation
of PLMs.
Palm leaf manuscripts (PLMs) were an important writing medium widely
used in South and Southeast Asia before the invention of paper.Historically,
PLMs were introduced into southwestern China along with the spread of
Buddhism. Until now, a large number of valuable ancient PLMs are still
preserved in Yunnan and Tibet, China. PLMs are made from palm species
such as Corypha umbraculifera, with mature leaves harvested, and then
processed through boiling, drying, and polishing, followed by inscribing or
writing text on their surfaces1–3. PLMs contain a wealth of textual content,
encompassing not only religious scriptures but also literature, history,
medicine,and astronomy, makingthemof immense historical,cultural,and
religious value. However, due to prolonged exposure to environmental
factors over time, PLMs are highly susceptible to deterioration, such as
embrittlement, which highlights the urgent need for appropriate toughen-
ing materials.
Natural plant essential oils, such as citronella oil, camphor oil, and
lemongrass oil, have long been used to toughen PLMs4. In the 1990s, Sur-
yawanshi et al. systematically evaluated the toughening performance of
variousnaturalplantessentialoilson PLMs5. Theyfound that cloveoil (CO)
improvedtheﬂexibilityofPLMsmoresigniﬁcantly,whichisattributed toits
light and dry nature, allowing for easier absorption by PLMs. Nevertheless,
due to its high volatility, CO provides only short-term effects and requires
repeated applications to maintain ﬂexibility6. Additionally, chemical
reagents such as glycerol have been explored as alternatives for toughening
PLMs. However, the volatility of glycerol can also cause PLMs to revert to a
brittle state upon evaporation7,8.
To address the limitation of volatility, encapsulation technology has
emerged as a promising strategy to enhance the stability and prolong the
effectiveness of volatile compounds by enclosing them within protective
matrices for sustained release9–11. Common encapsulation techniques
include emulsion templating, spray-drying, physical adsorption, and
molecular inclusion12. Among these, the emulsion templating approach has
attracted considerable attention due to its simplicity and high encapsulation
efﬁciency. This technique involves the use of stabilizers to emulsify active
compounds, forming emulsion templates, followed by the formation of

Methods
Materials
Cellulose nanowhiskers (CNW, 5 wt%) were purchased from Northern
Century (Jiangsu) Cellulose Materials Co., Ltd. Clove oil (CO, CP), poly-
methyl methacrylate (PMMA, MW ~ 35,000), chloroform (AR), hydrogen
peroxide (H2O2, 30%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, AR), formic acid (HCOOH,
AR), and ethyl acetate (EA, AR) were all purchased from Shanghai Sino-
pharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. and used without further puriﬁcation.
Modern palmleafmanuscripts(PLMs) were obtained fromXishuangbanna
Dai Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province. They were made from
Corypha umbraculifera palm leaves following the traditional preparation
methods.
Preparation of the target material
Aqueous and oil phase solutions were ﬁrst prepared. The aqueous
phase was prepared by adding 2.5/5/10 g of CNW slurry to a beaker
containing 27.5/25/20 g of deionized water, followed by ultrasonic
treatment for 10 min to obtain a CNW dispersion. The oil phase was
prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of PMMA and 0.5/1/2/3 g of CO in 10 g of
chloroform with continuous stirring for 1 h to ensure complete dis-
solution of PMMA to form a homogeneous oil phase. The aqueous and
oil phase solutions were then mixed and emulsiﬁed using a high-speed
homogenizer at 12,000/16,000/20,000 RPM for 4 min to form a stable
Pickering emulsion. The resulting Pickering emulsion was then stirred
for 12 h to allow gradual evaporation of chloroform. After stirring, the
mixture was further heating-stirred in a water bath at 80 °C for 30 min
to ensure complete evaporation of chloroform, yielding a white
emulsion. The emulsion was then subjected to vacuum ﬁltration to
collect the precipitate, which was redispersed in deionized water at a
concentration of 10 mg/mL to obtain the target material. This resulting
dispersion was subsequently used for the toughening treatment
of PLMs.
Preparation of control samples
Controlsamples,includingCNW-CO and PMMA/CNW,werepreparedto
verify the effects of each component in the formation of the target material.
The general procedure involved preparing aqueous and oil phases sepa-
rately, followed by homogenization and solvent evaporation, aligned with
the preparation process of the target material. Speciﬁc formulations and
differences are detailed below.
CNW-CO: The difference is that 2 g of CO was dissolved in chloro-
form, with no PMMA added to the oil phase.
PMMA/CNW: The difference is that 0.5 g of PMMA was dissolved in
chloroform with no CO added.
Composition and dispersion properties
The infrared spectra were collected using a Thermo Nicolet iS50 Fourier-
transform infrared spectrometer in the range of 4000–600 cm−1. Solid
samples were examined via an attenuated total reﬂectance (ATR) accessory,
with each spectrum acquired at a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 scans. To
further explore their compositional characteristics, ultraviolet-visible
absorption spectra were recorded using a Hitachi U-3900H ultraviolet-
visible spectrophotometer (UV-Vis) over the 200–600 nm wavelength
range. These measurements were conducted in solution form, with ethanol
used as the background solvent for CO, ethyl acetate for PMMA, and
deionized water for all other samples.
The dispersion properties of materials, including particle size and zeta
potential, were assessed with the use of Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90.
Microstructure
The microscopic morphology of the target material dispersed in water was
observed under an Olympus BX51 optical microscope. A Tescan Mira SEM
was employed to investigate the microstructure of the target material. For
sample preparation, a drop of the dispersion was deposited on a silicon
wafer, thoroughly dried, and then sputter-coated with gold. Images were
acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Fine structural features of the
target material were visualized by FEI Tecnai Spirit TEM. The particle sizes
in the SEM and TEM images were measured using the image analysis
software ImageJ v1.54f.
Thesurfacemicrostructuralchanges of thetoughened simulated PLMs
were also examined using the Tescan Mira SEM. Prior to observation, the
samples were thoroughly dried and coated with gold. To observe the cross-
sectional morphology of the simulated PLMs treated with the target
material, freshly impregnated samples were immersed in liquid nitrogen for
10 min and then fractured rapidly to expose a ﬂat cross-section. After
thorough drying and gold sputter-coating, SEM was conducted.

Preparation of simulated PLMs
The preparation of simulated PLMs was based on the method we previously
reported44. First, a peroxyformic acid solution was prepared by mixing
hydrogen peroxide with formic acid in a molar ratio of 1:1, and adding 2 wt
% sulfuric acid as a catalyst, which also accelerated the hydrolysis of cellu-
lose.Afterstirringevenly,itwasdilutedwithdeionizedwatertomakea10 v/
v% solution. Then, the purchased modern PLMs were cut into small pieces.
After wiping their surfaces with ethanol, the samples were boiled in deio-
nized water for 1 h to remove air trapped inside. The treated samples were
thenimmersedin the prepared peroxyformic acid solution at 50 °C for 24 h.
Subsequently, the sampleswere placed in a 0.5% sodium hydroxide solution
for 5 min to neutralize acidic substancesand facilitate the detachment of the
parenchyma tissue. Finally, the samples were repeatedly rinsed in deionized
water to remove residual chemicals. Once the chemical treatments were
ﬁnished, the PLMs were held between wooden boards and dried in an oven
at 105 °C for 48 h. Afterwards, they were conditioned in a chamber set to
23 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 3% RH for 12 h. After undergoing the above treatments,
the simulated PLMs can be obtained.
Toughening of simulated PLMs
A 10 mg/mL dispersion of the target material was added to a petri dish, and
the simulatedPLMs were immersed in it. The immersion was maintainedat
room temperature for 6 h to allow thorough penetration of the dispersion
into the samples. After soaking, the samples were pressed with lead
paperweights wrapped in acid-free paper and transferred to a constant
temperature and humidity chamber set at 23 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 3% RH for
48 h. After the above treatments, the samples were ready for subsequent
testing. Toughening treatments using CO, CNW, CNW-CO, and PMMA/
CNW werecarriedoutfollowingthesameprocedure.Inaddition,untreated
PLMs (UT) were used as controls.

Results
Synthesis of the target material
The synthesis of the target material involves two steps (Fig. 1): (i) the
emulsiﬁcation process, which is responsible for the preparation of the
Pickering emulsion templates, and (ii) the solvent evaporation process,
during which the capsule shell is formed. Initially, a mixed oil phase is
prepared by dissolving CO and PMMA in chloroform, while the aqueous
phase is obtained by ultrasonically dispersing CNW uniformly in deionized
water. These two phases are then mixed and emulsiﬁed using a high-speed
homogenizer to form an O/W Pickering emulsion stabilized by CNW. At
this stage, a white emulsion with relatively large droplets is formed. Fol-
lowing this, continuous stirring facilitates the evaporation of chloroform,
leadingtotheformationofthecapsuleshell.Aftercompleteevaporation,the
resulting mixture is vacuum ﬁltered to collect the precipitate, which is then
redispersed in deionized water. This results in a signiﬁcant reduction in
droplet size compared to the Pickering emulsion, conﬁrming the successful
preparation of the target material.
To further investigate the impact of synthesis conditions on the ﬁnal
product, we prepared the target materials under varying emulsiﬁcation
shear rates, CNW contents, and PMMA-to-CO mass ratios (Table S1). It
was found that shear rate and CNW content primarily affect the emulsiﬁ-
cation stage, with higher shear rates and CNW contents resulting in smaller
Pickering emulsion templates, but having minimal effect on the morphol-
ogyoftheﬁnalproducts(Figs.S1–S4).However,as theCO-to-PMMAmass
ratio increased, both the droplet size of the Pickering emulsion template and
the ﬁnal particle size progressively increased (Figs. S5 and S6). In addition, a
PMMA/CNW composite material without encapsulated CO and a CNW-
stabilized CO Pickering emulsion without PMMA shell formation (CNW-
CO) were synthesized as control samples.

two sharp peaks at 1509 and 1607 cm⁻¹ are observed, assigned to the C = C
stretching vibrations in the aromatic ring of eugenol, the main active

morphologyofthetargetmaterial(Fig.3).ForCNW,itexhibitsaneedle-like

CNW-CO, CNW stabilizes the CO droplets, acting as their “shell”. Upon

shows spherical particles with a diameter of 876 nm, where the needle-like

3c). These ﬁndings indicate that CNW fails to encapsulate CO in the solid

1.3 μm to 4.2 μm (Fig. 3d). High-magniﬁcation image of individual particles

structure (Fig. 3f)51. However, the target material displays a uniform sphe-
rical morphology, with particle diameters ranging from 300 nm to 1.4 μm
(Fig. 3g). In the high-magniﬁcation image, the surface of spherical particles
appears smooth, distinct from the rough surface of PMMA/CNW. Addi-
tionally, the ﬁbrous CNW is observed forming a network-like linkage
between the spherical particles and spreading across the substrate (Fig. 3h).
This morphology was consistently observed across multiple batches,
demonstrating good reproducibility of the target material (Fig. S7). TEM
image of a single particle shows a distinct core-shell capsule structure with a
diameter of 332 nm and a shell thickness of 45 nm (Fig. 3i), which is
identiﬁed as a CO-loaded PMMA nanocapsule. Additionally, dynamic light
scattering (DLS) measurement also shows a similar particle size distribution
trend (Fig. S8a, c–e). The larger size of CNW-CO and PMMA/CNW is
attributed to the fact that Pickering emulsion droplets are typically larger
than individual solid particles52. Based on the above results, the target
material is conﬁrmed to consist of core-shell nanocapsules, in which CO is
encapsulated within a PMMA shell, while CNW forms a continuous net-
work upon solvent evaporation, effectively linking and integrating the
nanocapsules (Fig. 3h).
During the solvent evaporation process in material synthesis, samples
stirred at different times were collected for SEM observation to elucidate the
formation mechanism of the target material. At 1 h, micron-sized spherical
particles (>5 μm) with rough and ﬁbrous surfaces are observed, along with
small spherical protrusions (~100 nm) on the surface (Figs. 3j and S8a).
These micron-sized spherical particles are Pickering emulsion droplets
stabilized by CNW. After 6 h of stirring, the particles shrink to approxi-
mately 4.5 μm in diameter and exhibit a wrinkled surface. The protrusions
grow into smooth-surfaced spherical particles of approximately 300 nm
(Figs. 3k and S8b). Based on the above observations, the formation of the
targetmaterialishypothesizedtofollowa“squeezingout”mechanism.First,
CNW stabilizes the mixed oil phase (CO and PMMA dissolved in chloro-
form) to form Pickering emulsion templates. The evaporation of chloro-
form from the mixed oil phase induces phase separation. Owing to the
polarity being close to that of the aqueous phase, PMMA becomes enriched
at the oil-water interface and tends to maximize the interface area25,53. This
leads to the solidiﬁcation of PMMA into spherical capsules, which are
gradually “squeezed out” from the Pickering emulsion templates’ surface,
forming PMMA-encapsulated CO nanocapsules (Fig. 3l).
Encapsulation properties and release proﬁles
To evaluate the encapsulation efﬁciency and loading efﬁciency, which are
essentialindicatorsforassessingtheeffectivenessofCOencapsulationinthe
target material, UV-Vis was employed. First, a standard curve was estab-
lished by analyzing the absorbance at 281 nm of CO solutions at various
concentrations (Fig. S10). Based on this curve, the CO content in four
formulations of the target material (prepared with PMMA-to-CO mass
ratios of 0.5:0.5, 0.5:1, 0.5:2, and 0.5:3, respectively, see Supporting Infor-
mation for details) was quantiﬁed. The corresponding encapsulation efﬁ-
ciency and loading efﬁciency were calculated. As summarized in Table S2,
the 0.5:2 formulation has the highest encapsulation efﬁciency and loading
efﬁciency of 50.8% and 35.2%, respectively, representing an optimal balance
for effective encapsulation. At the highest PMMA-to-CO mass ratio (0.5:3),
however, excess CO surpasses the encapsulation capacity of PMMA and is
removed during the puriﬁcation process, thereby reducing both encapsu-
lation efﬁciency and loading efﬁciency in the ﬁnal product.
Since sustained release is critical for the long-lasting toughening effect
for PLMs, the release proﬁles of all four formulations were further investi-
gated at room temperature with deionized water as the release medium54,55.
As shown in Fig. 4a, all formulations exhibit similar CO release patterns,
characterized by a rapid release within the ﬁrst 2 h followed by a slower
sustained release phase41,54,56–58. Speciﬁcally, the cumulative release rates at
2 h are 80.9%, 65.3%, 56.4%, and 51.3% for 0.5:0.5, 0.5:1, 0.5:2, and 0.5:3
formulations, respectively. By 4 h, their total release increases to 93.9%,
70.6%, 63.2%, and 61.3%, respectively. The slower release rate observed in
samples with higher CO content is mainly due to the larger capsule size,
which results in a reduced speciﬁc surface area.
To further clarify the CO release mechanism, we ﬁt the cumulative
release rate versus time curves using the Peppas–Sahlin and Ritger–Peppas
models (ﬁtting parameters are summarized in Table S3). As shown in Fig.
4b, the Peppas–Sahlin model offers an excellent ﬁt, with R2 values of 0.997,
0.999, 0.997, and 0.996 for the 0.5:0.5, 0.5:1, 0.5:2, and 0.5:3 formulations,
respectively. In this model, the diffusion constant k1 and the erosion con-
stant k2 quantify the contributions of Fickian diffusion and Case-II
relaxation, respectively. k1/k2 > 1 indicates diffusion-dominated release,
whereas k1/k2 < 1 implies erosion-dominated release43,54. Obviously, the k1/
k2 ratios for all four formulations all exceed 1, conﬁrming that Fickian
diffusion is the predominant mechanism for CO release. For comparison,
the simpler Ritger–Peppas model also yields acceptable ﬁts, with corre-
sponding R2 values of 0.996, 0.986, 0.979, and 0.983. The release exponent n
remains consistently below 0.45 across all formulations (Fig. S11), further
supporting a diffusion-dominated release process.
Considering the different temperatures that may be encountered in the
storage environment of PLMs, we further examined the cumulative release
proﬁles of the 0.5:2 formulation at 4 °C, 23 °C, and 40 °C. The results
indicate that temperature has only a minimal effect on the cumulative
release rate (Fig. S12). Moreover, based on the ﬁtting parameters (Table S4),
the release mechanism of CO remains Fickian diffusion under all tested
temperatures. Furthermore, the retention of CO in the optimized 0.5:2
formulation was also evaluated. Over a 7-day period, this material shows
only a 2.91% weight loss, whereas pure CO decreases by 33.1%. This
demonstrates that the encapsulated CO is over 11 times more stable than
CO, highlighting its excellent encapsulation stability (Fig. S13). The nearly
linear trend observed in the weight loss further suggests that this enhanced
stability could be maintained over a longer period. Similar trends of stability
enhancement through encapsulation have also been reported in previous
studies55,59,60.

integrity and resistance to mechanical stress. In addition, a portion of the

interfaces. Moreover, the embedded nanocapsules enable the sustained
release of CO into the PLMs, thereby prolonging its toughening effective-
ness. The dual mechanism, structural reinforcement from the surface ﬁlm
and sustained toughening through controlled CO release, synergistically
improves the toughness, strength, and ﬂexibility of PLMs, and ensures these
enhancements are maintained after accelerated aging.
In parallel with these mechanical improvements, it is important to
assess the potential impact of the material on the biochemical stability of
PLMs. The target material used in this study is not expected to compromise
the biochemical stability. CNW is chemically compatible with the cellulosic
composition of PLMs, and essential oils such as CO have been traditionally
used as toughening agents without harmful effects4,20,77,78. PMMA, primarily
serving as a capsule shell, is chemically stable under normal conditions.
Although long-term UV exposure can lead to photodegradation, this risk is
effectively mitigated in typical storage environments38,79,80. Overall, the
combination of CNW, CO, and PMMA is unlikely to introduce harmful
degradation pathways.
Discussion
In this study, we addressed the high volatility of CO by developing a
controlled-release toughening material, prolonging its effectiveness in
toughening PLMs. The material comprises CO-loaded PMMA nano-
capsules integrated with CNW, synthesized via Pickering emulsion tem-
plating followed by solvent evaporation. Speciﬁcally, the oil phase,
composed of CO and PMMA dissolved in chloroform, is stabilized by
CNW in the aqueous phase. As chloroform evaporates, PMMA solidiﬁes
at the oil-water interface, forming CO-loaded PMMA nanocapsules that
are gradually “squeezed out”. Under the optimized formulation, the
nanocapsules exhibit a diameter of ~396 nm and a shell thickness of
~45 nm. This material achieves an encapsulation efﬁciency of 50.8% and a
loading efﬁciency of 35.2%. CO in the as-synthesized is sustainedly
released via Fickian diffusion. CO volatilization rate in the material is
reduced by more than 11 times compared to pure CO. When applied to
simulated PLMs, the material causes no noticeable changes in appearance
but signiﬁcantly improves mechanical properties, including tensile
strength, fracture toughness, and elongation at break increase by 43.5%,
101.9%, and 57.8%, respectively. In addition, the treated simulated PLMs
withstand bending stress without cracking, outperforming the effect of
CO and CNW. After 4 days of moist-heat aging, the treated simulated
PLMs retain 20.7% higher tensile strength and 48.7% greater toughness
than UT, whereas CO-treated samples lose their toughening effect after
just 1 day. These improvements are attributed to a dual mechanism: a
continuous surface ﬁlm formed by CNW reinforces the substrate and
enhances toughening performance, while embedded CO-loaded nano-
capsules suppress volatilization and enable sustained CO release, thereby
prolonging the toughening effect. This study highlights a nanocapsule-
based controlled-release strategy as a promising approach for the durable
toughening of PLMs.
