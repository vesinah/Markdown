RICHARD SALOMON (with contributions by ANDREW GLASS):
Two Gāndhārī Manuscripts of the Songs of Lake Anavatapta
(Anavatapta-gāthā). British Library Kharoṣṭhī Fragment 1 and Senior
Scroll 14.
(Gandhāran Buddhist Texts.) xxv, 444 pp. Seattle and London:
University of Washington Press, 2008. ISBN 978 0 295 98905 1.
doi:10.1017/S0041977X11000516
The last two decades have seen the arrival of manuscript material from Afghanistan
and nearby areas which has the potential to enhance our knowledge of the develop-
ment of Buddhist literature in the period from the second century BC to the second cen-
tury AD. Although other material has surfaced since, the UK is fortunate in possessing
two of the earliest collections of such material to come to light: the British Library and
Robert Senior collections. The study of both these collections has been organized by
Richard Salomon and his team at the University of Washington, and for an outline of
their progress and achievements to date it is sufficient to refer the reader to their excel-
lent websites, containing a bibliography and a preliminary version of an online diction-
ary of nearly all the Gāndhārī materials which have come to light to date, as well as
additional resources to assist work in the Kharoṣṭhī script and Gāndhārī language.
See: http://depts.washington.edu/ebmp/ and http://gandhari.org/a_catalog.php.
The series of published volumes of which this is the fifth (or sixth if we include
an initial general account of the British Library collection) has been produced to a
high standard and the present volume is no exception. Those involved are to be con-
gratulated for the care they have taken to present the materials in a form that will be
accessible to future scholars and open to subsequent evaluation and emendation.
Each volume has included, when necessary, a careful description of the relevant
manuscript material as well as separate detailed accounts of the palaeography, ortho-
graphy, phonology, morphology and syntax employed by the particular scribe for
the manuscript in question.
It is clear that this is producing significant progress in understanding the Gāndhārī
language, as utilized by Buddhists writing in this period. There are, however, pro-
blems – because we do not know the source dialect of Middle Indian from which
those texts not composed in Gāndhārī would have been translated or transposed.
Indeed we do not know whether we should think of a number of spoken dialects in
which Buddhist literature was orally preserved in different parts of India or rather
of a written κοινή common to most of the subcontinent, containing words and
forms of disparate origins, as we find in inscriptions. Such a shared written language
with non-standardized spelling would be the ancestor of the written languages used by
the various Buddhist schools at a later date, especially for their canonical literature.
Neither do we know to what extent there was a specifically Buddhist mode of the
Gāndhārī language, although it is clear that at least some words have preserved a
form deriving from further East (e.g. bhikhu as against bhikṣave, both of which occur).
It is difficult to date the texts in question. Some were probably composed in the
north-west, probably in Gāndhārī, possibly by the scribe who wrote down the manu-
script we have. Others are unlikely to be of north-western origin, but we do not
know if the manuscript we have was transposed directly from another dialect or cop-
ied from an earlier Gāndhārī version. The volume reviewed here contains material

from both the British Library and Robert Senior collections. The latter is fairly
securely dated to the first half of the second century AD with an inscription on the
find pot almost certainly in year 12 of Kaṇiṣka and confirmatory radio-carbon dat-
ing. The texts in this collection were apparently written to order and so the manu-
scripts must be of approximately the same date. The situation is more difficult with
the British Library materials. Scandalously, these have still not been radio-carbon
dated, but it seems likely that they were deposited in the early first century AD.
However, there is no reason to suppose that the manuscripts (which seem in most
cases to have been heavily used) all date from the same time; some of them may
have been written considerably earlier.
The literary provenance of the texts edited and translated in this volume appears
more debatable. Dharmarakṣa translated a text (T. Vol. 4 No. 199) into Chinese in c.
304 AD. This text had 30 chapters, but was probably missing its final folio.
Dharmarakṣa appears not to have known the Indic name of this work; however,
an enlarged version (as Salomon convincingly argues) of the same work in 37 chap-
ters was later incorporated into the Bhaiṣajyavastu of the Mūlasarvāstivādavinaya
(MSV). That is extant in both Tibetan and Chinese translation and Heinz Bechert
was able to restore the title of *Anavataptagāthā from the former. No title is
given in the Gilgit MS of the Bhaiṣajyavastu which contains material from some
30 of the 37 chapters, but c. fifth-century fragments from Kïzïl which contain por-
tions from around 18 chapters indicate a title starting with An- or Av-. So we can
accept that the likely title for the work translated by Dharmarakṣa was indeed
*Anavataptagāthā (AG).
The Chinese translation of a Mahāsan.ghikavinaya refers to a “Lake Anavatapta
Sutra”, but this may not refer to the same work, as there is a Chinese translation (attr.
to Kāng Mèngxiáng but probably later) with an aberrant rendering of just the final
chapter. This might well be of Mahāsan.ghika origin. Salomon suggests that refer-
ences in one of the index scrolls of the Senior collection confirm the title, but
this seems doubtful. The index scroll refers to six aṇoḏatie (with a lacuna where
a seventh was probably mentioned). This would be for *anavataptikā, but that is
not evidence for a text named Anavataptagāthā, unless one is already convinced
of the existence of such a text. Rather, what we have evidence of here is a genre
of verse stories set in Lake Anavatapta where various of the 500 arhat disciples
tell the story of their past lives at the Buddha’s request.
This fits well with the emerging picture of the evolution of the group of genres of
Buddhist literature which can be loosely labelled as avadāna. What the Gāndhārī
volumes of avadāna-related material show us is that this literature is close to oral
composition. Some of the stories are presented in a very abbreviated form which
Salomon referred to in his introductory volume as “written supplements to oral
deliveries” – the presenter must creatively fill out the details with length and content
to suit the occasion. This evidences what must have been the situation at an earlier
date with the sutta literature and no doubt in the development of early Mahāyāna
literature too. Similar instructions to expand material along the lines of an estab-
lished model can also be found in abhidhamma texts of this period.
Salomon indicates that a comprehensive study of the Anavatapta literature,
although desirable, is beyond the scope of the volume reviewed here. He neverthe-
less includes some interesting comparisons of the different versions, enough to make
it clear that the history of these stories is quite complex. He does conclude that the
Gāndhārī version of this material represents “an independent text with similarities to
the other independent texts of the AG as preserved in the Turfan fragments and
especially in Dharmarakṣa’s translation, as opposed to the MSV versions”. The
Sanskrit texts have clearly been reworked from a Middle Indic base.

The situation is rather less clear with the surviving Pali material of this genre: the
late canonical Apadāna contains three apadānas of this kind, while the Theragāthā
incorporates a short section into the verses of Anuruddha and the later paracanonical
Nettippakaraṇa cites a number of verses apparently from the first chapter of an AG.
Salomon does not seriously attempt to address the relationship of the Pali material
either to the new Gāndhārī MSS or to the extant Sanskrit MSS. Clearly a new and
careful assessment is highly desirable. What is evident is that the Pali and Gāndhārī
passages share a common Middle Indic linguistic base and evidence an earlier stage
or stages in the development of this literature.
To conclude, this is an excellent piece of scholarship which provides exciting
new material for future study. As so often, the new evidence shows us that the earlier
situation was more complicated than we might have supposed.

Since this project partly misleads its intended global readership, a critical con-
structive review may assist deeper analysis. Law and religion is a hugely important
field nowadays, dominated in recent years by studies on Islamic issues. A volume
exploring Hindu dimensions is timely, but the framework chosen results in a some-
what forced marriage of Sanskrit-based Indology and law.
The introduction sets an ambitious agenda to distinguish “Hindu law” from
“Hinduism and law”. Since the whole book is built around this conceptual distinc-
tion (p. 7), preliminary definitions of Hinduism, dharma and law are crucial. The
heuristic utility of “Hinduism” is accepted as a family (p. 3) and “widespread
learned traditions” (p. 4) encourage greater incorporation of law and legal studies
into the study of Hinduism. “Hindu law” is then questionably presented as “a system
of religious law, analogous to other traditions such as Jewish, Islamic, or canon law”
(p. 6). Given ubiquitous pluralism, indeed “Hindu law would have been just one of
several co-existing legal or normative orders at any given place and time in India”
(p. 6). Explicitly, however, “a connection to Dharmaśāstra is the sine qua non of
categorizing a legal system or legal order as Hindu” (p. 6). This seems to exclude
much of what I and others call “Hindu law”. Text-based and elitist, this rigid
Indological definition excludes all kinds of secular and subaltern perspectives.
