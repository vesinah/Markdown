“Variant”, variation and pāṭha in Sanskrit
Abstract: Applying the notion of “variant” to Sanskrit texts is not self-evident. To
use the term “variation” seems preferable. The role of writing and manuscripts
was secondary in the transmission of Sanskrit texts, with some exceptions. The
examination of three important semantic nuances of the term pāṭha relates this
term to the utterance of texts. The fixation of the Ṛksaṃhitā hymnic corpus
through eleven modes of recitation, probably between 1200 and 800 BCE, was
done orally. At a much later period, textual variations on other texts are discussed
in the commentaries. The oral editorial process is recorded visually in the manu-
scripts. Poetic games, plagiarism, and adaptations of ritual prescriptions, among
others, testify that the intentional exercise of variation too was common. Con-
versely, hermeneutics allowed scholars and connoisseurs to establish semantic
variations without engaging in formal textual variations.

1 Introduction: variant or variation?
This chapter will discuss the notion of a variant in Sanskrit texts or, instead,
variation so as not to be overly influenced by Western modern philology. It will
not consider the numerous valuable works of literature in other Indian languages
(like Tamil and Pāli, for instance) nor those of Islamic traditions (however crucial
in the Indian subcontinent).1 The literature in Sanskrit, a language of non-Indian
origin, developed and was preserved in India. It dates back at least three mil-
lennia. Its oldest corpus, the Ṛksaṃhitā, was standardized for its phonetic and
grammar probably between the eighth and sixth centuries BCE and has been
transmitted to us almost unchanged. Pāṇini composed, probably as early as the
fourth century BCE, the Aṣṭādhyāyī, a sophisticated grammar of Sanskrit which

The variations of Sanskrit texts were, from a very early period, under close
observation by the users of this language, but for entirely different purposes than
those of modern Western philology, which uses the term “variant” in its peculiar
conception of textual criticism. As we will see, “reading,” another term of Western
textual criticism, cannot be applied indiscriminately to Sanskrit texts. The inten-
tion here is not to criticize that philology as a whole and its many valuable critical
editions but to insist on some limited aspects of textual variation as the multi-
secular Sanskrit literature treated it.

2.2 Epigraphic versus “literary” texts, writing and invariance
Renou observed that the use of Middle Indo-Aryan “appears in epigraphy before
Sanskrit: this is the great linguistic paradox of India.”21 The first epigraphic
records of Sanskrit passages date only from the first century BCE.22 This is com-
paratively long after epigraphic documents in Middle Indian languages had been
recorded on stone.23 This apparent paradox might be explained by the originally
selective and limited use of writing in India. The question is not that, general, of
the advent of this technique in the Indian subcontinent, but specifically of its
application to Sanskrit intellectual, literary, and religious texts and the textual
variations connected with their chirographic recording.24
Two categories of texts may be tentatively distinguished: on the one hand,
epigraphic and socially utilitarian documents,25 and the other, Sanskrit “literary”
texts. “Literary” refers here, for want of a more satisfactory appellation, to the
texts of religious or non-religious literature restricted to an erudite public. As for
epigraphic and utilitarian documents, they are administrative, notarial, commer-

an ephemeral material. A lapicide who was sometimes ignorant of reading the
script then engraved it (on stone or metal).28 This artisanal transfer could occa-

during which, again, interpretative oral and semantic variation could occur.
Sometimes, the inscription was recopied, in part or totality, in other inscriptions.

2.3 What meant reading?
The author of these lines does not know of any comprehensive study about the
manner, conditions, and psychology of apprehending and orally rendering the
visual documents that are written texts in ancient India. Such a study might be
impossible because of a lack of precise and historical information about the step-
by-step process of deciphering and interpreting written signs. As will be seen, the
meaning of Sanskrit terms such as paṭh, pāṭha is undecisive in dictionaries. It
appears that till the 20th century, the education of Sanskrit erudites (paṇḍita-s)
could last about twelve years from the youngest age. It started with memorizing
various texts through aural means: a grammatical text, a dictionary of synonyms,
one or several Vedic collections, gnomic verses, and main doctrinal points. Mental
and intellectual exercises and intense training (perpetuated later on) accompa-
nied this education.38 The mental recording and analyzing of literature was asso-
ciated with correctly pronounced language, not written signs.
Overall, scholars educated in this manner built a mentally internalized net-
work of grammatical, linguistic, and literary references. Their primary knowledge
did not lie in alphabetical training, that is, education in visual recognition of any
external collection of symbols. It does not mean that erudites systematically
avoided learning reading and writing. However, it is impossible to guess which
proportion of them learned these techniques and at which stage of education.
Acquiring the reading/writing ability probably came after the primary oral educa-
tion and, perhaps adventitiously, in parallel. Reading and writing was subsidiary
skill, helpful to a certain extent for practical purposes, and often independent

The verbal root paṭh is variously translated as “reading,” “repeating aloud,” “recit-

3.2 Shift to ideal invariance: the Ṛksaṃhitā as a verbal
monument bound with modes of recitation (pāṭha-s)
The collecting of Ṛksaṃhitā hymns, which are of varying ages and states of lan-
guage, could date to a period between 1200 and 800 BCE.85 At an undetermined
date, the question arose of fixing and preserving the collection for its transmis-
sion. This question is comparable to the problem of fixing large textual corpora
in other ancient and even recent societies. They adopted various methods. The
mnemonics employed to that effect used of sign objects either internal to the text
or external to the text and visual. The former included mere memorization and
memorization associated with metrics, music, stereotyped formulas, and recur-
ring elements, all directly associated with texts’ enunciation and pronunciation.
Thus in some Polynesian societies, public heralds preserved the oral heritage;
their memory had to be faultless, an error or a break in breath in the recitation
being considered a bad omen.86 African storytellers’ narratives included stereo-
typed formulas and refrains and were bounded by respect for obligatory episodes
(a change could result in the protest of listeners and the vengeance of supernatu-
ral forces), but included a large amount of improvisation, resulting in varia-
tions.87
Instances of mnemonics associated with external visual signs objects in non-
Indian societies are numerous: incised drawings on bark read from right to left,
necklaces of shells with significant order and colors (order of grains of various
colors),88 knots gathering cords of different colors in the quipu of Peru.89 Last but
not least, external sign objects include the many forms of “writing,” a memorized
technique using a limited number of visual signs to record and reactivate the text.
Such signs embody sounds or words. The apparent homogeneity of the notion of
writing masks the diversity of their functioning in connection with language.90

The task of copying texts also involved a certain amount of Indian “textual
criticism” or “editing”. The scribe, during his work, could indicate errors or what
would need correction after completion of the copy.149 In any case, the next step
after copying the text was to read aloud the completed text of the copy for its
revision and corrections.150 This point must be emphasized because modern au-
thors of critical editions in Sanskrit often rely on unrevised copied texts, that is,
manuscripts that are not complete or are mere drafts. The reading aloud of the
copy was performed either by the scribe himself or by an “utterer-aloud” (pāṭhaka,
vācaka, kathaka). Literature mentions the additional function of a reviser-editor
(grantha-dhāraka) who emended the texts. As mentioned, the first scribe could
also do the critical reading of the copy, sometimes during his copying, including
blank spaces or dash symbols when he could not grasp the passage. He sometimes
added a correction leaf that contained an omitted passage or corresponded to a
destroyed or lost leaf. This correction leaf bore a marginal title, truṭipatra (“leaf
(corresponding) to a cut (in the original)”) or śodhapatra (“correction leaf”). It
was sometimes copied from another hand. More often, all revisions were made
by the reviser (and thus recorded on the copy in another hand).
A textual illustration of such procedures is the mention of two learned func-
tions in the Jayākhyasaṃhitā, a Vaiṣṇava Pāñcarātra ritual manual probably com-
posed between 600 and 850. One is that of the “text expert” (śāstrajña) who per-
forms a kind of restoration of texts whose pāṭha-s are corrupt. Another is that of
the “keeper of the texts” (śāstra-dhāraka) who gathers the (textual) tradition and
preserves it with zeal.151 The Jayākhyasaṃhitā, however, in this context, only men-
tions texts (śāstra-s), not the physical notion of a manuscript.

4.3 Intentional alterations of texts for rites, playing poetic
games and complementing logical demonstrations
Variations were intentionally developed in different Sanskrit scholarly fields, like
those of Mīmāṃsā, poetry, and “New Logic” (navyanyāya). Mīmāṃsā is a system-
atic theory of Vedic ritual, including the analysis of the ritual injunctions. An
essential concept in this regard is ūha, “modification”. A particular modification
is that of a word in a ritual formula (mantra) to adapt it to another ritual context,
for instance, substituting the name of a god for another when the divine recipient
of the offering changes. The text of the formula is thus directly modified accord-
ing to the material practice.161
As for poetry, the Kāvyamīmāṃsā, a work of poetics by Rājaśekhara (late
ninth–early tenth century), mentions the variations of the poet on his text during
the creative process, plagiarism, and game variations based on famous poems.
Poetry is, above all, conceived as an oral performance whose two main aspects
are intonation (kāku) and recitation (pāṭha).162 The poet’s day begins with his
composing an oral draft which he memorizes. In the late afternoon, during group
exercises, he examines this morning’s draft alone or with others since he has
to revise the whole by distancing himself from feelings, which imposes (orally)
amending the draft. At night the poet subjects his text to further emendations in
the form of cuts, additions, and changes, ultimately up to the writing down of
the composed text.163 Later on, the written text will be reproduced in several
handwritten copies.164 It may be stressed at this point how the created poem is
above all a vocal gesture, if we may borrow this expression to the Western musi-
cal field,165 and that the written recording takes place at the end of the process
after the text of the day has been created.
In the meantime, the poet will have practiced an exercise in crafting varia-
tions based on famous poems. This exercise is the samasyāpūraṇa, literally ‘filling
(a verse) in colloquy’ (also called pādapūraṇa),166 one of the afternoon improvisa-
tion exercises practiced with fellow poets. Each poet takes as a starting point one
or more verses, or even half a verse, of a famous poem and orally completes it
with a variation of his or her own.167 One of the aims was to produce new verses

4.4 From variations in textual forms to semantic variations
It is perhaps inevitable at this point to hold in abeyance the simple idea of
“unique original” Sanskrit texts175 and the concomitant notion of “variant” and
to open them to broader questions: that of formal overall variations in the texts
and that of semantic hermeneutical variations.
Sanskrit literature abounds in various kinds of deliberate abridgments of
other works, doctrinal, epic, and others, for memorization, presenting the gist of
a theory, and other purposes. It also includes texts of the same title that differ in
extent, content, literary quality, or ambition. Such Western philological distinc-

5 Conclusion
Text is a cultural object artificially isolated from language. Its definition and re-
cording aim at an invariancy in some way (memorized, printed, written, oral,
machine-driven, or other). However, there never was nor is any perfectly undis-
putable record of a sound object.191 The meanings and forms of the text depend
on the perception of the author and hearer/reader and are beyond the record.
On the other hand, speaking of its variance is bound to refer to an ideal of
invariance. In this sense, the Ṛgvedic enterprise and the modern-day one have a
comparable approach.
There however seems to be not much common ground between the ideally
invariant speech object which is the Ṛksaṃhitā orally edited around three millen-
nia ago and the typographic visual critical editions of Sanskrit texts today with
their creation of new variants named “emendations” and “conjectures” and their
heavy reliance on the intermediate chirographic and visual stage of Indian manu-
scripts. Traditional Sanskrit culture and modern-day Indological culture contrast
as two different visions and systems of thought. While some Indian paṇḍita-s
could and can understand and master the critical attitude of Western textual
criticism, the reverse is probably impossible.

tional global sense of the history of a text often prevent us from understanding
well the nature of the Sanskrit text. They are also deeply at odds with the invent-

“variants” that are rejected (or placed in notes) by the modern critical editor are
often evidence of the principle of coexisting and diatopic variations, requires at
least some thought beyond the own limited knowledge of the modern academic
editors. Overcoming preconceived historical notions is an obvious step to take.
On the other hand, paradoxically, Sanskrit tradition could admirably preserve
an almost invariable text by mere mnemotechnics and without the help of manu-
scripts as exemplified by the Ṛksaṃhitā. The opposition between orality and writ-
ing does not exist in a culture where the uttering aloud of the text is a dominant
feature. This is not to deny the potential importance of manuscripts in certain
Sanskritist circumstances or milieus, as among Jain erudites, for instance, but
always with the main perspective of uttering aloud the text (Puṇyavijaya). The
transmission of texts in India was always closely connected with human groups
of different structures and values, but, in Sanskrit, the written record of the text
was not the center of gravity of the textual transmission. The analogy of the
artificiality and shortcomings of Western musical notation vis-à-vis musical per-
formance and transmission192 perhaps helps to understand the deep limitations
of scripts regarding the sonorous human elocution and transmission of Sanskrit
texts and the Sanskritic partial reluctance towards committing texts to visual
chirographic records.
As we mentioned, unedited/unrevised manuscripts do not seem to have more
value than first drafts. They do not necessarily reflect a significant historical stage
of the text. It is impossible to draw a general conclusion and we have probably to
accept that situations were different and that there is no homogeneous historical
direction regarding the use of manuscripts by ancient Indian scholars. This diver-
sity of situations directly challenges the blind application of principles of Western

textual criticism to Sanskrit texts. The Western notion of “variant” must be conse-
quently seriously challenged in the Sanskrit context. On the other hand, Indian
commentators had a definite notion of “textual criticism,” yet connected with a
synchronic view of what the text should be, not diachronic nor, of course, histori-
cal even though they at times distinguished older and venerable textual utteran-
ces from the new ones.
Whether one likes it or not, it is difficult to doubt the exceptional or at the
least striking character of Sanskrit among Indian and non-Indian languages. This
exceptionality lies in the constant dialectical relation of this language with an
exacting grammatical and lexical control from a Vedic time difficult to assess. This
endeavor was exemplified and cemented by human memory with the outstanding
monument of the Ṛksaṃhitā, a feat among other known oral corpora of humanity,
and often sat as a model for traditional Indian scholarships. Thence a Sanskrit
text for an Indian erudite is co-inherent and synchronic with an exacting com-
mand of such linguistic knowledge and later on of numerous traditional disci-
plines and stocks of texts and quotations as well. Sanskrit variance, its evaluation,
and control among ancient erudites were closely connected with that knowledge.
