Before
the advent of paper
in
India palm-
leaves were the main material on which writ-
ing was done particularly
in coastal
areas.
Palm-leaves as writing material was also
in
use in several other countries like Sri Lanka,
Thailand, Burma
, Lao, Indonesia and so on.
As
a result hundreds of thousands of palm-
leaf documents have come down to us in the
form of ancient heritage.
It
is our duty
to
save them from destruction and damage.
If
we follow some simple rules and take proper
precautions, the aim of preserving palm-leaf
manuscripts can be easily achieved.
In this
booklet, written by Anupam Sah, the principles
of saving palm-leaf manuscripts from being
destroyed are described. We hope that
it will
be useful in saving palm-leaf manuscripts not
only in India but in other countries as well.
In our mission to conserve palm leaf manu-
scripts NORAD has been extremely helpful
and so has been Japan Foundation Asia centre.
We are grateful to them for their continuous
support.

of years and palm leaf writing skill
is

HOW ARE PALM LEAF
MANUSCRIPTS MADE ?
Varieties of palm-leaf
There are many varieties of
palm-trees. However, the
leaves of only a few have
been used for writing.
The most widely used were
Borassus flabellifer Linn
(the palmyra palm).
Corypha umbraculifera Linn
(taiipot palm, fan palm) and
Corypha taliera Roxb.
In India, palm leaves are
known as
Tal,
Sritala;
in
Thailand as Lam:
in
Srilanka as Ola.
Tender
green
leaves of different
varieties
of palm
trees
are selected
and cut. These cut
leaves
are
then
dried
in
a
controlled manner
and seasoned.
The
leaves
may
be
smoked, boiled
in
paddy husk, buried
under
silt, fumi-
gated
in
kilns,
rubbed
smooth
over
wooden
rollers, etc in order
to
prepare
them
to
be
written
on.
The
seasoned
leaves
are
cut
to
the
required
size
and
often
a hole
or
two
are
punched
in them
through
which
a
cord
is passed
to
tie the bundle. The
writing
and
illustrations
are
incised
or
scratched
on
the
surface
of
the
palm leaves using
a
sharp
pointed
metal stylus. These
incisions are then
made
visible
by
usually
rubbing
into them carbon
black ink.

The text and illustrations are also
sometimes painted on the palm
leaf with a brush.
The leaves are then strung
to-
gether.
Sometimes the leaves are
stitched together to get a
bigger surface.
An Orissan artisan
grinding cinnabar,
a mineral,
to prepare red pigment
known locally as
Hingula
The various leaves are then sandwiched
between wooden boards which
are tied in place with the help
of a cord in order to maintain
the shape of the palm leaves
when in storage. These wooden
boards are sometimes painted and
shaped into various forms.

Light bulbs inside closed showcases heat and dry
up the air causing the manuscript to become brittle
and break easily. Remove these bulbs.
Manuscripts should be displayed at a light inten-
sity of not more than 40 lux. Use a lux meter to
measure this light intensity.
The Lux meter
should be placed parallel

If light intensity
is higher than 40 lux. bring
it

by dimming them using
a dimmer switch. Light

distance between the light source and the
manu-
scripts on display.
Sunlight and tubelight weakens the manuscript
because the ultraviolet rays damage the leaves.
Block sunlight by closing windows or by putting
curtains on windows or over objects
Ultraviolet filters can be put over window panes
and tubelights to cut off these harmful rays
Zinc oxide or titanium dioxide, which absorb UV
rays, should be used to paint the walls and ceilings
and the light from fluorescent tubes can be re-
flected off them.

HOW TO PROTECT
MANUSCRIPTS FROM DAMAGE
DUE TO TEMPERATURE AND
RELATIVE HUMIDITY
1
Keep temperature and relative humidity constant
as much
as possible by
keeping the manuscripts wrapped in destarched thick cotton cloth in an inner
room and keep moisture absorbent materials such
as cotton curtains and
wooden furniture around collections. These materials (buffers) absorb and
release moisture slowly thus decreasing the harmful effects of fluctuations.
2
In tropical countries, temperature and relative humidity are on the higher
side. Air-conditioners
if used should be functional 24 hours a day, 365 days
a year.
Constant switching air-conditioners on and off causes sharp fluctua-
tions which damage manuscripts more than when they are stored without air
conditioning.
3
Keep tissue paper between leaves which have paintings on them to avoid
damage due to abrasion, and also as a buffer.
4
Temperature and relative humidity can be measured using
a dry and wet
thermometer and can be recorded using a thermohygrograph.
These records
over a period of atleast one year will give an idea of the rate and extent of
fluctuations and then corrective steps can be taken.
5
Regulate an even movement of visitors.
6
Avoid water accumulation near collections

CONCLUSION
If we become concerned about our manuscripts, we will perhaps be able to care
for them better. Manuscripts in private possession are often in a better condition
because they
are cared for with
a personal touch.
Institutions which
start
collecting thousands of manuscripts now also have
a serious responsibility of
ensuring their upkeep.
The public, the art conservators, the scholars and policy
makers must support these institutions and help them to look after these collections
properly.
