Its use spanned from the medieval era into the nineteenth century, resulting in millions of manuscripts. These manuscripts encompass knowledge from various disciplines, and they deteriorated over time due to aging and environmental conditions. Hence, the digitization of palm leaf manuscripts is crucial for the preservation and dissemination of their rich knowledge. To improve the digitization performance, the line level segmentation and recognition are recommended over character level. Skewed lines, varied writing styles, and deterioration make line segmentation challenging, further compounded by the lack of diverse and publicly available datasets. To address this gap, we introduce LeafOCR-Line, a dataset for palm leaf text line segmentation, consisting of 1710 text line masked manuscripts with corresponding deterioration levels for each manuscript. Furthermore, extensive experiments demonstrate the high quality and practical utility of the proposed dataset. LeafOCR-Line will be publicly available as a valuable resource for advancing palm leaf manuscript digitization research. Computer science Information technology Background & SummaryPeople have used different writing media before the widespread use of paper; palm leaf was one among them. Hence, these manuscripts represent invaluable cultural heritage but exist in a severely deteriorated state due to aging and environmental exposure. The degradation of these documents is leading to the irreversible loss of precious historical knowledge1. Additionally, with each passing day, we discover that the knowledge system of our ancestors was far more advanced than we think; such knowledge provides information into many fields of discovery and insights into the life and thinking process of our ancestors. For effective utilization of this knowledge, these need to be made accessible in digital format. Many governmental and non governmental agencies are putting in their efforts to collect, preserve, and digitize these palm leaf manuscripts from across the country. However, employing technology to aid the digitization can fast track the process. This can help reduce the cost while ensuring the quality of conversion.A typical technology assisted digitization workflow begins with the collection of manuscripts from various repositories2. The collected images are then pre processed to enhance contrast and reduce noise. Following this, objects are segmented from the images. In document images, objects are lines, words, and characters. Finally, the segmented texts are recognized using machine learning or deep learning algorithms. However, due to the historical nature of the documents and challenges associated with their preservation, each document has a different level of deterioration; some are minimally deteriorated, some moderately, and others severely. Each level requires different techniques. Due to these deteriorations and the writing style of these historical documents, it is not practical to segment the individual characters and recognize them. Hence, for palm leaf documents that are moderately to highly deteriorated, line wise text recognition is a more suitable and effective approach compared to character wise recognition. However, to perform recognition at the line level, images must first be segmented using a line segmentation technique. This requires labeled images, but the available dataset is insufficient in terms of both the number of images and their diversity.Palm leaf line segmentation also encounters numerous additional challenges. Artifacts caused by stains or fungi can resemble text and confuse segmentation algorithms3. In Fig. 1, the black pixels within the red box are actually artifacts, not character pixels. Nevertheless, the algorithm might mistakenly interpret them as part of the text. Additionally, some images suffer from faded ink, which further complicates the task by making it difficult to identify text line boundaries. Moreover, irregular layouts resulting from non uniform line spacing, multi column writing, and skewed text lines make segmentation a difficult task. Furthermore, punch holes and tears in the image break the continuity of line variations in script and writing styles, such as differences in size, slant, and overlapping characters, also affect the process. Complex backgrounds further complicate the task. Some images have poor contrast, making it difficult to identify the presence of text in the document. To add to that, languages like Malayalam include compound characters formed by combining two distinct letters. Many of the characters feature loops and curves in their design, and certain elements are written in positions above other characters. Due to the wide variation in individual handwriting styles, these features often result in significant spacing above or below the characters4. This further increases the challenges associated with segmentation. Lastly, the available dataset is limited in both the number and diversity of images, making standardized evaluation and comparison difficult. Even though some historical paper document based datasets are publicly available, due to the unique characteristics of palm leaf manuscripts, including their background color, writing style, and deteriorations, those datasets are not sufficient for palm leaf manuscript line segmentation. Furthermore, although some work has been reported on text line segmentation of palm leaf images, the ICFHR 2018 dataset remains one of the few publicly available resources for direct download5.Fig. 1Full size imageVisual samples of Artifacts Misinterpreted as Text (a–c) Representative examples.The dataset ‘LeafOCR-Line’6, presented in this paper, has been developed to improve line segmentation accuracy and efficiency, thereby streamlining the overall text recognition process in palm leaf manuscript digitization. The proposed dataset is larger and includes annotations for document quality levels, which enhances its suitability for deep learning applications and for evaluating performance under realistic medium-to-severe deterioration conditions commonly observed in palm leaf manuscripts. LeafOCR-Line uses line level annotations without explicit character boundary information, the extracted objects are continuous line regions during segmentation. During the recognition phase, these continuous line regions supports the model in learning sequence-to-sequence mappings. This method is especially effective when characters are fragmented, partially missing, or lack clear boundaries, as frequently observed in deteriorated palm leaf manuscripts. This labeling strategy enables the model to learn both the locations and identities of characters simultaneously during the recognition phase.Prior studies indicate that line segmentation strategies generalize across groups of structurally similar scripts rather than being strictly dependent on individual scripts7. Therefore, LeafOCR-Line comprising only Malayalam script is sufficient to support cross script generalization within this structural group. In this work, Malayalam is selected as the representative script due to three key characteristics: (a) its structural similarity of multiple scripts, (b) the extensive availability of collections, and (c) distinctive Indic script characteristics, which make it particularly suitable for evaluating line segmentation in palm leaf manuscripts.The Malayalam script demonstrates notable structural and visual similarities with the Tamil, Tigalari, and Grantha scripts. Historically, Vatteluttu was used to write the native Malayalam language and originated from the Tamil Brahmi script. The Grantha script, from which Malayalam is directly derived, provided characters for Sanskrit phonemes, while Vatteluttu was used for indigenous sounds8,9. Tigalari, regarded as Malayalam’s sister script and used for Tulu, is also derived from Grantha. Both Tigalari and Grantha were extensively used for writing Sanskrit. Owing to these shared characteristics, a model trained for the segmentation of heritage documents like palm leaf manuscripts can generalize effectively to other structurally similar scripts10,11. This structural similarity enables robust cross script generalization within this manuscript domain. Consequently, the LeafOCR-Line palm leaf manuscript dataset, which consists of Malayalam script, can effectively support digitization efforts for all related scripts by assisting line segmentation task.Malayalam is among the 22 scheduled languages and 11 classical languages of India, with over 35 million speakers12. Moreover, major repositories across the state of Kerala in India, such as the Oriental Research Institute at the University of Kerala, various monastic institutions, and numerous private collections, collectively house tens of thousands of Malayalam manuscripts. The scale of these collections underscores the need for a focused and dedicated approach to the digitization of Malayalam palm leaf manuscripts, in which text line segmentation is a crucial component.Additionally, most Indic scripts follow a three zone system. Base characters are positioned on the middle zone, while matras typically appear in the upper or lower zones13. These matras appear vertically beyond the main body of the character. These horizontal and vertical stacking patterns contribute to the increased visual complexity of the script. When writing on media such as palm leaves, which have limited vertical space, writers maximized text density by employing tightly packed lines with minimal spacing. This minimal spacing causes the lower matras of one line to overlap with the upper matras of the following line, making it difficult to determine the correct association of strokes. These complex shapes often lead to segmentation errors. Malayalam, as an Indic script, exhibits these characteristics, which are also common in related scripts. Additionally, in the context of automated line segmentation for these manuscripts, several factors are significant: line geometry (horizontal, curved, or irregular), text background contrast, texture and degradation patterns, and region based boundaries defined by polygon annotations rather than character level labels. Because the ground truth utilizes polygon based line regions instead of character level polygons, the model learns to detect text line boundaries as continuous spatial regions, independent of the script. This approach further supports generalization across scripts with similar structural layouts.Although researchers have previously worked on palm leaf digitization14,15,16,17, LeafOCR-Line is expected to further improve the process and be widely adopted by the research community. The main characteristics of LeafOCR-Line are: The widely used public dataset, ICFHR20185, contains only 407 labeled images, making it unsuitable for deep learning based training. In contrast, LeafOCR-Line includes 1710 manuscripts from 20 different palm leaf bundles. Palm leaf documents exhibit varying levels of deterioration. A single processing technique cannot be applied to all levels of degradation18. Less deteriorated documents can be processed using traditional or simple deep learning methods, while moderately to severely deteriorated documents require more efficient and robust techniques. Therefore, it is important to include the deterioration level of each document along with its segmentation mask. LeafOCR-Line provides this information as well. Since LeafOCR-Line facilitates line segmentation, an essential sub task in palm leaf digitization, this dataset contributes to the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (UN-SDGs) by aligning with SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), thereby advancing efforts to preserve cultural heritage for the benefit of future generations.MethodsThis section presents the methodology employed to collect and annotate the palm leaf line segmentation dataset. A systematic pipeline was designed to build a LeafOCR-Line dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The annotation of palm leaf document images was carried out manually using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) (https://www.cvat.ai/). The overall workflow includes image acquisition, quality assessment based on deterioration, text line annotation, verification using text detection models, and final visualization.Fig. 2Full size imageThe pipeline for generating LeafOCR-Line dataset.Image acquisitionPalm leaf document images were collected from Shiju Alex’s repository (https://shijualex.in/). Due to the widespread availability of palm leaf manuscripts in Malayalam script, images in this script were chosen for the line segmentation task. Nevertheless, the annotated images can be applied to palm leaf manuscripts in any similarly structured script, as the segmentation algorithm is independent of individual script but influenced by factors such as writing style, document quality, and spacing. Therefore, while the dataset was created using Malayalam manuscripts, it is not restricted to them and can be applied to manuscripts in other Indian and non Indian scripts as well. Malayalam is a Dravidian language primarily spoken in the state of Kerala, located in southern India. Kerala has a rich tradition of palm leaf manuscripts, owing to its historical, cultural, and environmental factors. Moreover, the round shape of Malayalam characters could also be a reason scholars used palm leaf manuscripts as their writing medium, since these shapes were less likely to tear the fragile nature of palm leaves than straight forms19.According to information from Shiju Alex’s repository, the manuscripts date from 1000 to 1800. The estimated time periods were identified based on writing styles. All relevant metadata and detailed information are included with the dataset. A total of 20 manuscript bundles are collected in PDF format. After downloading the document, an online tool was used to extract individual pages from the PDF. Altogether 2917 images were extracted and saved in JPEG format. Each image featured a black background with a single palm leaf at its center. These individual leaves were then segmented from the background programmatically using Python. The contour detection technique was employed to remove margins and isolate only the leaf regions.The height and width of the documents are not uniform, the height ranges from 150 to 1200 pixels, and the width varies from 700 to 7000 pixels. The number of text lines in each document ranges from 3 to 12. Additionally, some documents have one punch hole, while others have two. In most cases, the first two and last two lines are continuous, while the middle lines often have broken continuity due to the presence of punch holes. These documents do not belong to the same time period or the same author. As a result, the writing styles differ significantly from one document to another. Not only the writing style but also the text alignment varies across documents.Quality assessmentThe extracted images exhibit different quality levels, and for all subtasks of palm leaf digitization, including preprocessing, line segmentation, and text recognition, different approaches need to be adopted depending on the quality. Therefore, quality assignment plays a crucial role. In this work, the images were categorized into three quality levels based on the degree of deterioration observed: less deteriorated, moderately deteriorated, and highly deteriorated. The quality classification and labeling follow the quantitative methodology presented in18, which employs a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) and Vision Transformer based framework for objective quality assessment of palm leaf manuscripts based on visual clarity, contrast, and physical condition. For the current line segmentation task, images from this quality assessed corpus were selected. Detailed descriptions and sample images from each category are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 3.Table 1 Quality level description of images.Full size tableFig. 3Full size imageSample Images from Different Quality Levels: (a) Less deteriorated (b) Moderately deteriorated (c) Highly deteriorated.Text line annotationDifferent standards and conditions have been adopted to perform the line segmentation task. One of the significant methods researchers have adopted to label the text line is the bounding box. However, bounding boxes often cut through letters that extend above or below the baseline; hence, they generate relatively poor segmentation data20,21. Moreover, palm leaf images exhibit a much more complicated structure due to extensive noise and diverse handwriting styles. Therefore, polygon mask annotations, which can precisely capture the irregular and curved nature of text lines, are more appropriate and effective for segmentation in such challenging historical documents. All the images were manually annotated using the CVAT. During segmentation, only text lines were masked and punch holes were not annotated. Polygons were drawn around the text lines.The annotation process consisted of two phases. Phase 1 involved explicit CVAT setup and annotation, while Phase 2 focused on verification. Fig. 4 shows the annotation workflow in detail. Graduate student volunteers from the university performed the annotation task, which was subsequently verified by experts proficient in reading the Malayalam script.Fig. 4Full size imageFlowchart of the workflow for line mask annotation.Phase 1 included two sub stages. The first stage was CVAT setup, which began with project creation. During this step, the project name, label name, and label type were specified. The project name could be arbitrary, but the label type was set to polygon to enable polygon mask annotation. Only one label was created, as the focus was exclusively on line labeling.After the project was created, the task was configured by uploading images and applying the labels defined during project setup. Once the task was configured, annotation jobs were distributed to annotators.The annotation stage commenced with the selection of the appropriate AI tool. Annotators were instructed to use the “Segment Anything 2.0” interactor, enabling the options to convert masks to polygons and to initiate with a bounding box. An initial bounding box was then manually drawn around the line text region. “Segment Anything 2.0” converted this bounding box into a mask, which CVAT subsequently transformed into a polygon.Upon creation of the initial polygon, portions of characters in the text line frequently extended beyond the polygon boundary. Manual refinement was therefore initiated at this stage. “Segment Anything 2.0” provides multiple access points (vertices) of the polygon as shown in Fig. 5. These vertices can be used to drag, reshape, or adjust the polygon according to the shape of the characters.Fig. 5Full size imageIllustration of text line annotation in CVAT: the green line indicates the masked text line, and the second line shows polygon vertices for refinement.During manual polygon refinement, multiple control points were used to adjust the inclusion or exclusion of text precisely. Special attention was given to cases where characters from adjacent lines appeared to touch or slightly overlap. In scripts such as Malayalam, certain characters (e.g., pa) often have strokes or diacritical extensions such as an upper right curve that may intrude into the space of a neighbouring line. These instances were carefully analyzed to ensure accurate segmentation without distorting character shapes. If the overlapping region was minor and its exclusion did not affect the structural integrity or recognizability of the character, it was intentionally ignored during masking. However, if removing a portion would compromise the character’s form, the segmentation boundary was adjusted accordingly. This character aware approach allowed slight protrusions across line boundaries, ensuring that line segmentation did not interfere with the readability or identity of the characters. After masking, the labeled images are exported by selecting the “Segment Anything 1.1” option, and the exported masks are downloaded as masked images as shown in the Fig. 6.Fig. 6Full size imageSample output from CVAT after line annotation: (a) Original document image and (b) the corresponding masked image generated for line segmentation.Once the masked images are downloaded, phase 2 begins, including the verification process. During verification, a manual verification process overlays the original image on the masked image. If any alignment issues are identified, the image is sent back for relabeling. This review step ensures the accuracy and quality of the labeled images. The original image and its corresponding masks are shown in the Fig. 6. In the given image, there are two punch holes. In the proposed dataset, the lines that break due to punch holes are considered as separate objects. Furthermore, the bumps in the line mask indicate the elongated parts of the characters in the upward or downward direction.The time required for the labeling process varies across images and depends on the deterioration level, the writing style of the document, and the width of the image. For images that are less deteriorated but exhibit skewed lines or lack spacing between lines, the labeling process required approximately 20 minutes per image. In contrast, images with sufficient spacing between lines and minimal deterioration could be labeled in about 3 minutes per image. For deteriorated images with adequate spacing between lines, labeling typically required around 5 minutes per image, as additional effort was needed to distinguish character pixels from stains. Furthermore, when character pixels were faded, the labeling process became more time consuming due to the difficulty of accurately identifying them.Data RecordsThe LeafOCR-Line dataset6 has been deposited in Figshare. In the proposed dataset, we processed 1710 palm leaf images from 20 different Malayalam literary works. Each image is uniquely identified using the naming convention “manuscriptname_pagenumber.jpg”, while the associated masks follow the same convention with a “.png” extension. The dataset is organized into two primary subfolders: image and mask. Within each of these subfolders, there are three additional subfolders: train, test, and validation. Out of 1710 images, 405 belong to the more deteriorated category, 911 to the medium deteriorated category, and 394 to the less deteriorated category. The training set consists of 284 more deteriorated, 637 medium deteriorated, and 277 less deteriorated images. Similarly, the test set contains 61 more deteriorated, 137 medium deteriorated, and 59 less deteriorated images. The validation set contains 60 more deteriorated, 137 medium deteriorated, and 58 less deteriorated images. In general, 70% of the total data were used for training, while the remaining 30% were allocated to the test and validation sets, ensuring category wise balance. The Table 2 shows the class proportions in each split. The class proportions are nearly identical across all three splits, with only minor variations. In the dataset, 53% of documents are classified as medium deteriorated, 23% as less deteriorated, and 24% as more deteriorated. Each split maintains the same class distribution as the overall dataset. The folder structure of the mask directory mirrors that of the image directory. The number of text lines present in each image varies from 3 to 12. Hence, using the given masks, researchers will be able to extract more than 10000 text line images from these 1710 palm leaves. Additionally, we provide an Excel file. The first tab contains two columns: the first column is the image name, and the second column indicates the deterioration level of the image. The considered deterioration levels are more deteriorated, less deteriorated, or medium deteriorated. The second tab of the Excel sheet consists of literary work or palm leaf bundle details, including the original manuscript name, total number of images available, number of images processed from that particular bundle, the average number of lines present in the images, and a final column representing the link to the manuscripts in the Shiju Alex repository. This additional information could be useful for the recognition task after segmentation. The trained models were tested on documents representing varying levels of deterioration, and the results were analyzed accordingly. The goal is to benchmark a variety of segmentation models on historical palm leaf manuscripts and to evaluate their effectiveness across different levels of deterioration. Performance was assessed using multiple evaluation metrics, namely Intersection over Union (IoU) and the Dice coefficient. These metrics provide a reliable measure of segmentation accuracy and effectiveness in isolating text lines from manuscript images. Through comparative analysis of these metrics, models are identified that perform best in segmenting lines from palm leaf manuscripts.Evaluation metricsIn this study, Intersection over Union (IoU) and the Dice coefficient are employed to evaluate the performance of the different models. The IoU metric quantifies the degree of overlap between the predicted mask and the ground truth mask. IoU is calculated as, $$\,{\rm{IoU}}=\frac{{\rm{Area\; of\; Intersection}}}{{\rm{Area\; of\; Union}}}$$ (1) The value of IoU ranges between 0 and 1. An IoU of 0 indicates no overlap between the two masks, while an IoU of 1 represents a perfect overlap, where the intersection and union areas are identical. Another metric used is the Dice coefficient, which measures the similarity between two samples. It is calculated as: $$\,{\rm{Dice}}=\frac{2\times {\rm{Area\; of\; Intersection}}}{{\rm{Total\; Area\; of\; Predicted\; Mask}}+{\rm{Ground\; Truth\; Mask}}}$$ (2) Like IoU, the Dice coefficient ranges between 0 and 1, with higher values indicating better agreement between prediction and ground truth. Additionally, the F1 score is used to provide a balanced measure of precision and recall.Comparison modelsSeveral baseline models were employed to evaluate the quality of the dataset for line segmentation in palm leaf manuscripts. The models used include U-Net, U-Net with Spatial Attention, FCN-8, FCN-16, PSPNet, LinkNet, SegNet, DeepLabV3, UNETR, and SegFormer, all of which have already demonstrated their effectiveness in segmenting various types of images. The number of parameters and layers of each model is shown in Table 4. All these models were trained and tested on datasets of varying quality, and the results are reported in Table 5.Table 4 Comparison of Segmentation Model Architectures.Full size tableTable 5 Performance comparison of different models on palm leaf manuscripts under varying deterioration levels.Full size tableDuring the experiments, the model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1e-4 (0.0001). A batch size of 4 was used, and training was performed for 20 epochs. The loss function employed was a combination of focal loss and dice loss.To prepare the data, the images were resized so that their height and width matched the closest multiples of 512, keeping the dimensions as close as possible to the original size. The resized images were then divided into patches of 512 × 512 pixels, which were used for training. After prediction, the patches were stitched back together to reconstruct the complete images. For computational efficiency, the color masks were converted into binary format prior to training.The reported results show that all models perform well on the palm leaf line segmentation dataset. However, DeepLabV3 shows a clear advantage, achieving the highest IoU and Dice scores across all three quality levels of documents. Notably, it maintains an IoU above 0.78 for highly deteriorated manuscripts, while achieving 0.81 IoU for less deteriorated and 0.80 IoU for medium deteriorated images. Its superior performance is primarily due to its Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling (ASPP) module, which captures multi scale contextual information using dilated convolutions while maintaining spatial resolution. This functionality is essential for handling the varying line widths, curvatures, and degradation observed in palm leaf manuscripts. U-Net with 31 million parameters, LinkNet with 11.6 million, SegNet with 29.4 million parameters, and FCN-8 with 134 million parameters utilize effective skip connections and demonstrated strong performance for palm leaf manuscripts. The IoU score of U-Net ranges from 0.80 to 0.74 across the three categories, with only a marginal difference between less and moderately deteriorated images. LinkNet shows a very similar performance trend, achieving IoU values comparable to those of U-Net across all categories. In contrast, SegNet exhibits a slight reduction in performance, particularly on moderately and highly deteriorated images, compared to U-Net and LinkNet. These findings suggest that multi level feature fusion and spatial inductive biases are particularly effective for modeling curved line geometry. In contrast, adding attention mechanisms reduced performance in the U-Net with 31.4 million parameters distributed across 72 layers, introduced computational inefficiency, while attention gates suppressed features informative in degraded regions. PSPNet with 9.2 million showed the weakest results, as its aggressive pyramid pooling removes the fine boundary details needed for thin, curved text lines, leading to a marked decline in performance on heavily degraded documents. The IoU scores obtained by PSPNet indicate minimal performance variation between the medium and more deterioration levels. Transformer based models such as UNETR with 416 million parameters and SegFormer with only 7.5 million parameters, also exhibited degraded performance compared to LinkNet and U-Net, though they still performed better than PSPNet. With more training iterations, these transformer based models may yield improved results. However, UNETR performs relatively well for less deteriorated images, achieving an IoU of 0.74, but yields lower IoU scores for the other two categories. In comparison, SegFormer outperforms UNETR on medium and highly deteriorated documents.Statistical validationTo statistically prove the above reported result and check whether all models perform equally, the Friedman test was conducted. It resulted in 62.7322 and P-value < 0.001 (highly significant). From the reported result, it is clear that there is strong evidence that all models perform significantly differently. Some models are better than others. Furthermore, post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Benjamini-Hochberg correction were also performed for pairwise comparisons. It revealed that DeepLabV3 is significantly the best, and PSPNet is significantly the worst. The reported performance hierarchy is DeepLabV3 > {LinkNet, U-Net, FCN-8} > {SegNet, FCN-16} > {U-Net with Attention} > {SegFormer,UNETR} > PSPNet.VisualizationFigure 7 shows the images of different quality and the corresponding segmented lines using different models. Even though the models are trained for only 20 epochs, all models are moderately able to segment the lines from the manuscript. DeepLabv3 is working exceptionally well compared to the other models. For good quality (less deteriorated) and medium quality images, DeepLabv3 segmented all the lines without losing any textual part from the manuscript. However, when it comes to the bad quality (more deteriorated) images, the DeepLabv3 model demonstrates the ability to accurately capture the overall line structure and continuity of palm leaf manuscripts. In the predicted example shown, the first two lines appear merged in certain regions, highlighting the model’s difficulty in separating closely spaced lines. This limitation is observed at the current training stage (20 epochs). It is expected that with further training over additional epochs, the model will better distinguish such fine line separations, leading to improved segmentation quality. For less deteriorated documents, the LinkNet model also shows no signs of over segmentation or under-segmentation. There were no merged lines and no structural discontinuities. All other models, except PSPNet, exhibited a moderate level of over segmentation and under-segmentation. However, PSPNet failed to segment the lines altogether. For medium-deteriorated documents, DeepLabV3, U-Net, and SegNet segmented the lines without any over segmentation or under segmentation, while all other models showed only a very minor level of under segmentation. Despite the reduced clarity of text in highly deteriorated documents, all models achieved acceptable segmentation, with DeepLabV3 and U-Net demonstrating superior performance and only negligible under segmentation. These findings, by emphasizing the strengths of each model, support researchers in selecting the most suitable approach for palm leaf line segmentation tasks.Fig. 7Full size imageVisualization of palm leaf line segmentation by different models.
