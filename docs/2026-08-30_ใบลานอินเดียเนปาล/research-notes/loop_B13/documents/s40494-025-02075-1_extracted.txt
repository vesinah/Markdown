Synergistic ATRP-IL approach for
improving the mechanical properties of
fragile palm leaf manuscripts
Check for updates
Yihang Zhou1,2, Kai Wang3
& Liuyang Han1,2
This study proposes a novel conservation strategy combining atom transfer radical polymerization
(ATRP) and ionic liquid (IL) treatments to modulate the mechanical properties of fragile palm leaf
manuscripts. ATRP was employed to graft methacrylate polymers (PAAEM, PHMA, and their
copolymers) onto lignin-rich regions of degraded samples, forming a reinforcement network within the
cell walls. Concurrently, 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([Bmim]Cl) was applied to enhance
ﬂexibility. ATRP consolidation signiﬁcantly improved bending strength up to 37.9 MPa for highly
degraded samples by thickening ﬁber cell walls. Ionic liquid treatment increased deﬂection to levels
comparable to sound manuscripts. The synergistic application of ATRP (PAAEM) and IL achieved a
toughness of 0.56 J/m³, 3.3 times as high as untreated samples. FTIR and histochemical staining
demonstrated successful polymer grafting and reduced exposure of degraded polysaccharides. The
study bridges advanced polymer chemistry and heritage conservation, offering a new pathway for
preserving fragile cultural artifacts.
Palm leaf manuscripts have served as a vital medium for recording
knowledge and culture in many parts of China, India, Nepal, Myanmar,
etc. for centuries1. They provide us with valuable scientiﬁc knowledge,
histories, and literatures of Buddhism concerning ancient South Asia and
Southeast Asia2,3. Palm leaf has been commonly processed by a series of
procedures to improve writability and durability, including cutting,
steaming, boiling, polishing, ﬂattening, drying, and perforating4. Despite
these complex treatments, they are still susceptible to deterioration over
time. The mechanical degradation of these manuscripts, often caused by
environmental factors, biological activity, and chemical changes in theleaf
structure, threatens the loss of invaluable historical and cultural infor-
mation. A case study on the palm leaf manuscripts from Burma shows
cracking in the S1–S3 layers of the cell wall and numerous circular or
nearly circular cavities caused by microbial erosion in the S2 layer, with
severe degradation of polysaccharides and pectin, which compromise the
tensile strength5. Under high relative humidity, fungal degradation can
also cause severe loss of hemicellulose and cellulose, and thus lead to
signiﬁcantly reduced bending strength6,7. To address this issue, innovative
approaches are required to restore and enhance the mechanical properties
of these fragile artifacts without compromising their integrity. Hydroxyl
substances like water and glycerol can help soften and toughen the palm
leaf manuscripts2,8, but subjected to volatility. Oils are conventional
conservation materials for palm leaf manuscripts1. Clove, camphor, and
eucalyptus oils was found to be helpful in keeping the leaves ﬂexible and
soft, while mustard, cedar wood, and castor oils increase the stiffness but
without improve the ﬂexibility9. The blends of citronella, neem, and
mustard exhibit the best compatibility and stiffness among different
essential oil blends10. Nonetheless, the application of essential oils can
cause discoloration and adhesion between leaf pieces4. Recently, ionic
liquid is proven to be an effective and more durable agent than plant
essential oils to increase the tensile toughness and ﬂexibility of simulated
palm leaf manuscript11.
In recent years, advanced polymer chemistry and material science
techniques have shown promise in the ﬁeld of heritage conservation. Atom
Transfer Radical Polymerization (ATRP), a controlled radical polymeriza-
tion method, has emerged as a powerful tool for modifying and stabilizing
materialsatthemolecularlevel.Ithasbeeninvestigatedandmodiﬁedforthe
purpose of consolidation and maintaining the dimensional stability of
waterloggedarchaeologicalwoodupondehydration12–14.Byenablingprecise
control over polymer growth on targeted region, ATRP offers the potential
to reinforce the ﬁbrous structure of fragile palm leaves, thereby improving
their mechanical strength (depicted in Fig. 1). Concurrently, ionic liquids
are common plasticizers of a different variety15 and have gained attention as
potential softening and toughening agent for palm leaf manuscript11.

This study explores the application of ATRP as well as its synergistic
effects with ionic liquids to modulate the mechanical properties of fragile
palmleaf manuscripts.Weaimto develop anovelconservationstrategythat
not only restores the structural integrity of these artifacts but also provides
long-term protection against potential mechanical damage. Through a
series of mechanical, chemical, and morphological analyses, we evaluate the
effectiveness of this approach in preserving the physical and cultural legacy
of palm leaf manuscripts, offering new insights into the application of
advanced materials science in heritage conservation.
Results
Characterization of artiﬁcially degraded palm leaf manuscripts
Considering the facts that historical palm leaf manuscripts are featured with
degradationofhemicelluloseand celluloseandanincreased relativeamount
of lignin5,16,17, artiﬁcial degradation using 20% sulfuric acid effectively
simulated the chemical and mechanical deterioration observed in historical
palm leaf manuscripts (Fig.2). Moderately degraded (m-degraded) samples
retained86.9%oftheiroriginalweight,withbendingstrengthanddeﬂection
values of 28.6 MPa and 0.029, respectively, representing approximately 60%
of the original mechanical performance (Fig.2a–c). Highly degraded (h-
degraded) samples exhibited further weight loss (80.8% residual), reduced
bending strength (14.7 MPa), and deﬂection (0.019), corresponding to less
than 40% of the original performance.
In the FTIR spectra (in Fig.2d), the absorption peak at 1733 cm−1
attributed to C = O stretching vibration of hemicellulose, was signiﬁcantly
reduced, indicating the loss of hemicellulose. Cellulose in the degraded
samples wasless degraded but possiblyfragmented, asevidenced bytheslight
decrease in the absorption band at 1376 cm−1 (C-H in-plane bending
vibration), and the shoulder peak of C-H out-of-plane vibration at 897 cm−1
remained noticeable. The degraded samples also exhibited more pronounced
absorption peaks at 1052 and 796 cm−1, attributed to amorphous silica, as the
partial loss of holocellulose exposed phytoliths. After staining with astra blue
andsafranine,theparenchymaintheh-degradedsample(Fig.2f)wasstained
more deeply by safranine, indicating an increase in lignin content. The ﬁbers
(Fig. 2f) were stained by astra blue after artiﬁcial degradation, which indicates
the exposure of cellulose and possible sulfonation of cellulose after disruption
of the cell wall structure. These chemical and mechanical features are well
align with fragile historical palm leaf manuscript.
ATRP treatment of artiﬁcial degraded palm leaf
The monomers for ATRP of palm leaf samples were selected based on their
reactivity, mechanical performance of the resulting polymers, and their
compatibility with lignocellulosic materials. Hydroxyethyl methacrylate
(HMA), with a hydroxyl group in the side chain, enables polymer (PHMA)
integration with cellulose, providing mechanical reinforcement via hydro-
gen bonds. Acetoacetoxyethyl methacrylate (AAEM), containing an acet-
oacetoxy group, serves as a hydrogen bond acceptor and promotes slight
crosslinking during polymerization, improving elasticity. After ATRP
treatment for 12 h, the weight percentage gains for the samplesconsolidated
with PAAEM, PHMA, and their copolymer PAAEM-co-HMA were
108.2%, 108.3%, and 121.7%, respectively (Fig. 3a). Higher gains were
achievable by prolonging the polymerization time (Fig. 3b). The synergistic
ATRP-IL treatment signiﬁcantly improved bending resistance, as visually
demonstrated in Supplementary Movie 1, where treated samples exhibited
enhanced ﬂexibility compared to untreated controls. However, no sig-
niﬁcant improvement inbendingstrength wasobserved (Fig.3c), indicating
that the 12-h treatment already reached the limits of consolidation for this
method and the chosen monomers.
In FTIR spectra (Fig.3d), the most pronounced peaks at 1713 cm-1 and
1226 cm-1 (1239 cm-1 for PAAEM) attributed to stretching and bending
vibration of C = O, along with newly appeared C-H bending vibration at
747 cm-1 suggests the successful introduction of methacrylate polymers into
the samples18,19, Furthermore, the ﬁbers in PAAEM samples could not be
stainedbyastrablueandtheparenchymawaslessstainedbysafranineinthe
microsection, which demonstrates PAAEM has considerably modiﬁed the
cell wall structure with much less original groups being exposed.
Further observation under SEM suggests the overall anatomical
structure was not altered by the ATRP process (Fig.5a–d). Vessels
and parenchyma maintain their original structure and are not ﬁlled
with any visible introduced polymers. Signiﬁcant increase in the
thickness of ﬁber cell walls can be noticed (Fig.5f–h), especially for
those in PAAEM consolidated sample (Fig. 5f) and PAAEM-co-
HMA consolidated one (Fig. 5h), while ﬁber cell walls are thin and
porous in highly degraded sample (Fig. 5e). These features conﬁrm
the observation in optical microscope (Fig. 4e), and the growth of
methacrylate polymers within the cell walls that is similar to the case
in waterlogged archaeological wood20.

Modulation of mechanical properties
The effects of IL on the bending properties were re-evaluated, as previous
researchdidnotassessbendingstrengthduetoequipmentlimitations(Ding
et al.11). Bending fracture is the primary damage mode for fragile palm leaf
manuscripts. As shown in Fig.4a, b, the deﬂection of moderately degraded
samples increased effectively with both 10% and 20% IL treatments, while
only 20% IL was effective in the highly degraded samples. For both IL-
treated samples, deﬂection was comparable to that of the undegraded
reference samples, conﬁrming IL’s role in increasing ﬂexibility. However, a
dramatic decrease in bending strength occurred with IL application (Fig. 5c,
d), indicating that IL improves ﬂexibility at the expense of bending strength.
Therefore, additional consolidation is necessary to improve or maintain
bending strength while enhancing ﬂexibility with IL.
The bending strength of ATRP-treated samples without IL
addition increased signiﬁcantly due to the introduction of the poly-
mer network (Fig. 6a–d). PHMA showed the best reinforcement in
bending strength (37.9 MPa) in highly degraded samples, approach-
ing that of the undegraded reference samples (45.4 MPa). However, a
high standard deviation suggests the consolidation was not uniform.
PAAEM-treated samples showed a signiﬁcant increase in bending
strength (27.2 MPa) with a lower standard deviation, and the samples
appeared visually ﬂat with no crimping tendencies. For moderately
degraded samples, PAAEM treatment raised bending strength to the
reference sample level (Fig. 5d). However, the introduction of 20% IL
caused signiﬁcant strength loss in ATRP-treated samples, similar to
untreated samples. PAAEM-co-HMA-treated samples showed less
sensitivity to IL-induced strength loss, possibly due to the strong
hydrogen bond network between AAEM and HMA.
Both ATRP and IL treatments signiﬁcantly improved the ﬂexibility of
highly degraded samples, with deﬂection comparable to that of the unde-
graded reference samples (Fig. 6e–h). Among all treatments, PAAEM
demonstrated the best balance of ﬂexibility and strength.
An application trial on a piece of ancient palm leaf manuscript
A piece of palm leaf manuscript of the Qing Dynasty was con-
solidated by the described method and shown in Fig. 7. Unlike
artiﬁcially degraded samples, real palm leaf manuscript samples are
more complicated, which have inscriptions and possible past treat-
ments of material like essential oil. Therefore, the treatment was
carried out under a modiﬁed and milder condition as described in
section 2.2, which prevents dissolving oil by adopting lower tem-
perature reactions and avoiding the using of pure ethanol. It can be
noticed that in Fig. 7a–d this piece of palm leaf manuscript are
slightly darker and less yellowish with a total color difference (ΔE)
4.30. The inscriptions become bold, which is probably caused by
higher reactivity within the inscription region. In terms of mechan-
ical properties, the untreated ancient sample is similar to the pre-
pared moderately degraded samples. The mechanical performances
are also improved like artiﬁcial degraded samples. The bending
strength rises from 33.4 to 44.1 MPa after treated by PAAEM and to
34.6 MPa after treated by PAAEM + IL (Fig. 7e). The deﬂection
also increases from 0.0267 to 0.0325 after treated by PAAEM + IL
(Fig. 7f), which suggests the effectiveness in the improvement of
ﬂexibility and toughness.
Discussion
The degradation of palm leaf manuscripts is largely governed by the loss of
hemicellulose and cellulose and the relative increase in lignin content,
consistent with the chemical changes observed in artiﬁcially degraded
samples16,17. The FTIR results clearly demonstrate the degradation process,
where the C = O stretching peak of hemicellulose (1733 cm−1) is dimin-
ished, and the cellulose exhibits fragmentation. The increase in silica
absorption in degraded samples further supports the loss of holocellulose,
which allows phytoliths to become more exposed (Fig. 2d). This ﬁnding
emphasizes the complex biochemical transformations that occur during
degradation, which are critical for understanding the preservation needs of
these manuscripts.
The increased lignin content in the degraded palm leaf manuscripts, as
indicated by deeper safranine staining in the parenchyma (Fig. 2f), is
another key feature of the degradation process. The exposure of cellulose in
the ﬁbers, as shown by astra blue staining, also suggests potential changes in
the cellulose structure, possibly including sulfonation, which further affects
the mechanical properties of the manuscripts. These changes highlight the
origin of fragility of degraded manuscripts and the need for effective con-
solidation methods.
The ATRP treatment demonstrated substantial effectiveness in con-
solidatingthedegradedpalmleafsamples.Theweightgainsobservedforthe
PAAEM, PHMA, and PAAEM-co-HMA treatments (Fig. 3a) show the
successful incorporation of the polymers into the degraded samples.
However, the lack of signiﬁcant improvement in bending strength (Fig. 3c)
suggests that the 12-h ATRP treatment reached its limits in terms of con-
solidation, indicating that a longer polymerization time or different poly-
merization conditions may be needed for further improvement.
The FTIR results (Fig. 3d) conﬁrm that the methacrylate polymers
successfully integrated into the degraded samples, which is consistent with
the observed changes in the chemical structure of the palm leaf cell walls.

The reduction in staining by astra blue and safranine (Fig. 3e) indicates the
effective modiﬁcation of the cell wall structure, making the ﬁbers less prone
to damage.
The results demonstrate that IL treatment signiﬁcantly increased the
ﬂexibility of both moderately and highly degraded samples. The deﬂection
of the IL-treated samples was comparable to that of the undegraded refer-
ence samples, conﬁrming IL’s role in reducing brittleness and improving
ﬂexibility (Fig. 5a, b). However, the substantial reduction in bending
strength(Fig.5c,d)suggeststhatILtreatmentalonemaynotbesufﬁcientfor
preserving the structural integrity of the manuscripts, particularly in terms
of strength.
Thus, while IL enhances ﬂexibility, additional reinforcement through
ATRP treatment is necessary to restore the material’s mechanical strength.
This ﬁnding emphasizes the need for a balanced approach that integrates IL
for ﬂexibility and ATRP for strength, particularly when dealing with highly
degraded materials.
The combination of ATRP and IL treatment resulted in the best overall
mechanical performance, particularly for the highly degraded samples. The
ATRP treatment signiﬁcantly reinforced the bending strength, while IL
enhanced the ﬂexibility (Fig. 8e–h). The combination of PAAEM with IL in
the highly degraded samples resulted in the highest toughness (0.56 J/m3),
which was considerably greater than that of untreated or IL-only samples
(Fig. 8a). PAAEM-co-HMA + IL treatment showed the highest toughness,
which aligns with the visual evidence of enhanced bending resistance in
Supporting Information Video S1, but its high standard deviation suggests
that the consolidation was not uniform, limiting its practical application.
For moderately degraded samples, PAAEM alone provided the best
toughness (0.89 J/m3), but the addition of IL slightly decreased toughness
(Fig. 7b). This indicates that IL can improve ﬂexibility, but its effect on
toughness is constrained by the ability to increase deﬂection without
sacriﬁcing mechanical strength. These ﬁndings highlight the importance of
optimizing both polymer and IL treatment conditions for the conservation
of fragile materials.
Although the described approach exhibits promising mechanical
improvements in both artiﬁcial and real palm leaf manuscript samples,
limitations and possible questions remain. Firstly, this approach involves
three steps or six steps if counting washing, which is seemingly more com-
plicated than any existing methods. For some palm leaf manuscripts with
proteinaceousmaterialsinthesupportlikethecasefromtheTibetanregion21,
the amino functionalization step can be omitted and the treatment would be
simpliﬁed.Secondly,palmleafmanuscriptwouldexperienceadryingprocess
after the treatment, which increases the risk of cracking. Efforts in controls of
drying process were not made in this study, which would be better to con-
sidered in application. Thirdly, changes in color and inscriptions can be still
noticed and requires further improvements in controlling the consolidation
process in the future. Last but not least, some conservators may argue that
reaction should not be carried out on the material of cultural heritage. But
actuallyanyconsolidationchangesthematerialnature.Wethinkitisamatter
of the Ship of Theseus. Some people don’t regard it as the same ship after
replacingalltheparts.Butothersholdthepointthatreplacingthepartsmakes
the ship persist. As for the case of consolidation of fragile palm leaf manu-
script by this approach, the effective reconstruction of new polymeric net-
work compensates the loss of cellulose and provides mechanical
improvements. The point is how to make it more applicable in the future,
with a satisfying balance of mechanical performance, appearance, durability
and robustness in different kinds of palm leaf manuscripts.

and PHMA) ontolignin-richregionsofdegraded samples,ATRP effectively
reinforced ﬁber cell walls, achieving bending strengths of up to 37.9 MPafor

various kinds in a more controlled manner. By bridging advanced polymer
chemistry and heritage science, this research underscores the potential of
interdisciplinary approaches to safeguard fragile cultural heritage against

Methods
Materials
AfewofQingDynastypalmleaf manuscriptswere collected fromamyanmar
collector.Duetothelimitedquantityofrealpalmleafmanuscriptsamplesand
the fact that inscriptions on it would compromise the mechanical testing,
artiﬁcially degraded samples were used as more standard and reproducible
samples. Considering the embrittlement is mainly caused by the degradation
of polysaccharides, they were prepared by hydrolyzing hemicellulose and
cellulose in 20% sulfuric acid at 60 °C for 2 h (moderately degraded one) or at
80 °C for 1 h (highly degraded one). Subsequently, the degraded samples were
washed in deionized water until the pH approached 7.
The reagents used in this research are as follows: CuBr2 (99%) from
Macklin Biochemical; 2-mecaptoethylamine hydrochloride (97%, MEA)
from Jiuding Chemical; sodium hydroxide (96%) and glucose from Tong-
guang Fine Chemicals, sulfuric acid (98%) from Xilong Scientiﬁc, 2-bromo
isobutyric acid (97%, BIBA) from Bide Pharmaceutical, 1-ethyl-3(3-dime-
thylpropylamine) carbodiimide chloride (99%, EDC) from Beijing MREDA
Technology, anhydrous ethanol (99%) purchased from Tongguang Fine
Chemicals, 2,2’-bipyridyl (99%, Bpy) from Aladdin Biochemical Technol-
ogy, 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (99%, HMA) from HEOWNS Bio-
chemical Technology, 2-(Acetoacetoxy)ethyl methacrylate (95%, AAEM)
from RHAWN, 1-Butyl-3-methylimidazolium chloride ([Bmim]Cl),
N-Hydroxysulfosuccinimide sodium salt (Sulfo-NHS) from B&D Bio-
technology, 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane (APTES, 98%) from Shanghai
Macklin Biotechnolgy. The inhibitors in the monomers were eliminated by
alkaline aluminium oxide columns before use.
Consolidation by atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP)
PriortotheATRP,initiatorswereimmobilized ontheligninofthedegraded
samples according to ref. 13 with slight modiﬁcations. Speciﬁcally, the
samples were ﬁrst functionalized with amino groups by reacting with
0.3 mol/L MEA at 50 °C, pH8.0 for 24 h, followed by thoroughly washing in
deionizedwateranddehydrationwithethanol.Thereafter,theywerereacted
with 0.1 mol/L BIBA and 0.12 mol/L EDC in ethanol for 3 h followed by
thoroughly washing in ethanol for at least 24 h to removed unreacted BIBA.
The ATRP was carried out with reference to refs. 12,13, with some mod-
iﬁcations to adjust the polymerization rate. The above samples were then
placed in a sealed glass container with 1.0 mg CuBr2 (1 eq.), 3.0 mg Bpy (5
eq.), 0.044 g glucose (50 eq.), 1.0 g monomer (HMA or AAEM or 0.5 g
HMA and 0.5 g AAEM), 4 ml ethanol and 5 ml water, and heated at 60 °C
for 12, 24 or 36 h. Thereafter, the consolidated samples were washed with
ethanol, dried naturally and are named after the polymers as PAAEM,
PHMA and PAAEM-co-HMA.
An application trial was carried out based on the results of con-
solidation of artiﬁcially degraded samples under a further modiﬁed and
milder condition. The part without inscriptions were cut from the sample
for mechancal testing and treated along with the major part. The piece of
ancient palm leaf manuscript was ﬁrst treated by 0.5% APTES water solu-
tion overnight and washedin deionized water thoroughly. Thereafter, it was
reacted with 0.1 mol/L BIBA, 0.12 mol/L EDC and sulfo-NHS in PBS
solution (pH 7.0) at room temperature. Then it was placed in a sealed glass
container with 1.0 mg CuBr2 (1 eq.), 3.0 mg Bpy (5 eq.), 0.075 g L-Ascorbic
acid 2-glucoside (50 eq.), 1.0 g AAEM 4 ml ethanol and 5 ml water, and
heated at 40 °C for 24 h. The consolidated sample was washed with 30%
ethanol and dried naturally.
Treatment with ionic liquid (IL)
The degraded samples and consolidated samples were treated with 10 wt%
or 20 wt% IL ([Bmim]Cl) water solution for 1 h to improve their ﬂexibility
and dried naturally.

Bending tests by thermal mechanical analysis (TMA)

group. The tests were carried out at room temperature (20 ± 3 °C, 55 ± 5%
RH) with an initial load of 0.1 mN and a loading speed of 5 mN/min until

To visually illustrate the chemical variation of the samples in anatomical
structure,thesampleswereembeddedinpolyethyleneglycol2000andcutinto

blue for 30 s, washed by water, stained by safranine and washed by water. The
stained microsections were observed under Nikon LV100N POL microscope.
