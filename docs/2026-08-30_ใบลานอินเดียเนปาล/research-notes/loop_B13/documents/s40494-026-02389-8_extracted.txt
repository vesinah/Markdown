Plant selection in ancient Tibetan palm-
leaf manuscripts: a novel approach to
rapid species identiﬁcation
Check for updates
Qingle Chen1,2, Yunjun Bai3, Jing Tang4, Huijuan Mai5, Shan Wang6 & Hongen Jiang2
Palm-leaf manuscripts, which served as a key writing medium in early South Asia, contain extensive
content such as religious texts and calendars, offering valuable material for historical and cultural
research. The leaves commonly used belong to three Arecaceae species: Borassus ﬂabellifer, Corypha
umbraculifera, and C. utan. Regional preferences in material selection mean that identifying the
botanical species of manuscripts can offer clues to their geographical origin, shedding light on
historical dissemination and cultural exchange. This study applied micro-computed tomography
(micro-CT) and epidermal observation to analyze leaf anatomy, combined with UPGMA clustering. The
three modern species were clearly distinguished, and two ancient Tibetan manuscript fragments were
identiﬁed as C. umbraculifera. This work represents the ﬁrst use of high-resolution micro-CT with
statistical analysis to achieve species-level identiﬁcation of palm-leaf manuscripts, providing a
methodological basis for tracing their origins and demonstrating the potential of non-destructive
techniques in heritage studies.
Palm-leaf manuscripts originated in ancient India and served as one of the
main written media in the ancient world. These manuscripts encompass a
diverse range of content, including astronomical records, calendrical
systems1, and notably, Buddhist scriptures. As Buddhism spread across
Asia, such manuscripts were disseminated to regions including China,
Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and Thailand. Due to the distinctive climatic condi-
tions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, substantial collections of ancient palm-
leaf manuscripts have been preserved in Tibet, China, thus providing
valuable resources for historical and cultural research.
The substrate of palm-leaf manuscripts, as the name implies, is derived
primarily from palm leaves, with key species belonging to the genera Cor-
ypha and Borassus. Historically, the material sources have remained notably
consistent. It is likely that plants of the genus Corypha correspond to the
historically documented Beiduo, while Borassus matches the plant referred
to as Duoluo in classical texts2. Within the genus Corypha, two species have
been widely used: the talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera) and the gebang
palm (Corypha utan)3. Corypha taliera has also been recorded in some
sources4, though it is now considered nearly extinct.
People in different regions have different preferences in terms of leaf
selection strategies. C. umbraculifera is extensively used across many South
and Southeast Asian regions, including Thailand, India, Sri Lanka, Cam-
bodia, Myanmar, Malaysia, and Indonesia4–8. While C. taliera was endemic
to a very limited area in Bangladesh9–11. In comparison, the use of leaves
from the palmyra palm (Borassus ﬂabellifer) appears to have been more
geographically restricted, with documented evidence from India, Nepal4,12,
Bali in Indonesia8, and Myanmar13. These regional patterns in species
selection suggest that identifying the plant species of a manuscript can offer
valuable clues regarding its geographical origin, thereby contributing to
tracing possible dissemination routes.
Distinguishing between the genera Corypha and Borassus is relatively
straightforward due to clear anatomical differences in their venation pat-
terns and leaf symmetry. Corypha species exhibit continuously distributed
transverse veins and abundant minor veins within the mesophyll, which
provide structural support to their large leaves14. In contrast, leaves of B.
ﬂabellifer are generally thickerand display a rectangular, brick-like venation
pattern. A further key distinction lies in leaf symmetry: Corypha leaves are
dorsiventral, whereas those of Borassus are typically isolateral, with only
occasional dorsiventral exceptions15. These diagnostic features are also
observable in historical specimens. For example, anatomical analysis of a
palm-leaf manuscript from India clearly identiﬁed it as belonging to the

However, further identiﬁcation within the genus Corypha remains
challenging. Species such as C. umbraculifera, C. utan, and C. taliera exhibit
highly similar venation patterns6. Beyond venation, leaf structure and epi-
dermal characteristics provide critical diagnostic criteria, which can vary
signiﬁcantly among taxonomic groups16. To date, research has primarily
focused on the leaf structure of C. umbraculifera17,18, while studies on other
species and their associated traits within the genus Corypha remain notably
limited.
Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is a noninvasive imaging
technique that visualizes the internal three-dimensional structure of sam-
ples through X-ray scanning. By acquiring sequential cross-sectional ima-
ges,itachieveshigh-resolutioncharacterizationatmicronorevennanoscale
levels19. This technology has been widely applied in archeology and cultural
heritage studies, like parchment, pottery, plant remains20–23, demonstrating
signiﬁcant potential across multiple research ﬁelds.
In plant sciences, micro-CT has established a solid methodological
foundation for studying tissue morphology. The technique has been
extensively used to examine plant organs—including caryopses, seeds,
leaves, stems, and petioles, as well as various tissues such as leaf venation,
xylem, aerenchyma, cell boundaries, and cell walls19. These studies con-
sistently demonstrate that micro-CT enables detailed, three-dimensional
structural analysis that is often unattainable through conventional methods.
This study applies micro-CT to characterize the leaf structures of C.
umbraculifera, C. utan, B. ﬂabellifer, and ancient palm-leaf manuscripts.
Complemented by epidermal morphological observation and cluster ana-
lysis, it aims to identify the botanical species of historical Tibetan palm-leaf
manuscripts. Our objectives are to improve the accuracy of species identi-
ﬁcation for these cultural artifacts and to evaluate the application of non-
destructive micro-CT technology in heritage science. This work provides a
methodological reference for future research in this ﬁeld.
Methods
Experimental materials
This study utilized modern leaf specimens from three representative palm
species: B. ﬂabellifer, C. umbraculifera, and C. utan. The leaves were col-
lected from the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden (21°55'12” N,
101°16'48” E), Chinese Academy of Sciences, in Xishuangbanna Dai
Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, China.
Traditional practices for producing palm-leaf manuscripts specify the
use of broad and tender leaves. Following this criterion, we selected robust,
mature plants and collected relatively tender, broad, and light-colored
leaves. After collection, the midribs were removed, and the leaves were air-
dried under shade. The widest section of each leaf was used for subsequent
analysis.
Currently preserved palm-leaf manuscripts in the Tibet Region are
largely concentrated in the 10–13th centuries. During this period, a sig-
niﬁcant number of manuscripts were brought from India, Nepal, and the
Kashmir Region24. Ancient palm-leaf manuscripts in Tibet are mainly
preserved in monasteries, museums, and libraries. These facilities house a
signiﬁcant number of ancient Buddhist palm-leaf manuscripts, including
many rare and well-preserved Sanskrit texts that are no longer available in
other regions. For this study, two sets of ancient Sanskrit palm-leaf manu-
scripts were randomly selected from a monastery. Damaged fragments
within the storage boxes were chosen as the subjects of analysis (Fig. 1).
Epidermal observation
Fragments were sectioned into approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 pieces. Fol-
lowing Franklin’s method25, the segments were immersed in a 1:1 acetic
acid–hydrogen peroxide solution and heated at 60 °C for about 24 h until
the tissues became transparent and began to separate. The macerated
samples were carefully peeled to obtain the epidermis, which was then
stained with 5% safranin solution. After sufﬁcient staining, the epidermal
strips were mounted on glass slides for microscopic observation.
Epidermal morphological features were examined using a Leica DM4P
optical microscope. Characteristic structures were documented, and
quantitativemeasurementswereperformedwithImageJsoftware(v.1.51n).
For each sample, the following parameters were recorded: the length of the
long axis of 50 stomatal complexes on both the adaxial and abaxial surfaces,
thewidthof20stomatalbands,andthestomatalindexfromthreerandomly
selected ﬁelds of view. Mean values were calculated for each parameter.
Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning
All micro-CT scans were conducted using a Zeiss Xradia 520 Versa X-ray
microscope (Carl Zeiss AG) at the Institute of Paleontology, Yunnan Uni-
versity. The scanning parameters were as follows: detector mode 4X, vol-
tage/power 40 kV/3 W, exposure time 1.5–3 s per projection, total
projections1601,andreconstructedslices993–996.Theresultingvoxelsizes
were 1.13 μm for modern C. umbraculifera, 1.82 μm for B. ﬂabellifer,
1.46 μm for C. utan, 1.62 μm for the ancient manuscript M1, and
1.46 μm for M2.
The acquired TIFF-format image stacks were processed using ORS
Visual software (v.1.8.0.1913) for three-dimensional reconstruction and
visualization. Scanning was conducted in three orthogonal planes: trans-
verse (XZ), longitudinal (YZ), and horizontal (XY) (Fig. 2). Morphometric
analysis was carried out with ImageJ, including measurements of leaf
thickness (three ﬁelds of view), diameter of 20 longitudinal veins, spacing of
20 longitudinal veins, spacing of 20 abaxial ﬁber bundles, and the distance
ratio from 10 transverse veins to the adaxial and abaxial epidermis. Mean
values were calculated for each parameter. Terminology for anatomical
structures follows refs. 14,26–28.

Results
Epidermal morphology of three modern palm leaves
Key epidermal morphology characteristics, including stomata type, anti-
clinal wall types, stomata band width and their coefﬁcient of variation (CV),
stomatal index, were listed in Table 1 for better review and comparison.
The leaves of B. ﬂabellifer exhibit similar epidermal morphology on
bothadaxialand abaxialsurfaces,each bearing stomata covered bya distinct
cuticular layer. The stomata feature reniform (kidney-shaped) guard cells,
each ﬂanked by two subsidiary cells positioned at the poles of the long axis.
Stomatal length averages 61.15 µm (range: 52.94–73.19 µm). These stomata
are arranged linearly within the intercostal regions, forming bands 2 to 4
rows wide. Epidermal cells are elongated and rectangular, with straight
anticlinal walls (Fig. 3).
Stomata on the adaxial and abaxial leaf surfaces of C. umbraculifera are
similarinsize and type(Fig.4),with an average long-axislength of 29.12 µm
(range: 25.10–35.63 µm). They conform to the tetracytic type, characterized
by two reniform guard cells surrounded by four subsidiary cells. Differences
between surfaces were noted, however, in stomatal band morphology and
epidermal cell features.
Stomatal bands on the adaxial surface are broader and more widely
spaced than those on the abaxial surface. Adaxially, stomata are densely
arranged in the intercostal zones into bands composed of 2 to 5 rows, with
an average width of 111.09 µm (range: 69.11–160.39 µm; CV = 0.27). In
contrast, abaxial stomatal bands are notably narrower, averaging 68.66 µm
in width (range: 48.38–114.15 µm; CV = 0.25).
Epidermal cell morphology also differs between surfaces, parti-
cularly in anticlinal wall patterns. Adaxial epidermal cells are elongated-
rectangular with straight to undulate walls, whereas abaxial cells,
though polygonal, display exclusively straight anticlinal walls. Addi-
tionally, the abaxial surface shows a higher stomatal density, as reﬂected
in the stomatal index (adaxial: 13.58%; abaxial: 16.48%). This increase
likely results from the narrower spacing between stomatal bands on the
abaxial side.
In C. utan, stomatal type and size are consistent between adaxial and
abaxial surfaces. The tetracytic stomata consist of two reniform guard cells
surrounded by four subsidiary cells, with an average long-axis length of
27.42 µm (range: 21.23–32.80 µm). As in C. umbraculifera, distinct differ-
ences were observed between surfaces in stomatal band dimensions and
epidermal cell traits.
On the adaxial surface, stomata cluster in intercostal regions to form
bands of 1 to 4 rows, averaging 46.37 µm in width (range: 18.20–75.01 µm;
CV = 0.36). Abaxially, stomatal bands are typically single-rowed (occa-
sionally double-rowed), with an average width of 35.65 µm (range:
28.31–48.82 µm; CV = 0.14). These ﬁndings indicate generally narrower
stomatal bands on the abaxial surface. The stomatal index further differs
between surfaces (adaxial: 9.48%; abaxial: 16.96%), indicating a higher
proportional representation of stomata on the abaxial epidermis. The larger
coefﬁcient of variation on the adaxial side also pointsto greater variability in
stomatal band width.
Anticlinal wall morphology likewise varied between surfaces. While
epidermal cells were polygonal on both sides, adaxial cells displayed both
straight and undulate anticlinal walls, whereas abaxial cells showed only
straight walls—a pattern highly consistent with that observed in C.
umbraculifera (Fig. 5).

Discussion
The anatomical morphology of leaves from B. ﬂabellifer and Corypha spe-
cies exhibits clear distinctions, particularly in stomatal size, and type; epi-
dermal cells morphology; the distribution pattern of the sclerotic sheath and
venation architecture. These characteristics allow rapid differentiation
between the two taxa based on morphological observation.
The stomata of B. ﬂabellifer are signiﬁcantly larger than those of Cor-
ypha species. The epidermal cell features of the adaxial and abaxial surfaces
are markedly different in Corypha species, whereas they are consistent in B.
ﬂabellifer. Within the genus Corypha, subtle differences exist in anticlinal
cell wall patterns: the adaxial surface displays both straight and undulate
types, while the abaxial surface exhibits only straight walls. In contrast, both
epidermal surfaces of B. ﬂabellifer show exclusively the straight type.
Additionally, the abaxial epidermis of Corypha leaves generally bears a
higher stomatal density than the adaxial one, which is a common trait
among many plant species30. Considerable differences are also observed in
stomatal band width between the two epidermal surfaces in Corypha,
whereas B. ﬂabellifer shows relatively uniform stomatal band dimensions
across both surfaces.
Stomatal distribution is inﬂuenced by environmental factors such as
temperature and light intensity16. The stomatal patterning in Corypha and
Borassus may reﬂect adaptations to their distinct leaf orientations and
physiological traits. These intrinsic morphological and ecological differ-
ences providea practicalbasisforidentifyingthebotanicaloriginsof ancient
palm-leaf manuscripts.
The venation pattern of B. ﬂabellifer and Corypha species exhibits
distinct characteristics. The transverse veins of B. ﬂabellifer form a regular
grid-like (or brick-like) pattern. In contrast, the transverse veins of Corypha
speciesrunacrossthelongitudinalveinsinarelativelylooseandoverlapping
arrangement. This distinction can even be discerned with the naked eye.
Leaf structure of two species is quite differential. Corypha displays a
typical dorsiventral type, whereas those of B. ﬂabellifer are predominantly
isolateral31. This phenomenon could be associated with their different
growth characteristic. Generally, the isolateral leaves of many Arecaceae
species grow approximately perpendicular to the ground, which results in a
symmetrical structure throughout the leaf. In contrast, dorsiventral leaves

The differences between Corypha and B. ﬂabellifer are consistent with
previous studies6,7,15. Due to the signiﬁcant differences in growth patterns
and characteristics between the plants of the two different genera, the dif-
ferences in their leaf structures are quite obvious. It is relatively easy to
distinguish the leaves of the two species. By comparing venation and epi-
dermal morphology, the genus-level origin of ancient palm-leaf manu-
scripts can be rapidly identiﬁed non-destructively. However, further
differentiation among species within the genus Corypha remains
challenging.
To address this, cluster analysis based on quantitative anatomical traits
proved highly effective. The application of multivariate statistical analysis to
quantitative anatomical traits allows for reliable taxonomic identiﬁcation of
ancient palm-leaf manuscripts, reducing the potential bias introduced by
degraded preservation, obscured imaging, or incomplete morphological
visibility.
In thiscase,micro-CTscanningdemonstrates signiﬁcant advantages
in this study. Compared to traditional free-hand section techniques,
micro-CT scanning eliminates complex operational procedures and
enables rapid, non-destructive analysis of ancient specimens. Moreover,
the high-precision analytical capability signiﬁcantly enhances the
understanding of leaf anatomical structures, substantially improving the
accuracy of species identiﬁcation for palm-leaf manuscripts. In principle,
the contrast in X-ray images depends on the density, thickness, and
atomic composition of the sample32. However, this method performs
poorly in visualizing stomata and epidermal cells, with low contrast at
their edges. Therefore, obtaining epidermal features still requires tradi-
tional methods such as the Franklin technique, followed by observation
under an optical microscope. Future research should focus on improving
resolution and enhancing the imaging performance of micro-CT scan-
ning for palm-leaf manuscripts.
This study employed epidermal observation and non-destructive
micro-CT scanning to compare the epidermal and anatomical features of
leaves from three modern palm species and ancient Tibetan palm-leaf
manuscripts. Combined with cluster analysis, it successfully distinguished
the three modern palm species and identiﬁed the botanical source of the
ancient Tibetan manuscripts as C. umbraculifera (talipot palm). This
research represents the ﬁrst application of this technology to species iden-
tiﬁcation in ancient palm-leaf manuscripts, offering a novel approach that
integrates non-destructive techniques with taxonomic analysis. The ﬁnd-
ings provide a valuable reference for future studies in this ﬁeld.
