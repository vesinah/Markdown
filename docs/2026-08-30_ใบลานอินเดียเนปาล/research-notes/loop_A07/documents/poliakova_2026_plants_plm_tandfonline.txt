Published by Informa UK Limited, trading as Taylor & Francis Group.https://doi.org/10.1080/23818107.2026.2634848open_in_newISSN 2381-8107eISSN 2381-8115Article HistoryReceived July 3, 2025Accepted February 16, 2026Online March 20, 2026Published in issue July 3, 2026Pages 333 - 358AbstractAiming to reconstruct local historical and modern production of palm-leaf manuscripts (PLM) preliminarily dated from the 14th to the beginning of the 20th century CE in South (S) and Southeast (SE) Asia, we faced a great volume of publications available in ca 20 languages, which briefly describe the manuscript production process and/or focus on PLM preservation traditions. However, we found a lack of publications with a botanical or ethnobotanical focus, and there are none that thoroughly document the detailed process of PLM production. Consequently, we conducted the first comprehensive analysis of literature and online sources dating from 1753 to 2024, covering approximately 270 years, and compiled a list of about 300 publications concerning both modern and traditional methods of palm-leaf manuscript production and preservation. By performing a geographical analysis and mapping of all retrieved botanical and ethnobotanical data related to this research, we compiled lists of plants used for PLM production and conservation in S and SE Asia. These lists are region-specific (e.g. for Bengal, Gujarat, Odisha, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and South India as a whole), and country-specific (e.g. for Sri Lanka and Indonesia). The lists of plant names mentioned in the analyzed publications range from as few as three in Nepal and Malaysia to as many as 79 in India overall. These lists are region-specific (e.g. forBengal, Gujarat, Odisha, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and South India as a whole), and country-specific(e.g. for Sri Lanka and Indonesia). Various plants,specific to regions, cultures, historical periods, ormanufacturers, are utilised in the multi-step palm-leaf manuscript (PLM) preparation process. Eachstep involves close contact between the writing sup-port and other plants – from seasoning and boilingwith various fruits and herbs to brushing (e.g. withdiverse grasses, sand, shells, and ash), oiling, inking,and applying plant-based solutions for protectionagainst insects. Understanding the methods and mate-rials used in the preparation and conservation ofPLMs is thus crucial for several reasons, both cultu-rally and scientifically. Although early specimens arerarely extant, we know that PLMs are among the ear-liest forms of written records in many Asian societies,including India, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Indonesia(Ciotti 2021). The content of these manuscripts, ran-ging from literary, religious, and philosophical texts tothe first scientific treatises and legal and administra-tive documents, offers profound insights into the civi-lizations that crafted them, revealing their linguistic,artistic, and technological advancements. Preservingthese manuscripts means safeguarding the narrativesand knowledge of previous generations and maintain-ing a link to shared human history. Scientifically, thestudy of PLMs provides critical data for academicresearch in fields such as history, philology, anthro-pology, and natural science. Nishanthiet al. The study was based on an Internetsearch (Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex)for literature with the keywords “conservation of palmleaf”, “preservation of palm leaf”, and “conservationand preservation”, as indicated in the original paper.The authors performed citation analysis and describeda trend of a growing number of publications on PLMconservation. Wiland (2022, 2023) carried outa comprehensive review of PLM conservation witha historic overview, a brief description of the country-specific palm leaf preparations, materials, and mediaused for leaf preservation in the past and nowadays.This two-part review used the available conservationliterature and surveyed results to address the issuesinherent in the practical conservation of palm-leafartefacts. However, to the best of our knowledge,neither publications with a botanical or ethnobotani-cal focus, nor lists of plants used for PLM preparationor conservation in specific regions or countries arecurrently available.With the aim of understanding what plants werehistorically used for the production of extant PLMsand which of them are still used nowadays in areaswhere PLMs are still produced (e.g. Bali), we per-formed a comprehensive analysis of literature andonline publications published from 1753 (i.e. Palmaeflabellifoliae 1753) to 2024 (i.e. Sun et al. 2024). Theresults of the work presented here will help us in theinterpretation of the palaeoecological and palaeoge-nomic data obtained during our material studies ofancient and modern PLMs from S and SE Asia(Poliakova et al. 2025). Furthermore, from the pointof view of cultural history, knowing in more detailwhat plants were involved in the production of PLMscan allow us to identify local production practices,thus providing us with a more nuanced understandingof the history of manuscript production, and investi-gating matters of continuity and change across differ-ent regions and sub-regions where this artisanaltradition has been thriving.Material and methodsLiterature search and algorithmizingA systematic literature review was conducted in orderto build up a comprehensive resource on the plantsregionally used for production and/or traditional pre-servation of PLMs. We used four main Internet plat-forms for searching for academic publications, namelythe Web of Science, Google Scholar, Mendeley, andScience Direct. The publication records were collectedthrough the search function across all databases, usingthe following keywords: “palm-leaf manuscript*” OR“palm leaf manuscript*” OR “palm manuscript*” OR“Pothi palm manuscript*” OR “tala palm manu-script*” OR “sritala palm manuscript*” OR “lontar”OR “writing on palm leaves” OR “writing on leaves ofpalms” OR “palm-leaf manuscript creation” (*create)OR “palm-leaf manuscript making” (*make) OR“palm-leaf manuscript production” (*produce) OR“palm-leaf manuscript conservation” OR “palm-leafmanuscript preservation”. We did not activate anyinternal automatic filters in the databases. The searchwas performed mainly in English, French, German,Polish, and Russian; translation of the keywords intoSouth and Southeast Asian languages was performedusing automatic translation through GoogleTranslator.We included in the analysis any literature andonline sources that mentioned botanical (regardlessof whether correctly or incorrectly spelled), trivial, orlocal names of plants used either for PLM productionor preservation. The following selection criteria wereapplied during the final compilation of the publicationlist: (a) The publication (i.e. literature or Internetsource of information) contains study (studies) ora revision on plants of any species, genera, and/orfamilies used in the PLM production process, in thePLM conservation (preservation) process, or for both.(b) The publication contains clear reference(s) to theplant names in any form and language. (c) The pub-lication contains clear indication of its author(s),production year, and issuing details. If the older syno-nym of the taxon is coherently referred to in thehistorical literature, the modern plant name is usedin this study (i.e. Ocimum tenuiflorum L. for Ocimumsanctum L.).
