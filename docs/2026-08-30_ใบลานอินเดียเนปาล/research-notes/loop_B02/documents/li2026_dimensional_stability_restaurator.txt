Abstract: Environmental humidity can induce irreversible deformation of manuscript supports (expansion, contraction, bending), threatening long-term preservation. Using a controlled-variable design, we exposed Tibetan paper, palm leaf, and 
birch bark supports to ﬁve constant relative humidities (30.0 %, 55.3 %, 76.2 %, 86.0 %, 
and 95.0 %) and monitored moisture content, dimensional change, and deformation 
for 145 days. Structural and compositional diﬀerences were considered to compare 
underlying deformation mechanisms. Deformation in all supports increased 
signiﬁcantly with humidity, and the magnitude, rate, and patterns matched their 
hygroscopic/desorption behaviour. Palm leaves showed the largest expansion– 
contraction, followed by birch bark, while Tibetan paper exhibited the best dimensional and planar stability. Palm-leaf deformation is attributed to highly hygroscopic 
constituents, a loose microstructure and oriented vascular bundles. Birch-bark 
lateral curling is governed by structural anisotropy and contrasting hydrophilicity 
between inner and outer surfaces. Tibetan paper remains stable due to its dense ﬁbre 
network and traditional manufacturing. Based on the results, ca. 55 % RH is identiﬁed as an optimal general storage humidity for all three supports, where projection 
and expansion ratios fall within relatively stable ranges. These ﬁndings establish a

1 Introduction
Sanskrit and historical Tibetan manuscripts form an important part of many collections and possess immense cultural heritage and research value. The religious, 
scientiﬁc, and cultural knowledge recorded in these manuscripts constitutes an 
irreplaceable primary source for the study of historical societies and traditions. 
These manuscripts are found on three principal support materials: Tibetan paper,

palm leaf, and birch bark. Like all organic materials, these manuscripts are 
vulnerable to deterioration from environmental factors such as micro-organisms, 
moisture, light, and airborne particulates, as well as from handling and transportation (Zhang 2021; ICCROM 2018). Of these factors, humidity is particularly 
critical: the hygroscopic and desorption behaviour of cellulose ﬁbres governs the 
expansion, contraction, and deformation of the support materials, potentially 
resulting in irreversible damage such as shrinkage, warping, embrittlement, and 
delamination (Li 2001). Survey data indicate that approximately 30 % of palm leaf 
manuscripts in China have suﬀered signiﬁcant deformation due to inadequate humidity control (Song 2023); birch bark manuscripts have undergone irreversible 
bending as a result of long-term humidity ﬂuctuations, and Tibetan paper manuscripts have been subject to mould growth or embrittlement under extreme humidity conditions. The three supports diﬀer markedly in structure and chemical 
composition, resulting in distinct responses to humidity: Tibetan paper forms a 
dense ﬁbre network through traditional stone-mortar beating and cooking process 
(Wei 2021); palm leaf has an epidermis–mesophyll–epidermis sandwich structure; 
and birch bark is characterised by structural asymmetry between the cork layer and 
the phloem. These structural characteristics directly determine how each support 
responds to humidity. Regional climate conditions add further complexity: the 
tropical humidity of Southeast Asia with an annual average relative humidity (RH) of 
75–85 % promotes mould growth on palm leaf (Yani et al. 2025); the arid conditions 
favour embrittlement of birch bark (Korosteleva and Sizova 2024); and the temperate 
continental climate experienced in many Tibetan paper collection sites (RH 40–70 %) 
causes the material to expand or contract. While a body of research exists on the 
humidity response and deformation behaviour of each of these supports individually, systematic comparative studies remain lacking, making it diﬃcult to establish 
the performance diﬀerences and interrelationships of the three materials across 
varying humidity conditions.
Research on palm leaf as a manuscript support has advanced considerably. Yi 
et al. (2024) identiﬁed lignin and hemicellulose content and crystallinity as key 
determinants of deformation sensitivity, noting that thickness diﬀerences between 
mesophyll tissue and epidermis generate uneven stress during moisture ﬂuctuation. 
Sharma et al. (2018) characterised the structure of 18th-century Indian palm leaf 
manuscripts, demonstrating that ﬁbre orientation and cell wall thickness distribution govern the dominant direction of bending deformation, further reinforced by 
vascular bundles aligned along the long axis. Yu et al. (2024) found that traditional 
steaming techniques reduce hemicellulose content and thereby limit expansion 
under high humidity, while Zhang et al. (2024) conﬁrmed experimentally that 
bending strength decreases signiﬁcantly with increasing humidity, identifying 50 % 
RH as optimal for mechanical stability. More recent studies have broadened the

scope of investigation: Sathiyamani et al. (2024) showed that ink composition may 
accelerate localised ﬁbre degradation, indirectly increasing deformation inhomogeneity; Chu et al. (2023) identiﬁed mould growth and hemicellulose degradation as the principal coupled factors driving deformation under high humidity; 
and Wiland et al. (2022), in a systematic review, conﬁrmed humidity control and raw 
material characterisation as the central research priorities for palm leaf manuscript 
preservation.
Research on birch bark has focused primarily on the inﬂuence of structural 
asymmetry on humidity response. Orsini et al. (2015) demonstrated that in humid 
conditions, lignin hydrolysis leads to microstructural degradation and weakening of 
inter-ﬁbre bonding. Klügl and Di Pietro (2021) measured adsorption isotherms of 
historic and modern birch bark, ﬁnding that water vapour plasticises the material 
and induces outward bending, with diﬀerential moisture expansion rates in longitudinal and transverse ﬁbres governing bending direction; this anisotropy was 
attributed to structural diﬀerences between the phloem and sclerenchyma layers. 
The same authors also compiled an archaeological micro-degradation map of birch 
bark, establishing correlations between macroscopic damage and microstructural 
features. Birch bark is inherently fragile, particularly once its layered structure has 
been compromised, making it susceptible to bending, folding or fracture under 
mechanical stress or improper handling. The birch bark artefacts recovered from 
Han dynasty tombs, for instance, were already deformed upon excavation and 
required ﬂattening and conservation treatment to restore their original form, 
illustrating the capacity of long-term burial conditions and environmental stress to 
induce macroscopic deformation (Beekman 2020).
Research on the humidity response and dimensional stability of Tibetan paper is 
relatively well developed. Yi et al. (2020) employed accelerated heat-humidity ageing 
and found that wet-heat treatment signiﬁcantly reduces dimensional stability, with 
shrinkage ratio ﬂuctuation decreasing by 30 % after 145 days of ageing. Liu et al. 
(2022) conﬁrmed that the traditional stone-mortar pulping process fully ﬁbrillates 
the ﬁbres, enhancing inter-ﬁbre bonding and reducing hygroscopicity. Huang (1992) 
clariﬁed that subsequent boiling and drying processes further balance ﬁbre properties, resulting in low hygroscopic characteristics. Zhang et al. (2022), in a study of 
Tibetan paper manuscripts from the Potala Palace, demonstrated that starch sizing 
further reduces hygroscopicity, maintaining equilibrium moisture content at 5–7 %, 
and proposed a storage humidity range of 45–60 % RH.
Despite this body of research, signiﬁcant gaps remain. Most studies address a 
single support material, and systematic comparative analyses – particularly quantitative assessments of the relationships between macroscopic properties under 
varying humidity conditions – are lacking. The diﬀerential hygroscopic behaviour of 
the three supports under equivalent environmental conditions, for instance, has not

2 Materials and Methods
2.1 Experimental Materials
2.1.1 Materials
Three types of support materials were selected, with ﬁve samples prepared for each 
type. All samples were uniformly cut into rectangles of 10 × 4 cm to ensure consistency in initial dimensions. The sample pretreatment strictly followed the experimental speciﬁcations for cultural heritage protection. To reduce errors, a vernier 
calliper was used for accurate dimension measurement in subsequent steps.
Tibetan paper:
– Date of production unknown.
– Manufactured using traditional Tibetan techniques (raw material preparation, 
soaking, stone-mortar beating, sheet formation and drying).
– Fibres derived from Thymelaeaceae plants (Daphne tangutica).
– Thickness approximately 0.18 mm.
Palm leaves:
– Collected from Xishuangbanna, Yunnan, in 2021.
– Processed through traditional techniques (cutting, cooking, cleaning, and 
drying).
– Raw material: Corypha umbraculifera, with a thickness of approximately 
0.35 mm.
Birch bark:
– Purchased in 2021.
– Prepared by removal of the outer bark, cutting and drying.
– Inner ﬁbre layer and outer waxy layer retained; thickness approximately 
0.28 mm.

2.2 Experimental Design
2.2.1 Moisture Content
Regular measurements of the moisture content of samples are conducted using a 
paper moisture meter. Moisture content is a key indicator reﬂecting the humidity 
response characteristics of the supports, while ambient relative humidity is a critical 
factor aﬀecting the stability of materials such as archival paper, palm leaves, and 
birch bark, directly determining the expansion and deformation patterns of these 
three supports. Measuring moisture content enables quantitative analysis of the 
response of the three supports under varying humidity conditions, clariﬁes the 
relationship between environmental humidity and support deformation, and 
further elucidates the hygroscopic and desorption behaviour of cellulose ﬁbres as 
governed by their diﬀering structural and chemical characteristics. This clariﬁes the 
macroscopic deformation behaviour of the three supports under varying RH conditions (including shrinkage, curling, and bending), providing quantitative data to 
support investigation of deformation mechanisms and the optimisation of storage 
humidity recommendations. In this study, the change in moisture content (observed 
value minus initial value) is introduced to eliminate individual diﬀerences in the 
initial moisture content of the samples. A positive value indicates hygroscopicity by 
the sample, whereas a negative value indicates desorption. Moisture content of the 
three supports was measured at regular intervals using a paper moisture meter 
(SH-01, Shenzhen Aigerui Instrument Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), operating on a 
high-frequency wave principle; values are expressed as percentages.
2.2.2 Expansion Length and Width
Prior to testing, all three supports were cut to rectangular samples and their initial 
length and width measured using vernier callipers (Mitutoyo 500-196-30, accuracy 
0.01 mm). Samples were then placed in the humidity environments speciﬁed for each 
experimental condition. Following exposure, the supports exhibited varying degrees 
and types of deformation, including edge warping and wrinkling. Samples were 
removed and gently ﬂattened on a horizontal surface before length and width were 
measured. The expansion ratio (deﬁned below) was used to characterise the 
expansion and contraction behaviour of each material.

The expansion ratio is deﬁned as the ratio of the sample area after exposure to its 
initial area. A value >1 indicates expansion, a value <1 indicates contraction, and a 
value approaching 1 indicates dimensional stability.
2.2.3 Projected Length and Width
The projected length and width of each sample were measured using vernier calipers 
prior to humidity exposure. As buckling or bending increases, projected dimensions 
decrease relative to the ﬂattened dimensions, providing a measure of deformation 
severity. The projection ratio is deﬁned as follows:
Projection ratio = Projection length × Projection width 
Stretch length × stretch width
(2)
The projection ratio is deﬁned as the ratio of the area projected by the sample onto a 
horizontal plane to its ﬂattened area. The projection ratio is always ≤1; lower values 
indicate greater deformation.
2.2.4 Bending Angle
Rectangular samples subject to deformation frequently exhibited U-shaped bending 
in longitudinal section, with upward curling along both long edges. In such cases, the 
bending angle on each side was measured. Where deformation did not conform to a 
U-shaped proﬁle, the deformation pattern was recorded descriptively without 
assignment of an angle value. Photographic documentation was made of all samples 
to facilitate visual assessment of deformation characteristics.
2.3 Experimental Steps
(1) Sample preparation and humidity environments: samples were cut at room 
temperature (23 °C); ﬁve desiccators were conditioned using aqueous CaCl 2 
solutions of diﬀerent concentrations (w/w) to establish constant humidity environments of 30.0 %, 55.3 %, 76.2 %, 86.0 %, and 95.0 % RH. Anhydrous CaCl 2 
was dissolved in deionized water to prepare the required CaCl 2 –H 2 O solutions, 
and the RH in each desiccator was allowed to equilibrate and then veriﬁed 
using a calibrated hygrometer.
(2) Initial measurements: moisture content, length, width, and projected dimensions were recorded for each sample prior to exposure.
(3) Sample placement: one sample each of Tibetan paper, palm leaf and birch bark 
was placed in each desiccator, which was then sealed and maintained at a

Birch bark has a higher lignin content along with a certain amount of hydrophilic polysaccharides. It undergoes only simple debarking and drying, preserving its 
structure and components intact, and its moisture absorption capacity lies between 
the other two materials (Orsini et al. 2015). The moisture absorption properties of 
Tibetan paper are determined by its ﬁbrous raw materials and pore structure. Made 
from ﬁbres of the Thymelaeaceae plant, Tibetan paper features high cellulose 
crystallinity and low hemicellulose content, which limits its hydrophilicity and 
moisture absorption (Cai & Zhang 2018).
The microstructure of the three supports diﬀers:
(1) Palm leaves possess an epidermis-mesophyll-epidermis sandwich structure, 
with loose, highly porous mesophyll tissue internally, oﬀering a large speciﬁc 
surface area that provides numerous adsorption sites for water vapour while 
accelerating moisture ingress and egress.
(2) Birch bark contains cork layer cavities and a layered structure. Although it has 
some water storage capacity, its outer waxy layer slows moisture migration, 
resulting in slower absorption rates and lower amplitudes compared to palm 
leaves.
(3) In terms of papermaking techniques, the distinctive processes used for Tibetan 
paper such as beating, steaming, sun-drying, and sizing (e.g., with starch) along 
with the formation of a densely interwoven ﬁbre network through stonemortar beating, further reduce its porosity and pore size. This increases 
resistance to water molecule diﬀusion, thereby limiting the moisture absorption capacity of the ﬁbres to a certain extent (Suolang & Qimei 1996; Chen et al. 
2024).

3.2.3 Expansion and Deformation of Birch Bark
The dimensional behaviour of birch bark follows the same general pattern: higher 
RH produces greater expansion, while lower RH produces contraction (Figures 11 
and 12). Notably, mould growth at 95 % RH appears less severe than at 86 % RH. The 
maximum expansion ratio is approximately 2 %, while the maximum shrinkage ratio 
at 30 % RH approaches 0 % (Table 10), indicating that birch bark shows little tendency 
to contract. In terms of deformation, birch bark displays a relatively consistent 
deformation mode, typically U-shaped bending with upward curling along the short 
edges (Figures 13 and 14). Deformation increases with moisture uptake, indicating 
comparatively poor dimensional stability.

3.2.4.2 Projection Ratio: Trends and Mechanisms
The projection ratio provides a direct measure of non-uniform deformation such as 
warping and curling; a larger ﬂuctuation range indicates poorer planar stability and 
greater susceptibility to damage that may aﬀect manuscript readability. The projection ratios of all three supports show trends broadly consistent with their 
expansion ratios, but the underlying mechanisms relate more speciﬁcally to structural asymmetry and uneven moisture migration.
Palm leaf shows the largest ﬂuctuation range in projection ratio (0.85–0.99) and 
the greatest tendency towards warping. Its upper and lower epidermis are dense and 
high in wax content, while the mesophyll tissue is loose and rich in hydrophilic 
components. During moisture uptake and release, the hygroscopic and desorption 
rates of the mesophyll tissue substantially exceed those of the epidermis, generating 
a pronounced moisture gradient across the thickness of the leaf and a directional 
warping moment that causes the material to curl towards the epidermis side 
(Thommes et al. 2015). Additionally, the vascular bundles of palm leaf are oriented 
along the long axis (Zhang et al. 2024, 2025), producing anisotropic expansion and 
contraction that further intensiﬁes warping deformation. Traditional cooking 
treatment partially damages the wax layer of the epidermis, exacerbating uneven 
moisture migration and increasing the amplitude of projection ratio ﬂuctuation 
(Huang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2022).
Birch bark shows the second largest ﬂuctuation range in projection ratio (0.87– 
0.99) (Table 11), primarily manifesting as lateral curling. This behaviour derives from 
the structural asymmetry and contrasting hydrophilicity of the cork layer and 
phloem: the outer cork layer is rich in wax and strongly hydrophobic, with slow 
water penetration, while the inner phloem is rich in hydrophilic polysaccharides and 
absorbs moisture rapidly (Klügl & Di Pietro 2021, 45). This diﬀerential moisture 
uptake and expansion across the two layers causes birch bark to curl consistently 
towards the outer (cork) side, with curling becoming more pronounced at higher

planar integrity. The weak inter-layer bonding of the layered structure further 
promotes micro-displacement between layers under moisture stress, amplifying 
projection ratio ﬂuctuation.
The projection ratio of Tibetan paper remains consistently close to 1.00 (ﬂuctuation range 0.90–1.00), indicating markedly superior planar stability. This is 
attributable to the structural homogeneity of the material: stone-mortar beating fully 
ﬁbrillates the ﬁbres, producing a uniformly interwoven network with no preferential ﬁbre orientation and a fully symmetrical cross-sectional structure (Jia et al. 
2021). Moisture migration is therefore uniform, generating no signiﬁcant moisture 
gradient or diﬀerential internal stress across the thickness. Starch sizing further ﬁlls 
inter-ﬁbre voids and reduces variability in water penetration (Janssen 2025), 
ensuring that expansion and contraction on both faces remain essentially equivalent 
across humidity conditions (Vibert et al. 2023) and that internal stresses cancel rather 
than accumulate. As a result, Tibetan paper resists warping and curling and maintains planar integrity across a wide range of humidity environments.
From a preservation perspective, the greater the deviation in projection ratio, 
the more blurred the manuscript handwriting and the more severe the page adhesion will be, which increases the diﬃculty of restoration (Zou 2024; Zhang & Song 
2024, Lyu & Zhang 2020). At 55.3 % RH, the projection ratios of both Tibetan paper and 
palm leaves fall within the optimal stable range (Tibetan paper: 0.99–1.00; palm 
leaves: 1.00–1.01), with no obvious warping or curling deformation. Although birch 
bark shows a relatively large variation in projection ratio (0.87–1.00) at 55.3 % RH, its 
bending angle becomes moderate. This further veriﬁes that 55 % RH is an appropriate storage humidity for all three support types and provides an empirical basis 
for the planar stability of manuscript supports across diverse regional contexts.
The three supports also diﬀer in bending direction. Tibetan paper and palm leaf 
both exhibit upward bending along the long edges, while birch bark bends upward 
along the short edges under all humidity conditions. In Tibetan paper, long-edge 
bending is a localised phenomenon occurring only at low humidity, attributed to 
slightly higher ﬁbre density along the long axis resulting from water ﬂow direction 
during sheet formation, which produces greater stress accumulation in this direction 
upon desorption (Bergander & Salmén 2002). In palm leaf, long-edge bending is an 
inherent deformation characteristic, driven by the directional arrangement of 
vascular bundles along the long axis; their lower expansion-contraction rate relative 
to the surrounding parenchyma cells generates a continuous upward bending 
moment along the long edge during humidity change (Yu et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2024). 
Short-edge bending in birch bark directly reﬂects structural anisotropy: the short 
edges are parallel to the transverse ﬁbres, which absorb moisture and expand at a 
substantially higher rate than longitudinal ﬁbres; combined with diﬀerential

In summary, the humidity-induced deformation of all three supports results 
from the interaction of environmental humidity and the intrinsic material properties of each support. Tibetan paper achieves superior humidity stability through the 
dual optimisation of structure and chemical composition aﬀorded by traditional 
manufacturing processes. Deformation in palm leaf is governed primarily by 
structural ﬁbre orientation, and in birch bark by structural anisotropy. These ﬁndings provide the experimental and mechanistic basis for the targeted formulation of 
preservation strategies for individual supports and mixed-support collections.
4 Conclusions
This study monitored three manuscript supports – Tibetan paper, palm leaf, and 
birch bark – under controlled humidity conditions over 145 days, systematically 
analysing changes in moisture content, dimensional behaviour, and deformation 
characteristics. The humidity response patterns and deformation mechanisms of 
each support were compared and interpreted, and targeted preservation strategies 
were formulated based on the ﬁndings. The principal conclusions are as follows:
Tibetan paper exhibits the greatest dimensional stability of the three supports, 
characterised by low hygroscopicity and limited expansion and contraction. Moisture content ranges from 3.5 % to 7.2 % across the humidity conditions tested. Below 
55 % RH, upward bending along the long edges occurs; the recommended storage 
humidity is 55–60 % RH. The stability of Tibetan paper derives from the synergistic 
eﬀects of traditional manufacturing processes: stone-mortar beating fully ﬁbrillates 
the ﬁbres to form a dense network; cooking reduces hemicellulose content and 
thereby lowers hygroscopicity; and starch sizing combined with calcium carbonate 
ﬁlling produces a uniform ﬁbre structure with low chemical hydrophilicity. Under 
humidity ﬂuctuation, stress is distributed evenly in all directions, with no pronounced tendency towards directional deformation.
Palm leaf exhibits the greatest hygroscopicity and desorption capacity of the 
three supports, with moisture content ranging from 4.5 % to 14.2 % across the humidity conditions tested, and the largest expansion and contraction amplitude, 
making it particularly sensitive to low humidity. Signiﬁcant upward bending along 
the long edges occurs at RH below 55 %. The recommended storage humidity is 50– 
70 % RH, with an optimal range of 50–55 % RH. Directional deformation in palm leaf 
is governed by the combined eﬀects of structural heterogeneity and chemical hydrophilicity: the epidermis–mesophyll–epidermis sandwich structure produces

uneven expansion and contraction across layers during humidity change; the 
directional arrangement of vascular bundles along the long axis generates a 
continuous bending moment; and high hemicellulose and pectin content increases 
hygroscopicity, amplifying deformation. Diﬀerential ﬁbre expansion and contraction between ink and uninscribed areas may further exacerbate localised 
deformation.
Birch bark exhibits U-shaped bending along the short edges under all humidity 
conditions tested, with bending angles ranging from 3° to 32° (Table 12), making it the 
less dimensionally stable of the three supports. Moisture content ranges from 5.2 % to 
10.8 % across the humidity conditions tested. The recommended storage humidity is 
50–60 % RH. Directional deformation in birch bark is primarily governed by structural anisotropy: diﬀerential moisture absorption and expansion rates between 
longitudinal and transverse ﬁbres produce greater dimensional change along the 
short axis; the contrast between the loose inner ﬁbre layers and the wax-rich outer 
layer generates an outward bending moment; moisture adsorption within the cavity 
structure of the cork layer further ampliﬁes deformation; and lignin hydrolysis 
under humid conditions progressively loosens the ﬁbre structure, increasing 
deformation over time.
Both excessively high and low moisture content are detrimental to manuscript 
preservation. High moisture content promotes mould growth and associated support 
degradation, while low moisture content causes embrittlement and deformation, 
particularly in palm leaf and birch bark, which are poorly suited to prolonged 
storage in dry conditions (Sah 2002). Based on the experimental results, an RH of 
approximately 55 % is considered compatible with all three supports and is proposed 
as a uniﬁed humidity benchmark for mixed-support manuscript collections. Combined with a constant temperature of 18–20 °C and humidity ﬂuctuation control 
within ±5 %, deformation risk across all three supports can be minimised. Customised physical support measures tailored to the deformation characteristics of each 
material can further enhance preservation outcomes: Tibetan paper requires no 
additional support; palm leaf beneﬁts from a lightweight ﬁbre mat providing support 
along the long edges; and birch bark should be housed in a U-shaped frame to limit 
short-edge bending. Together, these measures provide a framework for the evidencebased preservation of mixed-support manuscript collections.
This study addresses a gap in the comparative analysis of the macroscopic 
properties of three principal manuscript support materials, establishing a framework linking environmental humidity, macroscopic deformation and underlying 
material mechanisms, and oﬀering new insights for humidity management and 
preservation of cellulose-based cultural heritage. Future research directions include 
investigation of the eﬀects of ﬂuctuating temperature and humidity and extended 
ageing (beyond 500 days) on deformation behaviour, with a view to optimising

laboratory ﬁndings in actual regional storage environments would further support

support materials with expansion and contraction properties matched to those of the 
manuscript supports, and expanded investigation of the interaction between 
inscription layers (inks and pigments) and hygroscopic deformation, together 
providing a more comprehensive technical foundation for the long-term preservation of manuscript collections.
