Prefatory Note to the Second Edition

A preliminary version of this paper was orally communicated and circu-
lated at the Third Conference of the International Association of Sanskrit
Studies held in Paris in June 1977. It was read and circulated again on
September 24, 1977, at a lecture which was organized by the Reiyukai
Library and took place in the Kokusai Bunka Kaikan (International
House of Japan), Tokyo. Thereafter the text of lecture was printed in a
slightly revised version. It is a great pleasure for me to notice that what
was originally meant as a preliminary communication, subsequently be-
came the starting point of further research work on the two poets
Haribhatta and Gopadatta and much progress could be made over the
past fifteen years: the majority of the unpublished legends discussed in
my paper are now (or will shortly become) accessible through critical
editions and, partly, translations into Western languages; the attribution
of several anonymous legends to Gopadatta could be proven beyond
any doubt in at least one case; and the dates of Haribhatta and Gopa-
datta could be determined much more precisely than was possible in
1977.

The first edition of this lecture went out of print several years ago and
since some of the information it contains might still be useful for those
interested in the Buddhist narrative literature, especially in its most
ornate form, I am extremely grateful to Dr. Akira YUYAMA, Director of
The International Institute for Buddhist Studies, for his kind offer, to
reprint it in a revised and enlarged form.

In the present second edition the main text of the lecture was basically
left unaltered. Only a few factual errors are corrected and hints on new
results and insights concerning some of the problems dealt with in the
lecture are given partly in the main text, partly in the footnotes. The
supplement has been expanded by three more legends from Haribhat-
ta’s Jatakamala. First, because of the (relative) simplicity and beauty of
their style the stories offer excellent reading material for students of
Sanskrit who are in their second or third year. Second, together with the
text of the Mrgajataka (No. 11) which has already been published by
HAHN/KLAUS now all the extant stories belonging to the section on the

1. The Sanskrit studies during the second half of the twentieth cen-
tury, noted for their extended and more detailed research on the diverse
branches of Sanskrit literature, are above all characterized as a second
attempt at achieving a synthesis. Suffice it here to refer to only three
long-term, large-scale projects:

a) the new Sanskrit Dictionary on Historical Principles which is being
prepared under the direction of Dr. S. M. KATRE (now succeeded by Dr.
A. M. GHATAGE) and will replace the Sanskrit dictionary of BOHTLINGK
and ROTH;

b) the new History of Indian Literature which is being prepared by nume-
rous specialists under the direction of Prof. Dr. Jan GONDA and will su-
persede the Geschichte der indischen Litteratur by WINTERNITZ;

c) the New Catalogus Catalogorum which is being edited under the direc-
tion of Dr. V. RAGHAVAN (now succeeded by Dr. K. KUNJUNNI RAJA)
and will replace the meritorious, but meanwhile out-dated Catalogus Ca-
talogorum by AUFRECHT.

Further, there are a number of smaller projects in various countries
which may go further into details than with the large-scale projects. In
the Federal Republic of Germany the noteworthy series Katalogisierung
der orientalischen Handschriften in Deutschland ("Cataloguing of the Orien-
tal Manuscripts in Germany"), which has so far presented more than
forty volumes with detailed descriptions of manuscripts from various
countries including India, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Burma, Thailand and Cen-
tral Asia.

Apart from this cataloguing of already available library stocks, numer-
ous hitherto unknown manuscripts and texts are at present being made
accessible. This frequently presents unexpected and valuable additions

time ago I made a first attempt at typologizing these texts, an attempt
which will in the course of time certainly be improved upon from the
point of view of its theoretical basis as well as the material covered. At

The collections of legends which I would like to present here fall under
items 3.3 and 3.4. It may be said without any exaggeration that the
Campii form, which is a mixture of ornate prose and verse, presents in
terms of literary history the culminating point in the artistic composi-
tions of Buddhist narrations. In it, something resembling a typical form
was created which—as far as we know—has no parallel in the Brahmin
literature of that period.

Until now this kind of literature has been known to us in the Sanskrit
original by only one completely preserved work — Aryasiira’s Jataka-

the lack of material for comparison, scholars formerly tended to regard
the Maitrakanyakavadana, too, as a work of Aryastira, who alone was
considered to have been able to write in this style.

4. The first of the two authors of Jatakamalds in the succession of Ar-
yasiira is Haribhatta, allegedly a prince who voluntarily put an end to
his life in the Himalayas. Since the first reference to his work by F. W.
THOMAS in 1904 and the four legends which I have published since 1971
Haribhatta no longer remains an unknown poet. I was of the opinion
that his Jatakamala consisting of 35 legends no longer existed in Sanskrit
but only in Tibetan translation. But in 1974 I discovered two almost
identical sets of Haribhatta’s legends in their Sanskrit original in two of
the more recent collections of Buddhist birth-stories from Nepal—nine
in the Avadanasarasamuccaya and eleven in the Jatakamdlavadanasittra.

The manuscripts of these two collections so far available to me—one of
the Avadanasarasamuccaya and three of the Jatakamalavaddnasiitra—are in
such a bad state that the correct text of Haribhatta’s legends could only
be established by the help of the Tibetan translation. In the National
Archives of Kathmandu however, a manuscript has been preserved
which apparently represents the source for the two collections. This
manuscript of 46 folios bears the title Bodhisattvajatakavadanamala. It
contains 13 legends which will be listed below, in Table II. Folios 32 to
37, however, were removed from the text long ago and added to the
Divydvadana manuscript which has been edited by COWELL and NEIL. In
this way the Maitrakanyakavadana found its way into the Divyavadana,
where it does not belong at all. It may be said that it originally stood in
a much more suitable context. The remarkable thing about the Bodhisatt-
vajatakavadanamala manuscript is that it obviously represents an extrac-
tion from a lost manuscript which at that time was complete in that it
contained the full text of Haribhatta’s Jatakamala. This fact is revealed by
the colophons of legends 2 through 7, because there the legends are call-
ed the fourth, fifth, sixth, first, ninth, and tenth, contrary to their real
position in the Bodhisattvajatakavadanamala manuscript. This numbering
of the colophons corresponds precisely to the original numbering of
Haribhatta’s complete Jatakamala as we know it from its Tibetan transla-
tion. In the Tibetan translation, it may be found that the legends are
counted by decades; so that the eleventh legend becomes again the first
(i.e., of the second decade), the nineteenth the ninth and the twentieth
the tenth.

The legends preserved in their Sanskrit original make it possible to
better judge the literary value of Haribhatta’s Jatakamala than on the
basis of the Tibetan translation. As regards his literary technique, Hari-
bhatta predominantly follows the style of Aryasiira. Like his predeces-
sor he strives for a balanced proportion of prose and verse and for a
meaningful division of the functions of gadya and padya. As a rule, prose
is used for detailed descriptions of persons and circumstances. The com-
pounds of differing lengths, the appropriate use of chains of long and
short syllables, the use of rough and smooth consonant clusters combine
to yield a picture whose characteristic vividness is brought out more
effectively than in verse. This type of prose tends to have a static and
retarding character with regard to advancing the story line. There is a
manifest tendency with Haribhatta to reduce the prose towards the end
of a narration more and more. The same may be observed with Arya-
Sura, though to a much lesser extent. Haribhatta writes in verse mainly
for statements of a more general nature, for forms similar to subhdsitas,
for descriptions whose vividness is enhanced by the accompaniment of
a certain rhythm, and for parts which advance the plot. The verses
therefore have a more dynamic and accelerating character.

As regards the number of metre and their use, there is conformity with
Aryasira to a large extent. In the first ten of the tales preserved in
Sanskrit, Haribhatta makes use of 25 different metres of which all but
five are found with Aryasira, i.e., Mafijubhasini, Rathoddhata, Vaisva-
devi, Sragdhara, and Harini. The metrical skeleton of the narrations
consists of only the few basic metres of Anustubh, Vaméastha, Vas-
antatilaka, Malabharini, and Sardilavikridita. These are to be found in
the more general parts of the legends. In specific and prominent places
the more unusual metres are employed, but long sections composed in
the same metre are rare. A survey of the metres of the preserved le-
gends of Haribhatta’s Jatakamala is given in Table III (see next page).*

As with Aryagiira, the beginning and ending of narrations are standar-
dized. First, a moral is given which is to be illustrated by the tale. Then
the story is introduced by the stereotyped formula, tad yathanusriyate,
which is generally followed by the introduction of a Bodhisattva, a long
phrase in prose. In most cases, a metrical description of his particular
qualities then follows. The tale as such usually ends with some stanzas
markedly contrasted with the concluding repetition of the moral in
prose starting with tad evam. Haribhatta differs from Aryasira by not
formulating the moral at the beginning in prose, but by using a stanza
which is always an Arya. The introductory phrase in prose following
the tad yathanusriyate is also constructed in a different manner; it al-
ways starts with a series of attributive compounds. This contrasts with
AryaSiira who apart from three exceptions always uses the following
pattern for the first sentences:

bodhisattva(bhita)h kila(yam bhagavan) ....... babhtiva (or: cakara; or:
janma pratilebhe)

I have already mentioned the fundamental differences between Hari-
bhatta and Aryasiira as regards the structure of their legends. Aryastra
for the greater part sticks to the development of the plot of the material
on which he based his tales and which is mostly to be found in the Pali
Jataka. Frequently his stanzas are literally taken from the Pali and trans-
lated into Sanskrit. His concern is above all the formal elaboration of the
legends through the medium of kavya techniques. His additions serve
mainly to illustrate as clearly as possible the moral virtue (paramita)
manifested in each tale. In contrast, Haribhatta presents a new and ori-
ginal adaptation of a known subject. His aim is obviously to achieve, as
far as possible, a convincing characterization of the figures involved as
well as a consistent development of the plot. In general terms one might
say that AryaStira, with his somewhat more archaic style and his loyalty
to the contents of his sources, is closer to ASvaghosa whereas Haribhat-
ta, with his smoother and more elegant language and his independent
adaptation of his sources, can be put in the same category with Kalidasa
and Candragomin. If this observation is accurate, it might give us a hint
about the chronological position of Haribhatta whose date is not known
with certainty.

In 1933, Giuseppe TUCCI reports the acquisition of a fragmentary manu-
script of Gopadatta’s collection of legends. In the colophon it is said that
the complete work consists of 3,300 granthas. Thus it is only slightly
shorter than Aryasira’s Jatakaméla. On the basis of these scarce facts, we
may venture to draw two conclusions: Gopadatta’s Jatakamala also
seems to be modelled on the work of Aryasiira and Haribhatta and
therefore seems to have consisted of 34 legends. Gopadatta seems to be
the latest of these three authors, closest to Ksemendra and Somendra
and more popular than his predecessors. This last assumption will be
corroborated by the style and setting of Gopadatta’s legends.

Tibetan scholar Lobsang DARGYAY published the Tibetan translation of
Gopadatta’s Saptakumarikavadina accompanied by a German rendering
and a study of the legend which he wrote for his his Ph.D. thesis. The

work, but unfortunately no other legend of Gopadatta’s Jatakamala can
be found there. The Sanskrit text of the Saptakumarikavadana, however,

b) The argument of homogeneous use of metres

Table VI presents a survey of the metres which are used in 6 of the 15
legends I have studied in detail. When comparing it with table III which
shows the metres used by Haribhatta (and to a certain extent also Arya-
Siira) we see that the deviation from these two authors is considerable.
While Aryaéiira seems to use almost the same metres as Haribhatta, the
metres of the six legends in question have a different character. The lack
of the Arya is conspicuous. The reason for this undoubtedly is the fact
that at that time it was regarded as out-dated. On the other hand, we
find a few metres of a more modern character, which in the early classi-
cal time were used rather sparingly, e.g., the ‘impure’ Vaméamala, a
mixture of VamSastha and Indravaméa (formed by analogy to the Upa-
jati metre) or the long Dandaka metres in Maitrakanyaka and Ajatasatru.
Furthermore, the frequent use of Mandakranta (lacking only in Sarvam-
dada) is striking as it is not used by Aryasira and Haribhatta. Thus, on
the one hand, metres of these six selected legends are markedly differ-
ent from the other two collections while, on the other hand, they form
a widely homogeneous group. This homogeneity is proven by the fact
that the metres of the two legends I studied last in preparing Table VI,
Svan and Sarvamdada, did not increase the number of metres when add-
ed to the data of the other four.

predecessors had done can be discarded in the following way: it was
particularly AryaStira and Haribhatta themselves who had shown that
it is possible to standardize the framework of a tale in different ways

assumption as it represents a combination of Aryagiira’s and Harib-

The most remarkable example are the 12 stanzas from the Suprabhasa le-
gend which reappear in the *Ajatasatrvavadana. On the basis of their
text, many of the imperfect and imcomplete portions of the latter tale
can easily be corrected and supplemented. The other parallels are clear

the more gifted poets preferred rather to quote themselves than to
invite the accusation of plagiarism. The practice of quoting from one’s
own works can be shown in the case of many poets, e.g., Aryasiira (i-

e) Agreement of composition of the legends

When we regard the overall structure of the individual legends and
compare it with that of Aryaéiira’s and Haribhatta’s tales we have to
admit a degeneration in terms of form and content. In some legends the
parts written in prose are reduced to such an extent as to become
negligible, e.g., in Saptakumarika and Svan; nothing is left of a meaning-
ful division of functions between verse and prose. When what is ex-
pressed by the prose is again repeated in the succeeding stanzas—as in
the case of Saptakumarika—the prose seems to be completely super-
fluous. These tales represent a clear transition to the more recent ones
written purely in verse by Ksemendra.

The plot is increasingly reduced to the advantage of descriptions, dialo-
gues and instructions, with the dramatic element or action becoming
more and more unimportant. The plot of a legend may often be describ-

Postscript

Only two years after my first comprehensive report about the extant

portion of the Jatakamdlas composed by Haribhatta and Gopadatta in
which I formulated my ‘Gopadatta hypothesis’ I was able to give inde-
pendent proof for the correctness of my ascription of certain anomyous
legends to an author called Gopadatta for at least one of the legends
mentioned in my list. 1 communicated my findings in a short note ("On
the Identification of Gopadatta’s Jatakas") in the Journal of the Nepal

Research Centre, Vol. 4 (Humanities) 1980, pp. 221-2, and for the sake of

convenience I reproduce here the full text of this note:

"In Sarvananda’s Tikisarvasva on Amarasimha’s Namaliriganusasana we
find the quotation of half a verse which is attributed to Gopadatta.
Commenting upon Amarakosa 2.6.66 (Pt. II p. 328 in the edition of Gana-
pati Shastri, The Namalingdnusdsana of Amarasimha, Pt. Il, Trivandrum
1915, Trivandrum Sanskrit Series XLII), Sarvananda writes:

dasika | ke cid ikanpratyayantam manyante | tatha ca
diisikapatalaniruddhalocananta
durgandha bahalamalopaliptagatra

iti Gopadattah |

This is the first half of a stanza the full text of which can be found as a
verse of the still unpublished Svajataka — and also as verse 16 of the
Somajataka of the Mahajjatakamala (chapter 8), which is nothing but a
metrical adaptation of the Svajataka, the reason for this somewhat mis-
leading title not being clear to me. The full text of the stanza runs as
follows:

diisikapatalaniruddhalocananta

durgandha bahalamalopaliptagatra |

anye tu klamasithilasraya rujanam

ajagmur nilayatam [sic !] prabandhininam | 8 1

The verse is part of a lengthy description of the deplorable condition in
which the people of Jambiidvipa were living at the time when the Bo-
dhisattva was Indra, the Lord of the Gods. Seeing this, Indra decides to
reincarnate in the body of a dog in order to relieve the people of Jambi-
dvipa of their sorrow.

A second piece of information which I would like to add here concerns
Gopadatta’s date. Gopadatta’s lower limit is given by Haribhatta (floruit

Anleihe Gopadattas bei Candragomin"). Gopadatta’s upper limit is giv-

done around 800 AD. Stanza 103 of the Prajfia@danda is nothing else but
a quotation of Gopadatta’s Matsaranandavadana, 60, (cf. Michael Hahn,
"Notes on Buddhist Sanskrit Literature. Chronology and related topics",
