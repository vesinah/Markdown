## 1. McDaniel 2002 — curricular canon + genre mentions (LOCAL MINE)

**Nissaya core definition** (lines 6–14, abstract): "Nissaya texts are idiosyncratic vernacular notes composed and used by Buddhist monks in Northern Thailand and Laos between the 16th and early 20th centuries."

**Expanded definition** (lines 600–613): "These nissaya (or 'support') texts quickly became a popular medium to express doctrinal teachings... Nissaya texts consist of Pali words or phrases followed by vernacular translations, usually with numerous commentarial additions by the translator." Also lines 638–643: "Nissayas reflect a stage of local Buddhist literature in which composing texts in Pali was being largely replaced by vernacular translations, commentaries, summaries, ritual instructions and narratives."

**Curricular canon thesis** (lines 145–153): "the texts that were translated (i.e. nissayas) seemed to have formed a practical vernacular canon which dominated language instruction... The nissayas may be the evidence of, what I call, multiple 'curricular canons.'" Conclusion (lines 2545–2550): "If commentaries define what is considered 'canon' by a particular community, then nissayas define a set of texts that were considered 'canonical' for the sake of Buddhist education. Nissayas are evidence of what we can call a type of curricular canon operating in pre-modern Northern Thailand and Laos."

**Canon-on-the-ground** (line 453): "Lao monks refer to nissayas as gampz tipidok (canonical scripture)." [= kham phi traiphok]

**Designation footnote 17 — the genre-term map** (lines 645–667): "In Thailand and Laos there are several designations for translations: nissaya, nissai, nisai sab, khan, nisrai, and wohiin [wohān]. I am as of yet unsure as to why authors choose to use one term over another or why some scribes use two or three titles for their manuscript. For example, the Hii Sip Jiit Nissaya found at Wat Sung Men is entitled: 'Wohiin Hii Sip Chiit Nisrai Hii Sip Chat.' While I surmise that these titles are synonymous... In the Critical Study of the Northern Thai Version of the Panyasa Jataka published by the Dept. of Thai at Chiang Mai University... they state that the only difference between nissayas and voharas (wohan) is the amount of Pali used in the text. This is oversimplified."

**Named nissaya manuscripts surveyed** (lines 766–784): Channaburi Dhammapada Nissaya Mak Ton(?), Chiang Mai Sutmon Nissaya, Chiang Mai Kit Nissaya, Chiang Mai Atthasālinī Nissaya mak bhaet, Lampang Kammavācā Nissaya, Lū'ang Pabāng Kammavaca/Uposatha Khan/Xai Noi/Saman Nissaya, Phrae Madhurasachambu/Ha Chat Nissaya, (Vientiane) Panchakanipāt/Malai Bodhisat/Phrakaeo Nissaya.

**Related genres flagged for future study**:
- desana/dessanā (lines 2761–2772): "one manuscript from Laos is titled a 'nisai-desana.' Still, the study of desana as a genre of manuscripts remains a desideratum."
- Footnote 55 (lines 2815–2825): "nisai-itipiso-desana (ms. 06 01 85 05 003 05) from the Luang Pabang Museum... composed in CS 1199 [AD 1837]... this could suggest that this is evidence of a transition of texts titled nissayas and texts titled desana since nissayas declined in production after the 1840's and desana increased."
- Footnote 20 (lines 852–858): "There are a few manuscripts from Northern Thailand and Laos which have the titles 'desananissai' or 'voharanaissai' [vohāra-nissai]. There was clearly a link between these two genres."
- Lines 1811–1814: "This [bilingual dictionary-like function] is the function usually associated with wohara or sab texts, but some nissayas provide this service, and indeed some nissayas are titled nisrai wohan or nisai sab in their colophons." (re Dibbamon Nissaya, Wat Mai Suvannabhūmiārāma, Luang Prabang)
- Lines 2583–2586: "There are other types of Pali and vernacular texts similar to the nissayas, like sabs and wohiins, that need to be looked at..."

**Nama Nissaya (= namasadda-type)** (lines 1041–1052): "I have not as yet been able to identify the source text... of the Nama Nissaya from Chiang Mai; however, its purpose is clearly to provide a Pali grammar lesson in Northern Thai... it is unrelated to the Nāmāpasamāsa or the Nāmacāradīpaka cited by Oskar von Hinüber." Lines 1053–1059: grammatical nissayas "are largely not found in Laos."

**Actual word-by-word sample** (Atthakathāmātikā Nissaya, Chiang Mai; lines 1497–1547): mixed Pali/Yuan glossing of *vipākādhammā* e.g. "vipiika iti mak wii ni adhivacana pen ki5 arupadhamma..." with English translation lines 1548ff. Demonstrates the trigger-word gloss technique.

## 2. Laulertvorakul 2003 — WHICH nine languages? format? significance (LOCAL MINE)

**The nine languages** (abstract, lines 16–28): "They are composed in 4 languages: Pali, Mon, Cambodian and Tai, including Tai vernaculars: Siamese Thai, Northern Thai, Northeastern Thai, Lao, Tai Lue, and Tai Khoeun."
→ **Nine = 1. Pāli, 2. Mon, 3. Cambodian (Khmer), 4. Siamese Thai, 5. Northern Thai (Thai Yuan), 6. Northeastern Thai, 7. Lao, 8. Tai Lue, 9. Tai Khoeun.**
Cross-check via abbreviation table (lines 110–201): CBD, MON, NET, PLN, SMT, TKN, TL(TLC/TLE), TY(TYC/TYE), LAO, LVV, LPL, LPRa/b/c.

**Format** (lines 36–41): "The styles of composition dramatically vary between detailed enumeration and concision, prose and verse, translation in the style of nissaya and non-nissaya, single language and dual languages – Pali and another language." The Pali-Lao version IS a nissaya: PLN = "Pali-Lao nissaya version of Pathamasambodhi" (lines 171–172).

**§8.a The Pali-Lao Nissaya (PLN)** (lines 792–841): reported by Finot (1917), Lafont (1959), Thao Kene (1958), NCRI (1988), LPMP (1995–98); "the manuscripts consisting of 10 bundles are Pali-Lao Nissaya. The oldest manuscript is the one written in CS 1188 [AD 1826] and is kept in the Museum at Luang Prabang." Appendix list (lines 2506–2514): PLN at Wat Mai Suvannabhumaram, Luang Prabang, 10 bundles (complete), CS 1195. Lao Verse Version (LVV): found BE 2411 in Lao poetry form at a Lao monastery in Brisbane, Australia; transliterated/published 1999 by Prah Maha Jana Dhammadhajo (lines 842–851).

**Significance for multilingual gloss tradition**: (lines 49–56, 77–108) "The Pali version found in the Lanna region is the oldest complete version... which not only is rendered to Tai Lue, Tai Khoeun, Lao and Northern Thai, but evolved over centuries to become the three newer Pali recensions which later were rendered to northeastern Thai, Mon and Siamese Thai. The latter version was finally rendered to Cambodian." Content grew in three phases (legend of Bodhisatta → legend of Gotama → sāsana history under Prince Paramanujitjinorot); "The Northern Thai version is the transition link between the first and the second phase." → Shows the nissaya-style dual-language gloss technique operating across the entire Mekong/Tai sphere in ≥9 languages.

## 4. Precise definition of NAMASADDA genre

- **New Mandala review of Gathering Leaves** (Peter A. Jackson review, https://www.newmandala.org/book-review/review-of-mcdaniel/, 2010-02-07): "Indeed, namasadda, which are filled with marginalia, corrections, and emendations, literally means word-book or glossary." And: "McDaniel's logocentric methodology suits the three genres of texts used to teach Buddhism: nissaya, namasadda, and vohara."
- **Pannabhoga 2023** (Brill/DOI above): "Three variations of literary genres: nissaya ('support'), nāmasadda ('glossary'), and vohāra ('lifting words') are popularly used in Northern Thailand and Laos as pedagogical texts while students and teachers conduct 'languaging'." Plus: "the nissaya and nāmasadda manuscripts are characterized by close, word-for-word glosses" (attributed to McDaniel 2008, 131).
- **Etymology**: von Hinüber, as quoted in Pannabhoga 2023: "Hinüber also (1997, 100–1) states that nissayas in Thailand can be considered as vohāra or nāmasap (from Sanskrit nāma-śabda 'noun-word') as a word-for-word explanation and that the difference between vohāra and nissaya is not clear." [Full bibliographic identity of von Hinüber 1997: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน — cited only via Pannabhoga]
- **Thai Digital Monastery** (UPenn-hosted, http://tdm.sas.upenn.edu/monastery/Buddhist_texts.html, drawing on then-forthcoming McDaniel 2008): lists "Various Namasadda (Glossaries)" among monastic-library holdings; example title "Kammavaacaa Nissaya Naamasadda... explains the meaning of the Pali chanted at one of the most common of Buddhist rituals – the ordination... still employed by monks as a guide to giving a sermon on the importance of ordination."
- **McDaniel JIABS** ("The Art of Reading and Teaching Dhammapadas", *JIABS*, Heidelberg OA): "Usually they are vernacular glosses (nissaya, vohara, or namasadda) of some stories from the atthakatha and other local narratives", fn14: "Voharas and namasaddas are closely related to nissayas in Laos and Thailand. I describe the differences extensively in McDaniel (2003)."

**Working definition**: nāmasadda (< Skt nāma-śabda, "noun-word") = a word-book/glossary-type pedagogical manuscript giving close word-for-word Pali-to-vernacular glosses, heavily annotated in use; overlaps with nissaya; exemplified by Kammavācā Nissaya Nāmasadda and the Chiang Mai "Nama Nissaya".

## 5. Precise definition of VOHARA / VOHĀN genre

- **New Mandala review**: "Vohara lift words and phrases from seemingly ancient texts and contextualise them in a sermon delivered in the vernacular."
- **Pannabhoga 2023** (quoting McDaniel 2008, 131): "the vohāra cite longer passages of twelve to twenty-five words from Pali source texts and offer extensive and creative vernacular translations and commentaries. Generally, nissaya fall between the two genres and often cite four to ten words before offering glosses and creative asides." → **quantitative genre gradient: namasadda (word-for-word) < nissaya (4–10 words) < vohāra (12–25 words)**.
- **McDaniel 2002 local fn17** (lines 664–666): CMU Panyasa Jataka study claims "the only difference between nissayas and voharas (wohan) is the amount of Pali used in the text. This is oversimplified." — i.e. McDaniel himself rejects a purely quantitative boundary.
- **Orthographic variants** (McDaniel 2002): wohān / wohara / vohan / vohāra; colophon forms "nisrai wohan", "nisai sab", "Wohān Hū Sip Chāt Nisrai Hū Sip Chat" (lines 647–653, 1813–1814).
- Thai-language gloss (Medium summary of Gathering Leaves by Kridpuj D, 2026-03-11, https://medium.com/@kridpuj.d/gathering-leaves-and-lifting-words-...): โวหาร (Vohara) เน้นสำนวน/ยกวลีจากต้นฉบับมาขยายความ; นามสัททะ เน้นพจนานุกรมศัพท์ [secondary summary].
