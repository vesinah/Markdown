## 1. Blackburn, "Sūtra Sannayas and Saraṇaṃkara" (SLJH 23, 1997) — FULL TEXT MINED

**Provenance:** Downloaded full PDF from Peradeniya IR handle https://ir.lib.pdn.ac.lk/handle/20.500.14444/3801 (bitstream: .../bitstreams/0a503da3-753d-432d-8cd4-035e5c71f584/download). Metadata confirms: Blackburn, Anne M., 1997, publisher University of Peradeniya. Text extraction saved locally.

### Key verbatim extracts (from extracted text, page numbers = journal pages)

**Definition/format (pp. 77–78):**
> "A sutra sannaya is a type of commentary, distinguished by the type of text on which it comments and by the way in which it comments… satra sannayas are commentaries written on Buddhist suttas… In principle, a satra sannaya could be written for any sutta found in the Pali tipitaka. In fact, the manuscript evidence shows that sutra sannayas were written for a much smaller number of satras… the paritta (or piriti) suttas -- including Metta Sutta, Mangala Sutta, Karaniyametta Sutta, and Dhajagga Sutta, for instance -- were among these favourites."

> "A sannaya is an explanation or exposition (vyakhyava) which may be an elucidation of meaning (arthavivaranaya) or an exposition of detail (vistara kathanaya)… roughly parallels that between padavannana and atthavannana in the Pali commentarial traditions."

> "sutra sannayas typically introduce phrases, and sometimes longer sentences and even short narratives, to elaborate the Pali word or words in question." … "A sutra sannaya is best understood as an intermediate form between the narrowest and broadest Sinhala exegetical styles." (arthavyakhyana vs dharmavyakhyanas distinction, p. 78)

**History of genre (p. 78):**
> "Sutra sannayas appeared as early as the twelfth century but were, in Somadasa's words, only 'a minor literary genre prior to the eighteenth century' (1987, x). The production of satra sannaya texts began in the twelfth century and ceased in the fifteenth for reasons which remain unclear. It is likely that the early satra sannayas drew on earlier (5th-7th century) translations of Pali suttas into Sinhala but this cannot be confirmed as none of these earlier translations are extant." [fn: "In this regard see Godakumbura (1955, p. 23)."]

> Sri Dharmakirti "analyzes the prominence of sannayas in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries in terms of linguistic change within Sinhala culture, arguing that works written in the Sinhala language of the earlier Anuradhapura Period were no longer accessible to later readers of Sinhala and that new commentarial works were necessary to mediate between Pali and Sinhala (1961, 136)."

**Saraṇaṃkara / first new sutra-sannaya (p. 79):**
> "the first satra sannaya--called Sararthadlpani [Sāratthadīpanī] (or, Illuminator of Excellent Meaning)--written since the fifteenth century was written sometime between 1739 and 1747 by a novice monk named Velivita Saranamkara living in the Kandyan Kingdom."

> "Sararthadipani, the first of the new generation of surra sannayas, written between 1739 and 1747, draws on the fifth-century Pali atthakatha tradition for paritta suttas, either directly or as mediated through thirteenth-century works." (p. 81)

**Purpose / educational system (pp. 82–85):**
> Four-stage emergence: (1) temple schools influenced by the Silvat Samagarna, (2) Niyamakanda educational centre before 1753, (3) up-country centres after 1753, (4) extension to Sat Korale/southern temples under Dutch control. Education under Saraṇaṃkara had four class divisions; "Satra sannayas were appropriate for use in the third and fourth class."

> "satra sannayas were a natural pedagogical tool in this context, since these commentaries provided a copy of Pali suttas, explained the narrative and grammatical contents of a Pali sutta, and suggested patterns of exposition suitable for preaching. Even in the absence of a teacher, a student with a knowledge of the alphabet and a limited understanding of Pali language could work alone." (p. 84)

> "The use of satra sannayas in this way helped to standardize the educational experience of monastic students over a relatively large geographical area." (pp. 84–85)

**Not preaching manuals directly (pp. 86–87):**
> "these commentaries were almost certainly not used directly as a text from which to preach… The size of the script used in writing satra sannayas is consistently small enough to make them awkward as a preaching aid… Satra sannayas were written, read and copied as a guide to the comprehension of Pali suttas."

**Textual community concept (p. 96):**
> "I draw on the concept of a 'textual community' developed by Brian Stock… I adapt the term 'textual community' to mean a group of individuals united by their exposure, through reading and listening, to certain ideas and patterns of discourse which draw upon and are sustained by written textual sources."

> "Because the commentarial detail of satra sannayas fed into the act of Buddhist preaching, these commentaries played a pivotal role in the creation of a wider textual community of lay people and monastics…"

**MSS cited:** British Library OR 6600 (151) and Colombo Museum Library 1465 copies of Sāratthadīpanī "virtually identical to the 1891 printed edition" (ed. Beligalle Sobhita, Satya Samuccaya Press, 1891) — p. 85 n12.

**Blackburn bibliography nuggets:** Somadasa, *Catalogue of the Hugh Nevill Collection of Sinhalese Manuscripts* Vol. I (London: British Library and Pali Text Society, 1987); Sannasgala, *Simhala Sahitya Vansaya* (1964); Hevavasam (1966); Vacissara (1964); Stock, *The Implications of Literacy* (1983).

## 2. DAG (Dhampiyā-Aṭuvä-Gäṭapadaya) — VERDICT

**What it is:** a gäṭapada-type **difficult-word glossary** (gaṇṭhipada) to the Pali **Dhammapada-aṭṭhakathā**, NOT a complete interlinear translation. Compiled under King Kassapa V (traditionally 913–923 CE; some sources 898–914/914–923). Oldest datable extensive Sinhala prose (with the Mihintale tablets often named as older inscriptional specimen; one source ranks Siyabaslakara first among prose works).

Quotes with provenance:

> "The oldest extant Sinhala prose work we have dates back to the ninth century. This is the Dhampiya-Atuva-Getapadaya, a glossary to the Dhammapadatthakatha (a Buddhist story book in Pali) compiled by King Kassapa V (913-923 A.C.)."
— Virtual Library Sri Lanka ("Sinhalese prose", hettiarachchi.tripod.com/slpose.htm)

> "One such scholar was the author of Dhampiya-atuva-gatapadaya, a work written in archaic Sinhala of the tenth century. According to tradition he was a king of Sri Lanka, Kassapa V (914-923). This is in fact another glossary on terms and usages of the Pali translation of the Dhammapada commentary. That the author consulted the old Sinhala commentary in writing this work is evident from the fact that at several places he quotes an occasional phrase from it."
— Introduction to Carter & Palihawadana, *Dhammapada* (OUP), preview PDF: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780198021193_A35484484/preview-9780198021193_A35484484.pdf

> "The greatest find was a copy of the Sinhalese gloss on the Dhammapada, which, excepting the Mihintale Inscription, is the oldest known specimen of Sinhalese prose."
— Nyanatusita (comp.), *Pali Literature of Asia* (BPS PDF bp628h), recounting James De Zoysa's temple-library cataloguing mission. https://www.bps.lk/olib/bp/bp628h_Pali-Literature-of-Asia.pdf

> "It is evident that many of the early Sinhala prose works were intended as accessories for comprehensive Pali works."
— Virtual Library Sri Lanka (same page), re DAG and Jataka-Atuva-Getapadaya.

**Genre clarification (gäṭapada vs saṇṇaya):**
> "A gaetapadaya only deals with portions of a text while a sannaya or padārtha deals with the whole texts; see Vs 75f." … "Sometimes, possibly through confusion, a gaetapadaya is called a sannaya."
— Nyanatusita, Reference Table of Pali Literature (local PDF, notes vi–vii; gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil_elib/Nyt008__Nyanatusita_ReferenceTableOfPaliLiterature.pdf)

**Editions/scholarship found:**
- D. E. Hettiarachchi (ed.), *Dhampiya Atuwa Getapadaya*, University of Ceylon Press (Lanka Vishvavidyalaye Prakashana Mandalaya) — confirmed in University of Peradeniya Sinhala Dept PG Diploma syllabus PDF (arts.pdn.ac.lk/sinhala/ac/post/Diploma.pdf); modern Samayawardhana reprint, 350 pp, ISBN 978955579620, "Language: Pali, Sinhala" (emars.lk/dhampiya-atuwa-gatapadhaya-aa0105.html).
- Same syllabus lists: *Jathaka atuwa getapadaya*, eds M. Rammandala & D. E. Hettiarachchi, Lanka Vishvavidyalaye Prakashana Mandalaya, 1960.
- **W.S. Karunatillake DAG edition: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน** (no evidence found that Karunatillake edited DAG; his verified works are listed in §8).
- Dedicated modern critical studies of DAG beyond editions: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน (no standalone English monograph located in this loop; Godakumbura 1955 ch. on prose remains standard reference — archive.org copy is lending-restricted, identifier sinhaleseliterat0000cego, not downloadable).

## 3. Vesaturu-dā-saṇṇe (Vessantara Jātaka saṇṇa)

**Nature:** Sinhala saṇṇa (paraphrase) of the Pali Vessantara Jātaka verses/commentary; colophon: "Vessantara-Jataka-Attha-Vannana nitthita".

**Provenance & quotes:** Sinhala Wikibooks page "වෙසතුරු දා සන්නෙ - INTRODUCTION" (introduction to the printed edition):
> "Being a Sanne or a Sinhalese Paraphrase of the Pali Vessantara Jataka, the present work can justly be called a Vessantara jataka sane or in Elu, Vesture da – sanne."

Dating (same source):
> "this work, whilst containing a stratum of old language which may date back to the early polonnaruva or even the Anuradhapura times, is less Sanskritic than the Abhidharmaratha-Sangraha-Sannaya, Jataka-Atuva-Gatapadaya and other similar works belonging to the polonnaruva period. There is every possibility of the present work being older than the Jataka-Atuva-Gatapadaya. Hence the Vesture-Da-Sannaya may be dated somewhere about the 12the century A.D."

> Authorship unknown: "I have not come across in any Manuscript I have seen, any mention of the authorship of the Vesture-Da-Sanne."

Context (same source): "A few of the old Sanna extant now belong to the Polonnaruva period (12th century) but most of them seem to have been written during the Dambadeniya times (13th century) or thereabouts. Even after the 13th century quite a large number of Sanna were produced, and they are being written even today." Also lists dozens of printed 1890s–1920s sutra-sanne editions (Aggikkhandhopama, Angulimala, Atanatiya, Dhajagga, Dhammacakkappavattana, Mahamangala, Mahasamaya, Ratthapala etc.) with presses/dates — direct evidence for §7 continuity.

**Edition:** Vs = *Vesaturu-dā-sannī*, ed. D.E. Hettiarachchi, Colombo, 1950 (abbreviation key in Nyanatusita Reference Table, local text L341). Nyanatusita note vii directs to "the introduction to the edition of the Vesataru-dā-sannī (Vs)" for detailed information on sannayas.

Verdict: date/nature confirmed — verse-jātaka paraphrase saṇṇa, anonymous, ~12th c. proposed (possibly older strata), certainly pre-13th-c.; scholarly consensus places it among the earliest extant saṇṇas.

## 4. Mahabodhi(-vamsa)-Gäṭapadaya

> "Another early work, the Mahabodhi Getapadaya composed about the 12th century by an anonymous author is a glossary to aid students master the Mahabodhivansa, a history of the sacred Bodhi tree in Pali."
— Virtual Library Sri Lanka (hettiarachchi.tripod.com/slpose.htm)

Base text: Mahābodhivaṃsa (Bodhivaṃsa), Sanskritized Pali prose poem by Upatissa, reign of Mahinda IV (~10th c./~AD 980) — Wikipedia-derived IPFS mirror "Bodhi Vamsa"; WisdomLib (DPPN): "A Pali translation of a Singhalese original… written about the tenth century and is ascribed to Upatissa"; Saraṅkara later wrote the Madhurathaprakāśinī paraphrase on it.

Nyanatusita (local text L425-426) documents the library convention: "In Sri Lankan monastery-library indexes the titles… which have a sannaya as supplement the word sannaya is abbreviated to -aya, e.g., Mahā-bodhivamsa saha sannaya could be abbreviated to Mahā-bodhivamsaya." — confirming Mahabodhi-text+saṇṇa combos are standard manuscript units.

Verdict: Mahabodhi-gäṭapadaya = anonymous ~12th-c. glossary (gäṭapada) on the 10th-c. Pali Mahābodhivaṃsa. Confirmed.

## 5. The chain: lost Sinhala aṭṭhakathā → Buddhaghosa's Pali commentaries → later Sinhala saṇṇas reglossing

VERIFIED in multiple independent scholarly sources:

(a) Carter & Palihawadana, *Dhammapada* intro (OUP preview, URL in §2):
> "When the Buddhist tradition was introduced into Sri Lanka in the third century B.C., the monks who brought the tradition from India are reported to have brought with them… the comments… So adapted, the commentarial material came to be known as the Sinhala Commentary or Sihalatthakatha. In the fifth century A.D., there started a movement to translate this Sinhala commentative material… back into the Pali language. The prime mover… was an Indian Buddhist monk named Buddhaghosa…" … "With the translation of the old commentaries into Pali, the original Sinhala works went into comparative disuse but were available in Sri Lankan temples for several centuries to come."

(b) Same source completes the reglossing chain: DAG author (Kassapa V) consulted the old Sīhala-aṭṭhakathā; then "a monk by the name of Dhammasena wrote… Saddharma Ratnavaliya" (13th c. free adaptation); "another two centuries" later the anonymous *Dhammapada Purana Sannaya* (14th c.) "gives a word-for-word Sinhala translation of the Pali verses."

(c) Buddhaghosuppatti/prelude tradition (palikanon.com DPPN entry on Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā): "it is stated that he translated the work from Sinhalese at the request of a monk named Kumarakassapa."

(d) Nyanatusita Reference Table intro (local text L132-134): sannayas "can be quite old… and therefore can be very valuable because of giving variant readings, providing quotations from the old Sihala-atthakatha (Sinh. Helatuva) and other lost Pali texts and sannayas."

(e) Blackburn 1997 (p. 78 + fn): early sutra-sannayas likely drew on lost 5th–7th-c. Sinhala sutta translations; cites Godakumbura 1955: 23.

(f) BuddhaNet "Sacred Island" (buddhanet.net/sacred-island/introduction/): "By the 5th century CE the Sinhala [commentaries]… was becoming so archaic that it was increasingly difficult to read and thus it was decided to render them all into Pali… Buddhaghosa, the most erudite scholar of his time, was invited from India to perform this task."

(g) Journal of Religious History 2009 article (Dhammapada Traditions and Translations, buddhism.lib.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-MAG/mag204413.pdf): "Around the time when Buddhaghosa was translating a number of Sinhalese texts into Pali, around 450 CE, some of the existing Sinhalese traditions of stories and commentaries on the Dhammapada were translated back into Pali… By the thirteenth century, the Pali commentaries were again being translated back into Sinhala…" and lists ≥10 Dhammapada-related Sinhala works surviving in ms (Dhammapada Sannaya, Purana Sannaya, Vyakhya-va, Varnana, Atuva-va…).

CONCLUSION: The chain narrative is well attested; nuance = the "lost" Sīhala-aṭṭhakathā survives only in quotation (esp. via gäṭapadas/saṇṇas like DAG), and reglossing happened in waves (10th c. gäṭapada; 12th–13th c. saṇṇa boom; 18th c. sutra-sannaya revival under Saraṇaṃkara).

## 9. Direct saṇṇa ↔ nissaya comparison statements — FOUND (strongest chaining lead)

(a) Pannabhoga Herngseng, "Burmese Nissaya Literature: A Mainstream Monastic Education Learning Method and its Salient Features," *MANUSYA: Journal of Humanities* 26.1 (2023): 1–23, Brill, DOI 10.1163/26659077-25010027 (also academia.edu/101533625):
> "According to McDaniel (2008: 119), a similar genre of Pali literature also developed within Theravada Buddhist countries, i.e., in Sri Lanka, as *sannaya*, and in Thailand and Laos as *nissaya*, *namasadda*, and *vohara*… The Sutranipata-sannaya, a Pali-Sinhala word-to-word translation written in the 12th ce, is the earliest example of a nissaya, or sannaya literary genre found in Sri Lanka."

> "Tin Lwin (1961: 5–11) claims that the oldest extant nissaya in Burma is the Khuddakasikkha Nikaya nissaya, written by Mahakassapa during the reign of Narapatisithu (1174–1211)… he considers that they originate as Pyu nissaya (Sriksetra), Mon nissaya (Pagan), Sinhalese nissaya (*sannaya*) and then Burmese nissaya."

> Pruitt (1992: 278): "Sinhalese Sanne and Burmese Nissayas have been important in Pali studies in the West." (quoted inside same article)

(b) Nyanatusita Reference Table (notes vii–viii, local text L402–431): parallel classification table — "Gaṇṭhipada, gätapada (vi) (Sri Lanka, Indochina)… Sannaya (vii) (Sri Lanka)… Nissaya (viii) (Indochina)":
> "A nissaya is a word by word translation or paraphrase from Pali into one of the vernaculars of Indochina such as Burmese. It is comparable to the Sinhalese sannaya genre."

(c) Okell, John. 1965. "Nissaya Burmese: A case of systematic adaptation to a foreign grammar and syntax." *Lingua* 15: 186–227 (cited in Herngseng 2023) — linguistic counterpart study for Burmese side.

(d) Braun, Erik. 2013. *The Birth of Insight* (Chicago): nissaya defined as Burmese word-by-word translation/explanation of Pali (cited pp. 67, 174 in Herngseng).

(e) Bonus comparative datum (wrdingham.co.uk palistudy list, Lance Cousins et al.): the Burmese Abhidhammatthasangaha-mahatika "is to the largest part a quite literal translation of the Sinhalese Sannaya by Sariputta" — cross-tradition dependence of Burmese exegesis on a Sinhala saṇṇa.

Verdict: yes — explicit comparisons exist (McDaniel 2008; Pruitt 1992; Tin Lwin 1961; Nyanatusita 2008; Herngseng 2023), though no dedicated monograph-length comparative study of saṇṇa vs nissaya was found → research gap usable for the Thai project.
