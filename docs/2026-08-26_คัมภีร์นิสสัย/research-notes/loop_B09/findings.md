## Sub-topic 1 — LOCAL Whitman et al. 2010: definitions of kunten/kundoku/kugyeol and how vernacular markers work formally

Source: `Whitman_et_al_2010_International_Vocabulary_Vernacular_Readings_SCRIPTA.pdf` (SCRIPTA 2: 61–83, Hunmin jeongeum Society).

**Framing claim (p. 61–62):**
> "The practice of reading classical Chinese texts in the non-Sinitic vernacular (漢文訓讀 hanwen xundu; Korean hanmun hundok, Japanese kanbun kundoku) ... is a linguistic habitus found in all parts of the Sinosphere, implemented in the local language in all countries which have been touched by the culture of sinographic writing."

> "As an object of intellectual inquiry, vernacular reading of Chinese texts is no longer the exclusive property of one country or region; it is common, international property." (p. 63)

**Method:** 150 basic terms → 30 → core 24/27 terms, Japanese as matrix language; translations into English, Italian, Korean. Sources: Ishizuka Harumichi's Kuntengo jiten 訓点語辞典 (2001) etc.

**Key term definitions extracted (Table 2 + discussion):**
- 訓点 kunten/hunchŏm = **gloss** ("guiding marks for rendering Chinese into Japanese" per Kenkyūsha dictionary; Ishizuka's alternative = "reading mark", reserved by Whitman et al. for Dunhuang annotations, while "gloss" = annotation to be read *in another language*).
- 訓読 kundoku / 漢文訓読 hanwen xundu = **vernacular reading** / vernacular reading of Chinese texts; 音読 ondoku = Sinoxenic reading.
- 訓点資料 kunten shiryō = glossed material; 加点 katen/kajŏm = glossing/add gloss.
- 返点 kaeriten / 逆讀點 yŏktok-chŏm = **inversion gloss** (Korean tradition: yŏktok 逆讀 "inverted reading"); 返読 hendoku/pandok = inverted reading.
- ヲコト点 wokototen = **morphosyntactic gloss** (Korean: 點吐 chŏmt'o "point glosses"); 仮名点 kanaten / 字吐 cha-t'o = phonogram gloss.
- 角筆 kakuhitsu/kakp'il = **stylus**; stylus gloss = dry-point glossing (scratching paper surface, no ink). "In cases such as this, we have opted for the term already established in Western philology for the corresponding concept."
- 不読 fudoku/pudok = unread character; 再読 saidoku = iterated reading; 科段 kadan = paragraph mark; 句読 kutō = punctuation; 破音 haon = poyin marks; 声点 shōten = tone gloss; 朱点 shuten / 白点 hakuten = vermillion/white ink glosses; 奥書 okugaki = colophon (Korean: chiŏ 識語); 釈文 shakumon = re-transcription of glossed text; 訓読文 kundokubun = text interpreted in the vernacular; 漢字文化圏 = Sinosphere.

**Formal mechanics described:**
- Kunten system works by adding marks around characters that instruct word-order inversion (kaeriten numbers), grammatical particles/endings absent from Chinese (wokototen dots at fixed positions, e.g. dot upper-right = accusative *o*, right side = nominalizer *koto*), phonetic readings (katakana/furigana), tone marks, iteration and non-reading instructions.
- European parallel invoked at length: Würzburg glosses (Old Irish interlinear/marginal glosses on Latin Pauline letters, 8th c.), Reichenau glosses, Vespasian Psalter (Old English), Turin & Karlsruhe glosses, Glossa ordinaria; Bologna legal glossators (Irnerius) for marginal-gloss origins; dry-point/scratched glosses (Meritt 1934/1961; Rusche 1994) as exact functional counterpart of kakuhitsu.
- Terminological asymmetry noted: Japanese kunten ≠ Korean kugyŏl terminology one-to-one (e.g., Japanese kunten corresponds functionally to Korean t'o 吐; okugaki has no kugyŏl counterpart because most kugyŏl texts lack gloss-dated colophons).

**Chaining refs inside the PDF:** Kobayashi Yoshinori 2007 "Tracing the Spread of Kakuhitsu Glossing of Chinese Texts in East Asia" (AAS panel); Nam Pung-hyun 1999; Chang Kyungjun 2007 (瑜伽師地論 點吐釋讀口訣); Lee Seungjae et al. 2005–2009 (角筆口訣의 解讀과 飜譯 1–5); Ishizuka 1993 "The Origins of the Ssŭ-shêng Marks" (Acta Asiatica 65).

## Sub-topic 2 — LOCAL Lee Yong 2011: gugyeol typology and Buddhist sutra basis

Source: `Lee2011_Kugyol_Research_ALA.pdf` (Acta Linguistica Asiatica 1.1: 53–70, DOI 10.4312/ala.1.1.53-70; also live at https://journals.uni-lj.si/ala/article/view/16).

**Definition:** kugyol = "a convention for adding Korean grammatical markers at appropriate places to aid the reading of originally Chinese texts"; devices "characterized by additions of phonetic, semantic and syntactic markers made by brush and ink, as well as holes and scratches made by a stylus on paper."

**Typology (three stages):**
1. **Eumdok kugyol** 音讀口訣 (transliteration kugyol; 13th-c. Goryeo onward): kugyol letters placed at end of each phrase/sentence; functions = case markers + sentence-ending markers (Analects example: 㬔 = 'if/myen', 㫅 = interrogative 'a'). Letters formed by abbreviating Chinese characters, "closely resembles the formation of kana in Japanese."
2. **Seokdok kugyol** 釋讀口訣 (translation kugyol; older; discovered 1973 via Kuyeok-inwanggyeong): letters on BOTH right and left of columns; includes unpronounced purely syntactic markers meaning "do read / skip / return and read the skipped part now"; markers point out meaning AND instruct Korean phonetic reading; modality/aspect markers added. Reading rules: right-side read first; left-side skipped in first pass; reversal mark 㫬 = "go back from this point to the kugyol character on the left side" → produces SOV Korean reordering of SVO Chinese (worked examples given).
3. **Jeomto seokdok-kugyol** 點吐釋讀口訣: "point marks to translate into Korean... actually holes and scratches on paper"; each jeomto corresponds to one kugyol letter or group (Chang 2002); made with stylus, found via scopes; first discovered 2000 by Kobayashi in Yugasajiron vols 5, 8; ~15 additional stylus-marked texts identified; Chang's (2002) "point map" plots every possible dot position against syntactic/morphological categories.

**Buddhist sutra basis:** five key seokdok-kugyol xylographs, all Buddhist: Taebanggwangbul-hwaeomkyeongso vol. 35 (12th c.); Hwaeomkyeong vol. 14 (late 12th–early 13th c.); Keumgwangmyeonggyeong 金光明經 = Suvarṇaprabhāsa-sūtra vol. 3 (13th c.; contains BOTH kugyol letters + jeomto = double marking); Kuyeok-inwanggyeong vol. 1 (13th c.); Yugasajiron 瑜伽師地論 = Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra vol. 20 (latest linguistic features).
**Open question:** Hwaeommuneuiyogyeol (華嚴文義要訣, early 8th c., monk P'yonwon) bears brush-made points readable in Korean (per Kobayashi) but syntax-compatible with Japanese too — Korea/Japan attribution unresolved. Tōdaiji Fujumonkō (796–830) was written on its reverse.
