## Sub-topic 1: Japanese kundoku (訓読) — scholarly descriptions

**Core definition.** Wikipedia "Kanbun" (fetched 2026-08-26): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanbun
- Kanbun = system for writing Literary Chinese used in Japan from Nara period to 20th c.; "kanbun-kundoku" = "a Japanese reading of a Chinese passage."
- Terminology of the glossing apparatus: **kunten 訓点** ("guiding marks for rendering Chinese into Japanese"), **kundoku 訓読**, **okototen 乎古止点** ("diacritical dots... to indicate Japanese grammatical inflections"), **kaeriten 返り点** ("marks placed alongside characters indicating their Japanese ordering is to be read in reverse"), kutōten 句読点.
- Quote: "In the Japanese kanbun reading tradition, the Chinese text is transformed through punctuation, analysis, and translation into classical Japanese." Also notes Roy Andrew Miller: only *kanbun* among Sino-Xenic traditions (Korean hanmun, Vietnamese Hán Văn) has survived to the present.

## Sub-topic 2: Kanbun pedagogy & comparison to other interlinear traditions

- Whitman, Oh, Park, Alberizzi, Tsukimoto, Kosukegawa & Takada, "Toward an International Vocabulary for Research on Vernacular Readings of Chinese Texts (漢文訓讀 Hanwen Xundu)," *SCRIPTA* 2 (2010): 63–83 — **PDF downloaded to documents\**. URL: https://conf.ling.cornell.edu/whitman/WhitmanAlberizziTsukimotoKosukegawa2010Toward.pdf
  - Quote: "The practice of reading classical Chinese texts in the vernacular... is not restricted to Korea and Japan. It is a practice found in all parts of the Sinosphere, beginning with China itself..."
  - Explicit European comparison: "just as Japanese kunten and Korean kugyŏl materials are among the earliest sources for these languages [i.e., as] in Europe... monks added glosses to Latin texts, mainly the Bible and biblical commentaries, to assist their own readings." Proposes English "gloss" for kunten/hunchŏm and "reading mark" for Dunhuang-style annotations; discusses kakuhitsu 角筆 stylus glosses.
  - Cites Kobayashi Yoshinori 2007 "Tracing the Spread of Kakuhitsu Glossing of Chinese Texts in East Asia" (AAS paper).
- Alberizzi, "An Introduction to Kunten Glossed Texts and Their Study in Japan," *Dossiers d'Histoire Épistémologie Langage* 7 (2014) (cited via Bundschuh 2020, Osaka repository PDF: https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/75561/atcs_003_043.pdf).

- Kinsui Satoshi 2002, "The Influence of Translation upon the Historical Development of the Japanese Language" (UCLA paper, cited in Whitman et al.) — kundoku's effect on Japanese expository registers.

## Sub-topic 3: Tibetan tshig don (word-meaning) commentary & chen mo format

- **Wayman, Alex. 1984. "The Interlineary-Type Commentary in Tibetan."** In Louis Ligeti (ed.), *Tibetan and Buddhist Studies Commemorating the 200th Anniversary of the Birth of Alexander Csoma de Körös*, Bibliotheca Orientalis Hungarica 29.1–2, Budapest: 367–379. (Citation verified via Solmsdorf 2018 bibliography and Classical Tibetan Wiki.) — THE direct scholarly statement that Tibetan mchan-type annotation constitutes an interlineary commentary genre.
- **Solmsdorf, Nikolai. 2018. "Scribble and Notes: Remarks on the Tibetan Marginalia / Interlinear Notes (mchan bu)."** In *Saddharmāmṛtam* (Festschrift Jens-Uwe Hartmann), WSTB 93, Wien: 477–488+. Abstract & AI-key-takeaways at Academia.edu: https://www.academia.edu/37077247/
  - Quote (abstract): "authors and scribes used the margins of the page or in between the lines to add marginalia or interlinear notes, the so-called mchan bu (or mchan)."
  - Categories identified: glosses, scholia, interlinear corrections, interpolations; ~150 works catalogued under 'mchan' in TBRC/Buddhist Digital Resource Center (as of Jan 2017); notes extensive *mchan 'grel* commentaries by the Fifth Dalai Lama and Tsongkhapa.
- **Classical Tibetan Wiki vocabulary note** (https://wiki.learntibetanlanguage.org/en/Wilson-Resources/tibetan-vocabulary-notes): entry *mchan bu* = "interlinear note, insert [correction in the text], footnote"; entry *mchan 'grel* cites Wayman 1984 AND reports: "According to Matthew Kapstein, this type of commentary, made through 'footnoting' of the complete text, **is not of Indian origin**." — i.e., an East-African-of-the-Tibetan-plateau indigenous development analogous to nissaya.
- **Wilson, Joe B. 1996. "Tibetan Commentaries on Indian Śāstras."** In Cabezón & Jackson (eds.), *Tibetan Literature: Studies in Genre*, 125–137 (cited in Solmsdorf bibliography).
- Genre labels verified via Tsadra/Rangjung Yeshe dictionaries: *'grel bshad* = "detailed explanation/commentary" (https://rywiki.tsadra.org/index.php/%27grel_bshad); Buddhistdoor/Hopkins dictionary: "'grel bshad ... explanatory commentary; subcommentary." Structure of standard rnam bshad/don 'grel described at Tsadra bodhicitta wiki (Spyod 'jug rnam bshad): "(1) a topical outline [sa bcad], (2) full verses related to the topic, and (3) complete explanations of the verses."
- **tshig don titles as word-for-word commentaries**: BDRC archive.org items titled "tshig don" described as "Word-for-word commentary..." e.g., Longchen Nyingtig confessional text (https://archive.org/details/bdrc-W23231) and Hevajra Tantra word-to-word commentary listing (namsebangdzo.com). Dölpopa's famous Uttaratantra *tshig don* commentary (Rin chen sgron me) listed at buddhanature.tsadra.org.
- Dreyfus, *The Sound of Two Hands Clapping* (2003) treats 'grel ba commentary types in monastic education (referenced in Tsadra vocab wiki and elib.org.in siddhanta survey).

## Sub-topic 4: Chinese Buddhist translation practice — ge-yi, yi-shu, kepan

- **Geyi 格義 "matching meanings":** Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geyi — "Prior to Tao-an's time it had been popular to explain Buddhist works by a method of exegesis called ko yi 格義, 'matching meanings'." Also LEE Tsung-ting, "Revisiting the Buddhist 'Geyi' Method" (Academia.edu: https://www.academia.edu/127998784/) and Sheng 2022 (*Religions*/MDPI, citing Zürcher): https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/13/11/1081

- **Kepan 科判 / kewen 科文:** Qiang Wenjie(?) article "Buddhist kepan and..." *Frontiers of Philosophy in China* (Springer, DOI 10.1007/s11702-007-0004-8; PDF blocked, metadata verified): "Kepan, also named as kefen 科分, kewen 科文, keduan 科段, kezhang 科章, kejie 科节... refers to a method by which Buddhist monks interpret sutras..." URL: https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11702-007-0004-8.pdf
- Soothill *Dictionary of Chinese Buddhist Terms* via wisdomlib "Ke wen": "科文... the practice of dividing sentences and paragraphs into sections into sections. This practice began with Dao'an 道安 of the Qin dynasty"; DILA: "break a sūtra or treatise down into small sections for exegesis and analysis" (K. gwamun 과문, J. kamon カモン, V. khoa văn): https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/ke-wen
- Zhiyi's *Fahua Wenju* described as "detailed, phrase-by-phrase commentary" (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Tiantai entry: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buddhism-tiantai/).
- Note for nissaya comparison: kepan structures understanding through hierarchical division (like Thai khwam-sangkhep outlines in nissaya commentaries), while yinyi 音義 "sound-and-meaning" works gloss characters (cf. Xuan Ying's *Yinyi* cited in wisdomlib).

## Sub-topic 5: Korean ŏkhae/eonhae 언해 + gugyeol 구결 (closest East Asian parallel)

- **Wikipedia "Kugyŏl"**: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kugy%C5%8Fl — "a family of annotation systems for rendering texts written in Classical Chinese into understandable Korean."
- **Language Log (V. Mair, 23 Apr 2024) "Korean oralization of Literary Sinitic"** — fetched full text: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=63651
  - Quote: "in gugyeol, the original text in Classical Chinese was not modified, and the additional markers were simply inserted between phrases. The parts of the Chinese sentence would then be read in Korean out of sequence to approximate Korean (SOV)... A similar system... is still used in Japan and is known as kanbun kundoku."
  - Comment thread (Jongseong Park): distinguishes **eumdok gugyeol 음독구결** (Sino-Korean readings + inserted particles, sequential), **seokdok gugyeol 석독구결** (native-equivalent substitution + re-ordering = yeokdok 역독구결), rediscovered complex systems 1970s; National Hangeul Museum replica of 13th–14th-c. woodblock of *Yuga saji ron* (瑜伽師地論 Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra!) using gugyeol signs for reading-order change. Mair's two points: "sharp difference between oralization and vernacularization; kugyŏl belongs to the former, beon-yeog 번역 to the latter." Also mentions eonhae 언해 vs Japanese genkai 諺解; Mair 1994 "Buddhism and the Rise of the Written Vernacular in East Asia," *JAS* 53.3: 707–751.
- **Lee Yong, "Morphology and Syntax in Holes and Scratches: The Latest Stage of Kugyol Research," *Acta Linguistica Asiatica* 1.1 (2011): 53–70** — **PDF downloaded to documents\**. URL/DOI: https://doi.org/10.4312/ala.1.1.53-70 (landing: https://journals.uni-lj.si/ala/article/view/16)
  - Abstract: "The kugyol tradition (a convention for adding Korean grammatical markers at appropriate places to aid the reading of originally Chinese texts) is then explained with concrete examples in three parts: eumdok kugyol (transliteration kugyol), seokdok kugyol (translation kugyol), and jeomto seokdok-kugyol (translation kugyol with point marks)." Buddhist-text driven; discovery of *Yugasijiron* (2000) with detailed point maps.
- **Kim Young Wook 2006, "The Korean Kugyol(口訣) and its influence on Japanese Kunten(假片)," KoreaScience**: https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO200621138234686.page — "The writing systems of the Silla dynasty, the Kugyol(口訣), had influenced the earlier states of Japanes[e] Kunten during the 8th century."
- **Harvard "Glossary of Korean Rare Book Terminology"**: https://koreanvernacularbooks.omeka.fas.harvard.edu/glossary-of-terms — *_ch'aja p'yogi_* 借字表記 "borrowed-script orthography" covering **kugyŏl 口訣 (glosses for reading Literary Sinitic texts), hyangch'al 鄕札, idu 吏讀**; pre-Hangul systems co-existing with Hangul.
- **Royal Hangul sutra translations (Gangyeongdogam 간경도감, 1461–1471):** KCI paper on <Neungeomgyeong eonhae> (1462), <Beop hwagyeong eonhae> (1463), <Geumganggyeong eonhae> etc., "structure of Korean annotation of Chinese classics... a mode of translation for convenience of readers": https://www.kci.go.kr/kciportal/ci/sereArticleSearch/ciSereArtiView.kci?sereArticleSearchBean.artiId=ART001138622
- **Ross King 2023, "Prolegomena to a history of 'Chosŏn-style hanmun'"** (Academia.edu: https://www.academia.edu/104240566/): "advocates for a nuanced view of vernacularization, recognizing the persistent use of Literary Sinitic alongside the development of the Korean script." Cf. also Nam Pung-hyun 2007 "Korean Kugyŏl Markings and their Interpretation" (AAS paper, trans. Ross King; referenced in Kin/King 2021 editor's preface) and Nam Pung-hyun 國語史를 위한 口訣硏究 (2002, cited in ko.wikipedia 구결: https://ko.wikipedia.org/wiki/구결, which cites 박진호 2008 규장각 "구결 자료 해독의 방법과 실제").

## Sub-topic 7: "Hidden translation" concept — kundoku as non-translation

- **De Gruyter chapter (Saito Fumitoshi), "Early modern kanbun and kanbun-kundoku," *Handbook of Historical Japanese Linguistics* (2024), DOI 10.1515/9781614512851-027** — key quote from De Gruyter page snippet: "In contrast to the general case of translation in which no trace of the original text remains after the translation is complete, the case of kanbun-kundoku has the special characteristic that **the original text remains as is even after the translation is complete**." → exact conceptual analogue of nissaya where Pali remains visible under the vernacular.
- **Frellesvig, *A History of the Japanese Language* (CUP 2010), ch. 9 "The sinification of Japanese"** (chapter text verified at https://is.muni.cz/el/1421/podzim2016/JAP200a/um/65771952/Frellesvig_A_History_of_the_Japanese_Language_Sinification_of_Japanese_1_.pdf):
  - "kanbun-kundoku is the interpretation, explication or translation in or into Japanese of Classical Chinese text... popularly thought of as 'reading' Chinese text 'in Japanese'... Although kanbun-kundoku certainly is a kind of 'translation', **we use the broader term 'rendition'**..."
  - Pan-East Asian scope: attested 6th–7th c. "from places as far-flung as Japan, the Korean peninsula, Vietnam and Gao Chang"; transmission from Korea→Japan; "Both kanbun-kundoku and kunten and their histories must be viewed in a pan-East Asian perspective, where... the spread of Buddhism and Buddhist canonical texts in Chinese translation and commentaries written in Chinese played an important role."
- **Bundschuh (OSU), "Rethinking Japan's Earliest Written Narratives" (Osaka U repository PDF, downloaded)**: monks translating the Golden Light Sutra "had to read between the lines, both figuratively and literally"; written result called **kuntengo 訓点語** ("reading gloss language"); "one of over two hundred glossing schemes used in Japan during the Heian period" (citing Alberizzi 2014); argues kunten-marked translated tales "should be recognized as the earliest examples of fully formed narratives written in Japanese."
- Kinsui Satoshi's "virtual Japanese"/pseudo-translation line (Kinsui 2002 cited in Whitman et al.; Kinsui praised Frellesvig's book in CUP reviews).

## Sub-topic 10: General theory — interlinear translation as universal technique

- **Lindisfarne Gospels** (c. 970): "someone added a word-for-word interlinear Old English gloss to the Latin text. While it is a gloss rather than a full translation... the oldest extant version of the gospels in English" — Wordorigins.org: https://www.wordorigins.org/harmless-drudge/english-translations-of-the-bible
