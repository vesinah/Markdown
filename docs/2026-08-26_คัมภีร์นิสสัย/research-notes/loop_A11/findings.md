## 1. The Jain chāyā ("shadow") tradition — Sanskrit renderings beside/between Prakrit lines

The genre is documented primarily through *edition titles* that self-describe as "chāyā" renderings of Prakrit/Apabhraṃśa originals (i.e., a Sanskrit "shadow" running alongside the source text):

- **Doha-Kosa: with chaya and Hindi Tr.**, Sarahapada, Patna: Bihar Rastrabhasa Parisad, 1879, 598 pp. (IGNCA/ASI book 6478). Metadata: "Book Title: Doha-Kosa: with chaya and Hindi Tr." — i.e., Apabhraṃśa dohās + Sanskrit chāyā + Hindi translation.
  https://archive.org/details/in.gov.ignca.6478 — **downloaded**: documents\Dohakosa_1879_with_chaya_Hindi_translation.pdf
- **H.C. Bhayani (ed.), Dohā-gīti-Kośa of Saraha-pāda and Caryā-gīti-Kośa: restored text, Sanskrit chāyā and translation**, Prakrit Text Series 32, Ahmedabad: Prakrit Text Society, 1997. Verified via BSOAS review record (1998) indexed in Semantic Scholar:
  https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/search?query=chaya%20Prakrit%20Sanskrit%20interlinear (record title quoted verbatim above).
- **Candra-lekhā of Rudradāsa, ed. with chaya comm[entary] by A.N. Upadhye**, Bombay: Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan, 1967 (Shri Ganesh Varni Digambar Jain Sansthan copy). Archive.org item title confirms the phrase "Ed With Chaya Comm By A N Upadhye":
  https://archive.org/details/qgoc_chandra-lekha-of-rudradas-ed-with-chaya-comm-by-a-n-upadhye-1967-bombay-bharatiya-vidya-bhavan
- **Dasvekalī Sūtram "Saṃskṛt Chāyā Padārth Mūlātho Peṭh"**, Aatam Ram Ji Maharaj, 1946 — a Sanskrit chāyā laid out under the original verses ("padārth mūlātho peṭh" = word-meaning column beneath the original), i.e. interlinear layout:
  https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.349055
- Modern continuation of the same layout concept in Hindi: **Śūtrakṛtāṅga Sūtra (Hindi Chāyā Anuvād)**, Patel Gopaldas Jivabhai, 1938:
  https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.346504 — **downloaded**: documents\Patel_1938_Sutrakritanga_Hindi_Chaya_Anuwad.pdf ; sibling volume Ācārāṅga: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.346505
- Non-Jain extension proving the term's generic use: **Śivapariṇaya**, a poem in Kashmiri by Kṛṣṇa Rājanaka Razdan, "With a Chaya or gloss i [AUDIT NOTE: preface signed 1924; work issued in fasciculi from 1913 - cite as Grierson 1924 per B08 verification]n Sanskrit by Mahāmahopādhyāya Mukundarāma Śāstrī", ed. G.A. Grierson, 1913 (Calcutta; repr. Asiatic Society 1989):
  https://archive.org/details/sivaparinayahpoe00krsnuoft (metadata title quoted verbatim).

Assessment: "chāyā" = a continuous close rendering ("shadow") of a Middle-Indic or vernacular base text into Sanskrit (or later Hindi), typically printed/written line-under-line or column-beside-column. A formal scholarly *definition paragraph* (e.g., in Bhayani's Indological Studies series) has not yet been captured in this loop — **ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน** for a quotable definition sentence; the genre reality is however multiply attested by the primary editions above. Wikipedia has no article on the genre (opensearch "chaya" returns only personal names/disambiguation).

## 9. Tamil/Malayalam (South Indian) interlingual gloss genres

- **Manipravalam** (Wikipedia, fetched live, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipravalam ): "macaronic language… combines Sanskrit lexicon and Tamil morpho-syntax"; 14th-c. *Līlātilakam*: Manipravalam is a bhāṣā where "Dravida and Sanskrit should combine together like ruby and coral, without the least trace of any discord"; Ciotti & Franceschini (2016) trace Tamil-Sanskrit blending in manuscripts/colophons over centuries; Blackburn (2006): "eventually a hybrid idiom (*manipravalam*) mixing Sanskrit and tamil words, and Sanskrit words with tamil inflections, was devised."
- **Manipravala champu** (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manipravala_champu ): medieval Malayalam champu = "verse portion written in Sanskrit meters while the prose portion [in] medieval Malayalam" — bilingual alternation genre.
- Caveat for our project: maṇipravāḷa is a *mixed-language composition* style rather than an interlinear gloss format; its value to nissaya research is as the South-Indian bilingual register par excellence.
- Tirukkuṟaḷ commentary tradition (Parimelalākar etc.) as intralingual Tamil glossing; Sri-Vaiṣṇava maṇipravāḷa commentaries on the Divya Prabandham as documented interlingual gloss practice: **ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน** in this loop (pages not yet fetched; candidate sources: Pollock 2003 *Literary Cultures in History*, pp. 449, 455–472, already surfaced in the Manipravalam references).

## 10. Did Indian chāyā/cūrṇī practices influence Southeast Asian nissaya?

Southeast-Asian side anchors (for comparison with Loops on Thai/Lanna/Burmese nissaya):

- **John Okell, "Nissaya Burmese"**, Lingua, DOI 10.1016/0024-3841(65)90013-6 (Crossref) — foundational linguistic description of the Burmese nissaya interlinear translation register.
- **P.S. Jaini (ed.), Subodhālaṅkāra Porāṇa-ṭīkā (Mahāsāmi-ṭīkā) by Saṅgharakkhita Mahāsāmi; Abhinava-ṭīkā (Nissaya) (anonymous)**, Oxford: PTS, 2000 — review J.C. Wright, BSOAS, DOI 10.1017/S0041977X02850078 — shows "nissaya" operating as a named commentary type within Pali ṭīkā culture.
- **Peter Skilling, "Reflections on the Pali Literature of Siam"**, DOI 10.2307/J.CTT1VW0Q4Q.25 — Siamese Pali literary context.
- **Explicit transmission claim** (Indian chāyā/cūrṇī → SEA nissaya): **ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน** — no source asserting a direct genetic link was found in Crossref/Semantic Scholar/archive.org metadata searches this loop. What *is* established: (a) both traditions place a close rendering immediately adjacent to a canonical-language base text; (b) both use religious-vernacular registers (chāyā→Sanskrit/Hindi; nissaya→Burmese/Siamese/Lanna); (c) the Pali commentarial network (Jaini's PTS volume) shows the term nissaya circulating inside the same Pali-ṭīkā ecosystem that transmitted texts to SE Asia. Any stronger claim must await manuscript-provenance evidence.
