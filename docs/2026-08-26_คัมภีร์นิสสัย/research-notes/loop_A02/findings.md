## 1. Bode 1909 — her account of nissaya translations & Burmese Pali literature (mined locally)

**Framing of vernacular interpretation (Introduction, pp. xiv–xv; txt lines 386–393):**
> "When we leave Pali and come to Burmese interpretations of Abhidhamma texts we feel that we follow our commentators in a (to us) somewhat dark adventure; but yet the effort of these authors in their own vernacular is interesting as an effort to do more than recite the consecrated words of their ancient masters. Here is the key to the sense really given by the Burmese to Indian abstractions, the key not to be found where they use the exotic and traditional Pali form."

Footnote (lines 395–399) compares Burmese vernacularization to Bede/Alfred's England: abstract questions were discussed in English only "six centuries after Bede."

**Grammatical tradition (pp. xiv–xv; lines 413–424):**
> "The technicalities of Indian grammar have attracted Burmese authors from an early period. The work of the famous Kaccayana is all but canonical among them, and the procession of his followers and commentators stretches through eight centuries of literary history." … "The Pali scriptures had not been a hundred years in Upper Burma before a grammar — the Saddanlti [Saddanīti] — was composed in Pali that called forth the wondering admiration of the scholars of Ceylon."

**Earliest Burmese-language literary use (p. 25; lines 1446–1458):**
> Saddhammasiri (Pagan period, author of Saddatthabhedacinta) "also translated the Brihaja (?) into the Burmese language. He was probably among the first to use Burmese as a literary instrument." Footnote 4 quotes Sasanavamsa p. 75: "So yeva thera Brihajam nama Vedasattham pi marammabhasaya parivattesi." Footnote 5: "M. Duroiselle mentions inscriptions in Burmese of the tenth and eleventh centuries, containing words of Sanskrit derivation…" (→ sub-topic 7.)

**First named vernacular metaphysical authors, Ava period (pp. 43–44; lines 2566–2608):**
> Ariyavamsa "stands out in the Sasanavamsa's record of literary theras as the first name connected with a metaphysical work in the vernacular. He composed an atthayojana or interpretation in Burmese of a commentary called the Anutika on the Abhidhamma."
Silavamsa likewise "laboured to spread religious learning by interpreting Pali texts in the vernacular" — a Burmese atthayojana of the Nettipakarana.
Footnote 3 (line 2606): "I am not sure that this word should not be Mahanissaya (chief commentary or gloss in Burmese)" — re: Ariyavamsa's "Mahanissara."

**Sagaing school, 17th century (pp. 54–55; lines 3098–3137):**
> "The Sagaing monasteries also produced a number of Burmese nissayas (interpretations or paraphrases) on Abhidhamma texts during the seventeenth century."
Ariyalamkara: "(1) Interpretations of the Atthasalinī … the Abhidhammatthavibhavanī … and the Vibhanga… (2) A Pali tika on the Kaccayanabheda, entitled Saratthavikasinl. (3) Ariyalamkara was careful to add a Burmese version to what we should nowadays call his revised edition of Kaccayana's grammar." Work done at Dakkhinavana vihara near Rajamanicula cetiya, Sagaing.

**Abhidhamma nissaya tradition (pp. 58–62; lines 3301–3479):**
> "We have seen how the several books of the Abhidhamma were interpreted and paraphrased in Burmese during the seventeenth century…"
> "Everyone who has seen a collection of Buddhist manuscripts from Burma must have noticed the numerous copies of Abhidhamma texts with vernacular interpretations."
> "**Probably no Pali work on the Abhidhamma has been more often translated and paraphrased than the Abhidhammatthasangaha**, of which the Pitakatthamain alone mentions **twenty-three different Burmese nissayas**. All the most noted theras of the seventeenth century took it in hand…"

**Catalogue of nissaya titles in the Piṭakatthamain list (Bode pp. 105–109; txt lines 5892–6301):**
Atthasalini-nissaya (182), Kaccayana-nissaya (183), Rupasiddhi-nissaya (184), Jataka-nissaya (185), Dhammapadaganthi-nissaya (187), Amarakosanissaya (250), Pindo nissaya (251), Kalapanissaya (252), Sarasangaba-nissaya (287), Rogayatra-nissaya, Roganidana-nissaya, Saddatthabhedacintanissaya, Paranissaya, Sbyaramitikabyanissaya[?] (292–293), Brihajjataka-nissaya.

**Printed colonial-era editions listed in Bode's front matter (lines 127–177)** — evidence of living nissaya practice c. 1900:
- "Milindapaiihavatthu. Burmese translation from the Milinda." Rangoon 1882.
- "Maharupasiddhi, with Burmese interpretation." Rangoon 1906.
- "Nvadi-Moggallana. Pali treatise on Grammar, by Sangharakkhita, based on the Grammar of Moggallana, **with Burmese nissaya**." Rangoon 1900.
- "Alankara-nissaya. Being the Subodhalamkara of Sangharakkhita in Pali, with commentary." Rangoon 1889.
- Later catalogue section (lines 5041–5161): Alahkaranissaya of the Yaw-mya-sa Atwin-wun; Vuttodaya "followed by a nissaya in Burmese (1897)".

## 6. History of monastic education & rote+nissaya learning (Dhammasami thesis, mined locally)

- **Method (thesis pp. 47–48; extracted.txt lines 1970–1987):**
  > "Paraphrasing, also known as nissaya, was done initially by the teacher, and was repeated aloud by all students. Every sentence and paragraph was studied… the teacher would delegate the task of paraphrasing to the best student… He would then be known as **zar-so**, 'reciter of the Burmese paraphrase', and was treated as an assistant teacher… **Students were not usually allowed to read the nissaya by others** in their preparation for the class because that would misled the teacher when he assessed the competence of his students in Pali…"
- **Curriculum sequence (p. 42; lines 1779–1783):**
  > "the student learnt the MaNgala-sutta… He **memorised the MaNgala-sutta in Pali, and then the nissaya, before learning their meaning** as expressed in a few Burmese poems."
- **Institutionalization**: King **Thalun (1629–1648)** "was the first to introduce a curricular system of monastic education and formal examination" (Herngseng quoting Dhammasami 2004, 11; extracted.txt Herngseng lines 328–341); "the works on the nissaya literature were produced in almost all the reigns from the time of Thalun… because a nissaya was mainly, and still is, for students rather than scholars." Under Thalun, Vinaya nissayas dominate; the shin-kyint-wut (novice rules) nissaya "were compiled more than 30 times" (Dhammasami 2018, 21).
- **Bodawpaya (1782–1819)**: "repeatedly made attempts to impose formal examinations as a tool to bring the Sangha under tighter monarchical control. Bodawpaya systematised the existing formal examinations, the **Pathamapyan**, and introduced new ones, the **Vinaya examinations**" (lines 779–790). Yet "until the mid-nineteenth century [education] was still by and large based on informal textual study."
- **Modern degrees**: TipiTakadhara examinations requiring memorization of the entire Tipitaka (lines 491–493); Pakhokku method retains zar-so tradition (line 5152).
- Monastery libraries keep "only the TipiTaka and some nissaya literature currently in use" (lines 10372–10376).

## 8. Konbaung royal patronage

From Dhammasami thesis (local):
- **Bodawpaya**: 1783 appointed four sayadaws (*thathanhtein*) to Sudhamma Council; royal order 24 May 1784 appointed eleven sayadaws as **Pathamapyan examiners**; 1786 replaced council with single **thathanabaing** (GuBamuninda); 1787 successor **Maung Taung Ñāṇābhivaṃsa** "had been in charge of copying the TipiTaka" (ROB IV, p.414) (lines 1493–1532).
- Cost context: "only the king and some members of the royal family had the authority and resources to donate a complete set of the TipiTaka because 'a copy of the piTaka was more costly than erecting a hollow pagoda'" (lines 1600–1603).
- **Mindon (1853–1878)**: revived Sudhamma Council; **Fifth Council (Pañcamasaṅgāyanā) held 1871**, "lasted five months and three days; all the Pali canonical texts and their commentaries were recited, inscribed both on palm-leaves and on **729 marble slabs**, by **2400 bhikkhus**"; slabs preserved at **Kuthodaw Pagoda**, Mandalay; king, queen, crown prince each nominally took charge of transcribing Suttanta-/Abhidhamma-/Vinaya-piṭaka; marble chosen over the already-available printing press against feared British conquest (lines 4473–4501). Note: Thailand does not recognize the Mandalay council (n. 93).
- **Bagyidaw (1819–1837)** after the First Anglo-Burmese War: "copying the TipiTaka… and holding the Pathamapyan" among merit-works tying monarchy to Saṅgha (lines 4503–4514).
- From Bode 1909 (local): Ñāṇābhivaṃsa "at the request of the king [Bodawpaya] undertook a Burmese translation of the Pali commentary on the Jataka (the Jatakatakatha)" (p. 78; lines 4260–4262); his *RajadhirajavilasinI* composed for Bodawpaya's consecration at the king's command (lines 4267–4299). Under **Pagan Min**: "The Anguttara-, Samyutta-, and Dighanikayas were translated with their commentaries" by Paññajotabhidhaja, Mañijotasaddhammalamkara, Medhābhibvaṃsa (p. 92; lines 4891–4898). Under **Mindon**: Sangharaja Ñeyyadhamma expounded Majjhimanikāya commentary; "Under his direction a Burmese translation of the commentary was prepared"; Jataka text translated by Medhabhivamsa; Mindon treasured the title "'Convener of the Fifth Council'" (pp. 92–93; lines 4937–4973).
- Chaining lead (unverified content): SSRN preprint "Pali Buddhism and the Burmese Monarchs: Patronage of Buddhist Texts in the 19th Century," DOI 10.2139/ssrn.5169532.
