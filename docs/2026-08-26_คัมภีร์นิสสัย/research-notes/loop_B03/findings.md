## Sub-topic 2 — BITEXT TYPOLOGY, EXACT WORDING

### (a) Three formats — *Liquid Language*, JIPh 50 (2022)
From the published abstract (Springer Nature Link, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10781-022-09516-2, fetched live 2026-08-26):

> "The three bilingual texts or 'bitexts' analyzed in this article each follow a different format: **rearranged phrasal gloss, selective sentence gloss, and vernacular expansion**."

Context from same abstract: "These formats draw on the pan-Theravada technology of the bitext to create a dynamic oscillation between Pali and Khmer passages, amplifying patterns established by differential practices of listening and recitation. The key medium in this process is language, which serves as a fluid intermediary that makes the relational activity of scripture possible."
Case studies: "three examples of Pali-Khmer sermons composed in Cambodia between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries."

### (b) Three-step composition model with four presentation modes — JAOS 2020, Table 1
Exact wording from `jaos2020_fulltext.txt` lines 121–167 ("Table 1. The three main steps of bitext composition, along with attendant options, techniques, and modes"):

> "**1. Selection** — Selecting an Indic passage to analyze — 1a. Citation: Citing an existing Indic text; 1b. Invention: Inventing an Indic text.
> **2. Analysis** — Reading and translating the selected passage — 2a. Parsing: Dividing the passage into its component parts; 2b. Amplification: Supplying contracted and implied Indic phrases; 2c. Rearrangement: Changing the word order to vernacular syntax; 2d. Annotation: Marking grammatical features with particles; 2e. Gloss: Providing Indic and/or vernacular definitions.
> **3. Presentation** — Tailoring the analysis for a specific purpose — 3a. Philological: Selective analysis in an abbreviated style; 3b. Exegetical: Scholastic expansion of analysis in prose; 3c. Homiletic: Narrative expansion of analysis in prose; 3d. Poetic: Aesthetic expansion of analysis in verse."

Accompanying prose (lines 168–180): "All Indic-vernacular bitexts purport to select (1) an Indic text or texts to analyze passage by passage… Each analyzed passage may be subsequently presented in various modes (3). The four most common are philological (3a), exegetical (3b), homiletic (3c), and poetic (3d), but variants and combinations of these appear as well. Each mode roughly corresponds with a particular intended audience: a philological presentation favors linguistic training, an exegetical approach is suitable for scholastic readers, a homiletic mode is honed for public preaching, and a poetic treatment may be intended for recitation to court circles or other highly literate audiences."
Also (lines 172–174): "In Siamese and Lanna contexts it is not uncommon for all five techniques (2a–2e) to be applied to each passage." Pali-Sinhala bitexts omit 2d because Sinhala signals it morphologically (n. 10, lines 199–200).

### (c) Dissertation-era precursors of the typology (Ch. 6, pp. 317–377)
- Four basic forms of Khmer *samrāy*: "1) original-syntax fully bilingual prose, 2) syntactically rearranged fully bilingual prose, 3) partially bilingual prose, and 4) fully bilingual Pali/Khmer verse" (`diss_fulltext.txt` lines 15297–15299; parameters listed lines 15289–15295).
- Burmese taxonomies surveyed: Tin Lwin's four types of nissaya "(verbatim, free translation, ornate, and translation with short notes)" (lines 15404–15407); U Nyunt Maung's tripartite "(extended gloss, condensed gloss, and explanatory)" (lines 15409–15411); Pyi Phyo Kyaw on *ayakauk/akauk* (lines 15412–15413); Kirichenko's seven kinds (personal communication cited, lines 15451–15457).

## Sub-topic 3 — Definition of "bitext"; distinction from translation/commentary

Canonical definition, JAOS 2020 (`jaos2020_fulltext.txt` lines 38–44):

> "**I define bitexts as texts that are presented bilingually, with portions in one language mixed together with portions in another, typically in an interphrasal or interlinear arrangement.** Indic-vernacular bitexts may be structured in various ways, and need not contain equal proportions of Indic and Southeast Asian material. In most cases, the vernacular portions of a bitext provide an analytic reading of the Pali or Sanskrit portions, sometimes accompanied by an interpretive or literary commentary. Such bitexts, known by diverse names in local languages, form a significant portion of all extant written material produced between the twelfth and nineteenth centuries in what is now Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, and Thailand."

Distinction logic extractable from his usage:
- Bitext ≠ plain translation: the vernacular is an **analytic reading** (parsing/amplification/rearrangement/annotation/gloss of the source), not free rendering; conversely even "wholly vernacular texts" governed by nissaya principles "might properly be considered bilingual as well" (diss lines 15475–15480, 15495–15502).
- Bitext vs commentary: commentaries are monolingual Pali elaborations (aṭṭhakathā/ṭīkā); bitexts deliver equivalent services ("glossing each syntactic constituent… additional commentary beyond the gloss… lightly rearranges or adds implied words") **in the vernacular** (diss lines 15524–15530). In exegetical mode, however, "their scholastic expansions read much like the monolingual Pali commentaries they engage" (JAOS lines 483–487) — i.e., the boundary is format/language-mixing, not function.
- Two arrangement formats named: "interlinear" (khaam hvăt/tvă kṣien inked annotations between lines) and "interphrasal" (vernacular interleaved phrase-by-phrase) (JAOS lines 13, 38–39, 241–247, 466–477; diss §6.5.3 lines 17670–17790).
- Hybridity formula: "even as they localize the Pali into the vernacular, they move the vernacular toward the Pali. Nevertheless, even this hybrid vernacular maintains the fluency of a living language" (diss lines 16992–16995).
- Against McDaniel's "idiosyncratic sermon and lecture notes" reading, Walker argues "strict conventions were followed… shared across mainland Southeast Asia from the eleventh century onward" (JAOS lines 59–67; diss lines 15306–15332, 15724–15740, 17816–17823).

## Sub-topic 4 — His use of "nissaya" for Khmer/Thai materials

Walker **does NOT simply adopt the Burmese term as umbrella**. Strategy: (i) coin supra-regional English "bitext"; (ii) list local genre names per tradition; (iii) use local spellings (nisai/nissăy) for Lanna/Lao/Siamese material; (iv) use Khmer samrāy/prè lot prayog for Cambodia.

Local-names footnote, JAOS 2020 (n. 1, lines 77–79):

> "The most common local names for bitexts include **nissaya (Burmese), naṃ/trā-ai (Mon), nisrai/nissăy (Lanna, Lao, Siamese), nāmaśăbd/săpº (Lanna, Lao, Siamese), cuṇṇiyapad (Siamese), ṕlè yak śăbd (Siamese), and prè lot prayog (Khmer)**."

Key passages:
- Lanna title usage: "The latter [nisai] comes from Pali nissaya. But whether săpº/nāma săpº manuscripts represent a different genre than nisai manuscripts remains unclear" (diss lines 17247–17253); "If nisai/nissaya and săpº/śăbd/nāmasadda are practically one and the same, then those that bear vohānº (vohāra) in their titles are quite different… these latter manuscripts contain texts that are partially, rather than fully, bilingual" (lines 17254–17256). Footnote 119 quotes McDaniel: difference between nāmasadda and nissaya "not consistent enough to state definitively" (lines 17260–17263).
- Lanna genre label in manuscript collections: "nisai (nisăy นิสัย), the term applied to this particular genre of bilingual text in Lanna manuscript collections" (n. 101, lines 16980).
- vohāra colophon marker: "Almost all manuscripts bearing this term in their title are bitexts presented in a homiletic style" (JAOS lines 840–844).
- Khmer: *samrāy* "differs from the usage of nissaya, nāmasadda, and vohāra in Siamese, Lao, or Lanna contexts in that the Khmer is broad enough to cover the latter three terms… more parallel to nissaya in Burmese or perhaps even sannaya in Sinhalese" (diss lines 15252–15254); *samrāy lot prayog / prè lot prayog* = "translation [made by] leaping [around in each] sentence" (lines 15258–15261); Thai parallel *yak śăbd / ṕlè yak śăbd* = "translation [made by] lifting words…" (lines 15282–15283).
- Generalization: "nissaya is more than simply a genre of bilingual Pali-vernacular texts. It is an approach to grammatical analysis, translation, and composition…" (diss lines 15475–15477).
- ⚠ Separate Pali-religious sense also documented (do not conflate): Khmer *nissăy/upanissăy* as "karmic affinity/dependence" (diss lines 27482–27494; line 8196 "karmic affinities (nissaya)"). Relevant caution for Thai usage: Thai นิสัย in manuscript titles = the bitext genre sense (nisai), while ordinary Thai nisai = "disposition/habit."

Okell anchor quote (diss lines 15427–15443, quoting Okell 1965): nissaya "could be a kind of grammar or manual as well as a dictionary… there is nothing to distinguish a nissaya rendering of 1491 from one of 1910."

## Sub-topic 5 — JAOS 2020 periodization + case studies (1450–1850)

Periodization claims:
- Title range: **1450–1850** (line 4). Bitexts "form a significant portion of all extant written material produced between the twelfth and nineteenth centuries" in Cambodia/Laos/Myanmar/Thailand (lines 51–53).
- Diffusion chronology (lines 191–216): first-millennium Pali-Sinhala bitexts with the three steps; earliest mainland SE Asian example = Pali-Mon inscription "late eleventh or early twelfth century"; Pali-Burmese extant from late thirteenth century; Sanskrit-Burmese listed in a 1442 inscription; among Tai groups by mid-second millennium: "**The earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext is thought to date from 1482 in Ayutthaya. The earliest securely dated Pali-Lanna bitext surviving in manuscript form dates from 1552**"; Sanskrit-Lanna 1578 (DLNTM 011318001_01); oldest surviving Pali-Lao bitext (Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha) CS 948 = 1586/87 CE (n. 16, PLMP 08040102007_04). Caveat: "Pali-Siamese, Pali-Lanna, and Pali-Lao bitexts almost certainly existed prior to the late fifteenth century, though the extreme paucity of manuscripts before that time precludes firm conclusions" (lines 214–216).
- First-millennium epigraphic background: interphrasal Sanskrit-Pyu inscription tentably sixth century; Sanskrit-Khmer inscriptions early second millennium with "parallel content, if divergent form" (lines 181–190).

Four case-study clusters (lines 217–227):
1. **Philological**: mid-nineteenth-century interlinear Pali-Siamese annotations, Dhammapada-aṭṭhakathā MS, Swift Family Collection, Bancroft Library, UC Berkeley (ff 4MS PL4251 no. 14) — "a hitherto undeciphered form of manuscript annotation in seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Siam" (khaam hvăt / tvă kṣien script; numerals 1–4 and syllables ka/kā/ki/kī encode syntactic rearrangement; ăn vā¹ nominative particle; maḥ = Pali-Pali gloss marker). "One of the most sophisticated forms of vernacular grammatical analysis to emerge prior to the development of modern linguistics" (lines 381–385).
2. **Exegetical**: two Pali-Lanna bitexts on Abhidhammatthavibhāvinī-ṭīkā, Ālambanasaṅgaha — anonymous *Nissayº ṭīkā abhidhammatthasańgaha*, 1585 CE (CS 947), Văt Hlai² Hin Hlvaṅ, Lampang (DLNTM 030103002_00); and former monk Nandapañño's *Investigation* (*biccaranā*), 1638 CE (CS 1000), Chiang Saen (DLNTM 010720003_01). Rhetorical-question refutations; multiple interpretations separated by Pali vā; quotations from Saddatthabhedacintā, Balāvatāra, Saddasāratthajālinī, Abhidhammāvatāra.
3. **Homiletic**: *Nisrai mahosathapaṇḍit* (Mahosatha-jātaka), earliest copy 1563 CE, Văt Pān² Hluk, Lampang (PNTMP 030607170_00) — author's own two Pali invocations then parsed/rearranged/glossed; explicit audience "listeners" (sotūnaṃ); and *vohānº yo so / ṭīkā yo so*, 1666 CE, Văt Hlvaṅ Rājasăṇṭhān, Phayao (DLNTM 070710018_00) — Sāratthadīpanī ten epithets; earliest stock sermon opening "sādhavō / Listen, O good people…" formula "witnessed in countless homiletic bitexts across Tai and Khmer cultures from the seventeenth century to the present" (lines 856–862).
4. **Poetic**: *Mahājāt́i gāṃ hlvaṅ* (Mahachat kham luang), composed 1482 under King Borommatrailokkanat of Ayutthaya (r. 1431–1488) — "the earliest extant Tai-language version of the Mahāvessantara-jātaka and one of the earliest known pieces of Siamese literature" (lines 940–944); glosses rhymed in rāy¹ yāv meter; ch. 7 Mahāban mixes Pali words into glōṅ stanzas — "the earliest recorded instance of such experimentation" (line 1195); began the royal gāṃ hlvaṅ genre (lines 950–953).

Conclusion section restates the four modes with dates (lines 1212–1248) and closes: digitization will place bitexts "in the global history of reading and translation" (lines 1249–1255). Parallel East/South Asian framing: "vernacular reading," following Whitman & Kornicki (lines 102–108 and nn. 7–8).

## Sub-topic 7 — Theoretical framing vs Pollock

Direct engagement in diss Ch. 1 (`diss_fulltext.txt` lines 2919–2927):

> "by Sheldon Pollock's articulation of a progression from a '**Sanskrit cosmopolis**' to a '**vernacular millennium**,' the role of Buddhist translations in the formation of vernacular literature has not been fully explored. Steven Collins' 'Pali imaginaire' and Peter Skilling's 'databank' metaphor suggest related models by which translators and authors could create vernacular Buddhist texts that draw on the resources of Pali. Nidhi Eoseewong, by comparing various Thai translations of the Vessantara-jātaka, shows how vernacular literature emerges from changing attitudes towards translation and adaptation."

Footnote 131 (lines 2949–2952) cites Pollock, *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men* (UC Press 2006); "The Cosmopolitan Vernacular," JAS 57.1 (1998): 6–37; "Cosmopolitan and Vernacular in History," Public Culture 12.3 (2000): 591–625. Also cites Hallisey ("in medieval Sri Lanka, it was Sinhala that was superposed onto Pali," lines 2928–2931) and Blackburn's "dialogical interplay between translocal and local languages" (lines 2937–2940).

The JAOS article operationalizes the Pollock thesis in its own thesis sentence (`jaos2020_fulltext.txt` lines 14–19) — this is the single best quotable formulation:

> "Such bitexts, arranged in both interlinear and interphrasal formats, gradually allowed second-millennium Southeast Asian writers to sprout a vernacular literature from the established branches of Pali genres. Bitexts themselves formed the basis for a new literary style that stemmed from the techniques of vernacular reading, a style that set the standard for belles-lettres until the early twentieth century. **The spread of Pali-vernacular bitexts in Southeast Asia allowed for the literary elevation of the vernacular without renouncing the cosmopolitan idiom of Pali.**"

(That final clause mirrors Pollock's "cosmopolitan vernacular" argument but substitutes Pali for Sanskrit and monastic reading practice for court kāvya.) He also flags divergence: SE Asian bitext techniques "supported a wide variety of textual genres, including linguistic study, scholastic commentary, public sermons, and versified belles-lettres" unlike European/East Asian parallels (lines 105–108). Diss repeats influence-on-prose claim via Okell: bilingual conventions shaped "good prose style… into the twentieth century and beyond," even composing Thai prose "in Pali first, then back-translated" (diss lines 15888–15885 incl. n. 48).

Current monograph statement (Stanford HCBSS profile, fetched live): in-progress "**Classical Reading, Vernacular Writing: A Bitextual History of Southeast Asian Buddhism**" — "an intellectual history of Buddhist translation… reframes the story of how vernacular literature came to thrive in mainland Southeast Asia by centering Buddhist approaches to reading and writing."

## Cross-cutting synthesis for the Thai nissaya project

1. Walker gives TWO compatible typologies: macro (JAOS Table 1: selection→analysis[5 techniques]→presentation[4 modes]) and micro-formats (JIPh 2022: rearranged phrasal gloss / selective sentence gloss / vernacular expansion). The micro-formats align roughly with diss forms 2 (syntactically rearranged fully bilingual), 1 (original-syntax fully bilingual), and 3 (partially bilingual/vohāra-style) respectively — mapping inference from diss §6.5 wording; exact one-to-one mapping inside the paywalled article: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน.
2. Terminological recommendation extractable for our Thai project: use "bitext" as analytic umbrella; reserve นิสัย/nisai (Lanna), นามสัทท์/săpº/yok sap ยกศัพท์ (Siamese), vohāra (homiletic partial bitexts), samrāy/prè lot prayog (Khmer); "nissaya" strictly for Burmese or when quoting Pali etymology.
