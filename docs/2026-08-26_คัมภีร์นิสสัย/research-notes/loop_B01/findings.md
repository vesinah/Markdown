## 1. The seven nissaya interpretive methods attributed to Kāruṇika (2008)

Provenance: [P:454–476] (section 5 opener). Lead-in sentence:

> "Different types of nissayas may be employed as different methods of translation and interpretation. In other words, the word-to-word translation of Burmese nissaya uses a spectrum of methods. According to Kāruṇika (2008, 10), the following seven methods are commonly employed in Burma:" [P:454–457]

The complete list, verbatim:

1. "**pāṭhānusārī nissaya** method – a simple translation of Pali sentences without adding words;" [P:458–459]
2. "**pāṭhasesa** nissaya method – words may be added in order to better explain the text's actual meaning. In this case, the additional words are drawn out from the original Pali text as well as previously mentioned statements, canonical texts, commentary or sub-commentary or occasionally from a Tipiṭaka authority expert. The principle is that additional words cannot be any of personal opinion but strictly from authoritative references or sources;" [P:460–466]
3. "**saddattha** method – interpretation is based on the original similar to 'literal translation';" [P:467–468]
4. "**saṅketattha** method – interpretation follows the precedent example which is traditionally or conventionally accepted;" [P:469–470]
5. "**paññatyattha** method – same as 4: focus on conventional terminology to interpret the texts for better understanding;" [P:471–472]
6. "**vohārattha** method – same as 4 and 5 – focus on conventional usage to better understand the meaning of the texts;" [P:473–474]
7. "**adhippāyattha** method – provide nissaya with direct or easily understood meaning." [P:475–476]

Worked examples (Table 1) mapping words to methods: *khīṇāsavo* (saddattha: "The one who has completed the eradication of the taints."; paññatyattha/vohārattha/saṅketattha: "Arahat, or Saint."; adhippāyattha: "Venerable Sariputta or Moggalāna (depends on the context)"); *dvāriko* (saddattha "One who is associated with the door." / adhippāyattha "Gatekeeper"); *buddho* ("The knower." / "The Buddha or The Lord." / "Venerable Gotama, or Monk Gotama."); *karopeti* ("Let him make." / "Order him to do." / "Please do it or speak out.") [P:503–532].

Source cited: "Kāruṇika, Ashin. 2008. *Dhammacariya Myatshu* (Dhammacariya Method), 3rd Edition. Yangon: Siritipitaka Publication." [P:756–757]; confirmed identically in the Crossref-deposited reference list (CIT0018) [X: api.crossref.org/works/10.1163/26659077-25010027].
**Verification status:** list quoted faithfully across all three hosted copies of the article; the underlying 2008 Burmese-language book itself — ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน (no independent catalog/library hit found online beyond this citation chain).

Related: the article also reports two regional training styles: "the Mandalay method and the Pakhokku method", e.g. Mandalay approach lecturing *Namotassa bhagavato arahato sammāsambuddhassa* "for months consecutively with various analyses using all available methods together with commentary and sub-commentary explanations (Pannabhoga 2020, 29)", cross-referring Dhammasami 2004, 134–147 [P:482–501].

## 2. Ane'-de margin annotation and classroom procedure (zāso repeater)

All key passages verbatim:

- Author's auto-ethnographic grounding: "In this section I provide the actual nissaya training in a Burmese monastic classroom based on my observation as a monastic student for over a decade at two leading monastic institutes in Mandalay and Pegu. This will substantiate my research findings, yet with some limitations." [P:571–574]
- "Most nissaya authors used to be zāso 'repeaters' or 'under tutelage' students. The literal meaning of zāso means poet, composer or annotator; the verb zāte means learning Buddhist scriptures under the tutelage of a mentor and zāwā-lecture is the course of learning in a Buddhist monastery. The zāso is someone who has acquired proficient knowledge and has mastered various genres of literature in the course of learning Buddhist texts. Learning by heart is also part of the skill set required of the zāso. … Thus, someone cannot become a zāso unless he devotes his time to it and undergoes methodical preparation. A well-known modern nissaya producer, Ashin Janakābhivamsa, used to be a zāso when he took courses in Pakokku." [P:581–594]
- Procedure: "Basically, nissaya classroom teaching is where the teacher leads by reading out loud and the zāso follows, preferably with a louder and clearer voice. One of the requisites is that a zāso should be clear with good voice in order to lead the classroom while assisting the teachers. At this level, there is only one teacher and one repeater reading the nissaya in the classroom. The other students may listen carefully and note their nissaya's interpretations. This process is known as ane'-de: 'marking annotation in the margin' (see figure 3)." [P:595–603]
- Definition: "An Ane'-de: is a technique whereby they learn a nissaya's interpretation while making notes around the available space of a Pali or an Atthakathā textbook." [P:603–605]
- Sub-techniques: "there are many learning and interpretative techniques used in the nissaya training, such as ane' kau': interpretation; ane' pjan': translation; ane' pwa: extended meaning; ane' hpwin: definition; ane' hpo: elucidation; and ane' adei' be: interpreted meaning." [P:609–611] (cross-ref Myanmar-English Dictionary, [P:612])
- Composition maxim verse: "paṭhamaṃ kare padacchedaṃ, samāsādiṃ tato kare; samāsādo kate pacchā, atthaṃ niyyātha paṇḍito." with translation "The wise one, first of all, should break up the compound words and, hereafter, the meaning should be brought out." [P:617–620], then demonstrated word-by-word as a nissaya-style gloss [P:622–626], sourced to Saṃpyaṅṭīkā/Thanbyin Ṭīkā and Janakābhivaṃsa 2000a [footnotes 3–4, P:641–646].
- Figure captions: fig. 1 "Sample of nissaya with pāthasesa (inserted terms), Pārājikān Nissaya text, Chaṭṭhasaṅgīti Edition." [P:366–367]; fig. 3 "A session of nissaya class with instructor and a repeater (zāso), second from left, followed by student-monks interlineating nissaya's interpretation in their printed textbook. Veluvan Monastery, Taunggyi, Shan State." [P:381–383]; fig. 4 "Sample of Ane'-de, with annotated translation in the Aṭṭhasālinī-aṭṭhakathā textbook. (Maw Lu Sayadaw student textbook unpublished)." [P:410–411]
- Reference entry: "Maw Lu, Sayadaw (undated) Ane'-de … an unpublished Manual." [P:763–764]

**Verification status:** first-hand observational data (single-author ethnography); no independent corroboration sought/available — treat as primary testimony of a practitioner-insider.

## 3. Article's account of earliest nissaya evidence

(a) Abstract/intro claim: "Archaeological excavation in Myanmar found that the Sanskrit-Pyu nissaya at Srikṣetra in the 7th ce, and Kyansittha's inscriptions at Pagan in 12th ce were the earliest forms of nissaya." [P:55–57]

(b) Fuller version, citing Tin Lwin: "It is, however, almost impossible to state with certainty when the first Pali-Burmese nissaya was written. Tin Lwin (1961, 5–11) claims that the oldest extant nissaya in Burma is the Khuddakasikkhā Nikāya nissaya, written by Mahākassapa during the reign of Narapatisithu (1174–1211). … According to Lwin, the development of nissaya can be dated back to before and after 1448 and he considers that they originate as Pyu nissaya (Srikṣetra), Mon nissaya (Pagan), Sinhalese nissaya (sannaya) and then Burmese nissaya. Pyu King Vikrama founded the Pyu kingdom at Srikṣetra in 637 ce. A prehistoric excavation (7th ce) by the Department of Archaeology, Ministry of Culture, found a Pyu nissaya, consisting of an interpretation of a Sanskrit passage at Srikṣetra, near modern Pyay, 260km north-west of Yangon (Tin Lwin 2014, 14–15; 43–49)." [P:248–261] Plus: "'the nissayas have been known to Burmese scholars at least for the last five centuries' (Tin Lwin 1961, i)". [P:264–265]

(c) Counterweight caveat quoted approvingly: Ruiz-Falqués (2015, 10): "nissayas are later than the Pali grammatical texts. We actually have no record of grammatical nissayas from the Pagan period"; article concludes: "We can only assume that nissaya became popular from the 15th century, as Tin Lwin suggests, although we need further study." [P:265–270]

(d) Walker 2020 support: "Walker (2020, 678) supports the view that Pali-Burmese examples of nissayas are extant from the late thirteenth century and several Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts are listed in an inscription from 1442. The date of the Thaungdwing governor's inscription contains … a lengthy list of Pali and Sanskrit works together with their nissayas which the pious governor and his wife offered to the monastery…" [P:271–278]

(e) Thanbyin/Saṃpyaṅṭīkā footnote: "This stanza appears in the grammatical text called Saṃpyaṅṭīka (Thanbyin Ṭīkā). It was composed by Minister Thanbyin, a Pali scholar who served at the king's court during the reign of Narapatisithu of the Pagan dynasty (1174–1211) (as the oldest attestation…)" [fn.4, P:643–646]; fn.3: "Saṃpyaṅṭīkā also spelled as Thanbyin Ṭīkā (1275, 2, 8–9); Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa (2000) Abhidhammattha-vibhāvinī Nissaya; Falqués (2015, 168)." [P:641–642] — mirror-confirmed verbatim on ScienceDirect copy [X].

**Verification status:** see §4 below (Thanbyin) and §"Historical claims scoreboard".

## 5. Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa Bhāsāṭīkā series and Ti Kar Kyaw

- "modern nissayas such as Bhāsāṭikā-s titles are some of good examples by the late Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa (1900–1977) who is popularly known as monastic education reformer of the 20th century. This is perhaps due to the orientation of monastic learning and focus on examinations, whereby Janakābhivaṃsa carefully crafted his nissayas to meet the students' needs." [P:191–199]
- Eight-step study program from Silakkhan Bhāsāṭīkā: "relying on nissaya alone, as well-known scholar and learned monk, Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa (2000b, 594) has pointed out, is not enough; it is necessary to go beyond nissaya for further studies, especially the Sutta studies. His recommendation for how nissaya should be used and studied is by following these eight steps:" — steps verbatim: (1) beginner word-to-word nissaya; (2) fluent nissaya → translate every word into Burmese prose; (3) prose without using all Pali words; (4) focus on specific meanings, summarize/expand; (5) read Pali slowly without nissaya; (6) compose Pali sentences, verse, well-spoken Pali; (7) devote faith/wisdom/energy to Buddha's virtues; (8) translating Sutta Piṭaka benefits Burma, "Both monastic and lay lives will attain a higher level for the promulgation of the Buddha Sāsanā (Janakābhivaṃsa 2000b, 594)." [P:203–225]
- Dominance of the series: "the Bhāsāṭīkā series by Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa is widely and frequently used at all levels of student-monks more than any other nissaya because the author has provided clear explanation, interpretation and more additional comments. Furthermore, he also divided his nissayas into different levels. For example, in a compendium of Abhidhamma text like Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha, he has two kinds of nissaya, one for basic level and the second for higher level. The Bhāsāṭīkā series has served as handbooks for modern students." [P:557–564]
- Conclusion echo: "the widely used Bhāsāṭīkā series of nissaya texts by Ashin Janakābhivaṃsa in many monastic institutions will not disappear anytime soon." [P:703–705]

## 6. Thai/Lao comparison (McDaniel) and the "Thai curricula lack nissaya" claim

- "McDaniel (2008, 131–136) calls this method of learning 'lifting words' in similar monastic learning institutions in northern Thailand and Laos." [P:61–62]
- Genre triad: "Three variations of literary genres: nissaya ('support'), nāmasadda ('glossary'), and vohāra ('lifting words') are popularly used in Northern Thailand and Laos as pedagogical texts while students and teachers conduct 'languaging'. … According to McDaniel (2008, 6), the nissayas, (supports), were used by monastic teachers in the pre-modern period from the 16th to 19th centuries in Thailand and Laos." [P:351–361]
- Overlap: "Unlike in Burma, McDaniel explains (2008, 131), in Thailand and Laos nissaya, nāmasadda and vohāra genres overlap in style, pedagogical methods and social use. He describes how the nissaya and nāmasadda manuscripts are characterized by close, word-for-word glosses, whereas the vohāra cite longer passages of twelve to twenty-five words from Pali source texts and offer extensive and creative vernacular translations and commentaries. Generally, nissaya fall between the two genres and often cite four to ten words before offering glosses and creative asides." [P:362–374]
- Parallel genres elsewhere: "According to McDaniel (2008, 119), a similar genre … in Sri Lanka, as sannaya, and in Thailand and Laos as nissaya, namasadda, and vohāra in contemporary periods. The Sutranipāta-sannaya, a Pali-Sinhala word-to-word translation written in the 12th ce, is the earliest example … (Pruitt 1992, 278)." [P:229–240]
- Thai insider testimony: "According to Gandhasārābhivaṃsa, (Personal Interview, 6 February 2020) a Thai Pali scholar monk who studied in Burma for over ten years and received a higher degree, the Dhammācariya, nissayas in Thailand are not as influential in the learning tradition as they are in Burma. While Burmese nissaya include canonical texts, commentaries, sub-commentaries, and so on, Thai nissaya are limited to canonical texts and other commentaries. Nissaya texts are called gambhi-nisai (gambhi-nissaya, from Pali gambhīra-nissaya 'deep support'), which are available to us as Kaccāyana Gambhi-nisai and Saddasāratthajālinī nisai for example. He also discovered that nissayas in Burma have different recensions according to their historical time periods: for example, nissaya in the Pagan and Ava periods tend to have fewer explanations, such as the Ne Yin Sayadaw nissaya, while later nissayas offer more explanations and interpretations such as the Pakokku nissayas along with others." [P:375–396]
- Hinüber: "Hinüber also (1997, 100–1) states that nissayas in Thailand can be considered as vohāra or nāmasap (from Sanskrit nāma-śabda 'noun-word') as a word-for-word explanation and that the difference between vohāra and nissaya is not clear." [P:396–399]

## 7. Definitions of nissaya and the encyclopaedic-compilation thesis

Definitions layered through the article:
- Vinaya root sense: "The Pali term nissaya denotes a concept found in the Mahāvagga Pāḷi of the Vinaya-piṭaka texts as a requisite rule for the newly ordained novice and nun (Oldenberg 1879, 58). In this sense, nissaya is a 'guidance'. A newly ordained monk or nun is required to live under the 'guidance' of his or her preceptor (upajjhāya) for a minimum of five years… If the new monk loses his preceptor, he must seek nissaya from a teacher." [P:156–165]; four requisites list piṇḍiyālopabhojana/paṃsukūlacīvara/rukkhamūlasenāsana/pūtimuttabhesajja [P:174–178].
- Lexicography: equivalent to Skt niśraya, corresponding to āśraya "to sit on, or that on which anything depends" (PED; Soothill & Hodous); Princeton Dict.: "requisite," "reliance," or "support" [P:166–173].
- Working genre definition: "nissaya refers to the literary genre of word-to-word translation of either canonical Pali or Aṭṭhakathā texts, which is a popular form of Buddhist monastic interpretation. However, they are not simply renderings of the Pali, rather they are meant to provide various interpretive resources in a compact encyclopaedic style." [P:186–191]
- Anti-"mere translation" stance: "although nissaya is regarded as a sort of translation work, it would be difficult to categorize it as mere translation in the modern academic sense of the term because nissaya may offer more meanings in the monastic education system." [P:63–66]; "it is not categorized as 'translation' as generally understood because nissaya offers not only a definition but usually more than one meaning and caters to varied opinions within." [P:131–134]
- Linguistic angle (Okell): "John Okell (1965, 186–227) nissaya is a linguistic analysis with special emphasis on Pali grammar features in the Burmese language." [P:241–242]; Braun: "nissaya is a Burmese text that translates and explains Pali works, often on a systematic word-by-word basis" [P:292–295].
- Encyclopaedic thesis (stated twice): abstract — "this study argues that the Burmese nissaya literature can be compared to be on par with the encyclopaedic compilation on a specific subject." [P:18–20]; body — "I argue that nissaya literature is something more than translation and no less than an encyclopaedic source of knowledge available today." [P:139–141]; conclusion — "nissaya textbooks serve as compulsory handbooks or encyclopaedic reference… a pedagogical text… a guideline or support… a point of reference for textual analysis, criticism and interpretation." [P:690–693]; and "not only a tool of interpretation, but a resource of reference, a handbook for students, and a syntactical text, apart from merely a word-for-word translation or bitext as Walker (2020) puts it." [P:696–699]
- Function metaphors: "nissaya is a guidebook, a manual, a handbook or even a teacher to guide a student on a journey of exploration through Tipiṭaka." [P:496–498]; "traditional Burmese scholars… consider the textbooks of nissaya as either guidebooks, manuals or handbooks for students and teachers alike." [P:77–80]
- Relationship to official Tipiṭaka translation: "current Tipitaka Burmese translations were obviously adopted from old nissayas"; Clark (2015, 114): Chaṭṭhasaṅgāyanā translation "nearly identical to that of nissaya wording… merely reproductions or adoptions from a certain nissaya by simply removing the Pali words." [P:69–77]; block quote of Clark on Apadāna [P:322–328]; Pruitt: "only a slight adjustment is needed…" [P:329–333]; pāṭhasesa convention: added words appear in parentheses in Chaṭṭhasaṅgīti translation [P:334–336].
- Pedagogical sequencing: memorize first, then nissaya: "He memorises the Maṅgala-sutta in Pali first and then its nissaya, before learning their meaning… (Dhammasami 2004, 42)" [P:426–429]; status hierarchy: "Burmese pure translation is considered for ordinary and uneducated people as inferior texts. On the one hand, nissaya is considered a superior type of text especially for scholars and educated people" (Khamphorn in Pannabhoga 2020, 26) [P:434–440].
- Living-tradition reach: Triple-Gem formula glossed [P:415–419]; plays include nissaya expressions; dhamma-talks, Buddha-vandanā [P:420–424]; King Thalun (1629–1648) introduced curricular system/formal examination, nissaya works then Vinaya-heavy; Shin-kyint-wut novice-rules nissaya compiled "more than 30 times" [P:300–313].

Dhammasami, DPhil (Oxford), Rector of ssbu, Dr Aleix Ruiz-Falqués, Dr Pyi Phyo Kyaw (ssbu) and Dr Khamindra Phorn (Chiang Mai) for their thoughtful feedback… rearrangement of the subtopics and to correct some errors…" [P:708–714]. License: CC-BY 4.0 [P:27; Crossref license field].
- **Methodology (§2):** "this research employs various qualitative techniques, such as interviews with experts and students, targeting specific groups of study, sorting primary literature and secondly data and my personal observations in monastic classrooms. My own observation as an individual is unique and significant as I have been a student of monastic education and received training for almost two decades." [P:101–106]; childhood novice-training anecdote [P:107–118]; corpus scope: canonical Pali, Aṭṭhakathā, ṭīkā, Anuṭīkā "all of which are translated or interpreted using nissaya methods" [P:119–124]; aim statement [P:129–141].
- **Stated limitations:** (i) classroom chapter "will substantiate my research findings, yet with some limitations" (observation limited to "two leading monastic institutes in Mandalay and Pegu") [P:571–574]; (ii) dating uncertainty conceded: "almost impossible to state with certainty when the first Pali-Burmese nissaya was written… We can only assume that nissaya became popular from the 15th century… although we need further study." [P:248–250, 269–270]; (iii) closing: "Due to the study's limitations, much remains to be done in the study of nissaya across mainland Southeast Asia as well as in Sri Lanka." [P:706–707]. No explicit reflexivity on interviewer bias; single-author insider ethnography.
