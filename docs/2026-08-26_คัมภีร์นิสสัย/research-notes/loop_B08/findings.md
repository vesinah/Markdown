## Sub-topic 1 — Grierson, *Śivapariṇayah* edition: chāyā defined

**Item:** `sivaparinayahpoe00krsnuoft` — *Sivaparinayah; a poem in the Kashmiri language by Krsna Rajanaka, Razdan. With a Chaya or gloss in Sanskrit by Mahamahopadhyaya Mukundarama Sastri. Edited by George A. Grierson* (Calcutta: Asiatic Society, Bibliotheca Indica Work No. 224).
https://archive.org/details/sivaparinayahpoe00krsnuoft ; full text downloaded → `documents\sivaparinayahpoe00krsnuoft_djvu.txt`.

⚠️ Dating correction to Phase A: Grierson's Preface is signed "CAMBERLEY, 23rd June, 1924" (djvu.txt line 181). The work appeared in fasciculi (an earlier "Preliminary Note (Ad Interim)" was issued with the first fasciculus, line 68); loop_A11's "1913" should be treated as unconfirmed for the whole work.

**Quotable formal definition of chāyā** (Preliminary Note, djvu.txt lines 118–120; OCR renders chāyā as "chdyd"):

> "The Sanskrit **chāyā is an extremely literal, word for word, translation**, prepared under my supervision by Mahamahopadhyaya Mukundarama Sastri."

Grierson adds that he freely relaxed rules of Sanskrit syntax when editing it, "my sole aim being to assist the reader in disentangling the corresponding Kashmiri words from the suffixes with which they [are] encumbered" (lines 121–124, partly OCR-damaged).

Supporting title-page wording (OCR line 43): "...YA OR GLOSS IN SA[NSKRIT]" (= "with Chaya or Gloss in Sanskrit"). Preface records Mukundarama Śāstrī died 1921 while the work was passing through press (lines 144–146).

## Sub-topic 4 — Physical layout of the chāyā (codicological evidence)

**Grierson (primary, quotable)** — Preliminary Note, djvu.txt lines 125–129 (OCR gaps restored in brackets):

> "As originally prepared, the **chāyā was interlinear, each Sanskrit word being written under the corresponding Kashmiri word. Practical considerations of typography have led me, against the intention of its author, to print it separately on the page facing the corresponding** [page]."

So: (a) manuscript/original form = **interlinear word-under-word**; (b) print form = **facing pages**. This is the strongest explicit statement found that chāyā practice was laid out line-under-line/word-under-word.

**Dohākośa (Sāṅkṛtyāyana ed.)**: printed arrangement shows Apabhraṃśa dohā blocks headed "(हिन्दी छाया-सहित)" with footnotes below (djvu.txt lines 3994–4028); djvu.txt cannot preserve whether Hindi chāyā sat line-under-line within each verse — page-image inspection would be needed. ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน for interlinear vs block layout in this print.

Comparative SEA parallel (for chaining): Trent Walker, "Indic-Vernacular Bitexts from Thailand", *JAOS* 140.3 (2020): bitexts "arranged in both interlinear and interphrasal formats"; some Siamese palm-leaf Kaccāyanavyākaraṇa copies have vernacular annotations "typically carved directly into the leaves instead of inked on the surface" (interlinear inscribed). PDF: https://lockwoodonlinejournals.com/index.php/jaos/article/view/679/532

## Sub-topic 6 — Nalini Balbir / cūrṇi language-mixing technique

Balbir's *Āvaśyaka-Studien* (1993, Franz Steiner, ANIS 45; ISBN 9783515061490; CiNii BA23487076) own wording still not directly quotable from accessible sources: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน. But rich quotable material exists elsewhere, including from **Balbir herself**:

- **Balbir 2025**: "Multi-Sided Analysis in Early Śvetāmbara Jain Exegesis: The Niryukti-Discourse", *Journal of Indian Philosophy*, publ. 2025-08-01, DOI 10.1007/s10781-025-09613-y. Quotes the Anuyogadvāra-cūrṇi definition of nirukta: "'Etymology [is] syllables of a designation being uttered in a way as to get a determined meaning' (*abhidhāṇakkharā ṇicchitatthovaladdhippagāreṇa uccarijjamāṇā ṇiruttaṃ*, Jambūvijaya 1999, p. 341)"; and observes of the explanatory sentences: "**they are all in Sanskrit, and none of the commentaries seems to note this fact**" — explicit code-switching observation. Also (same article) niryukti designation: root YUJ- "connection".
- **Ueda Masahiro 2017**: "Study on the Exegetical Literature of Śvetāmbara Jainas", *IBK* 65.3, DOI 10.4259/ibk.65.3_1130: layers "(1) Niryukti (around the 1st century CE), (2) Bhāṣya (500–700 CE), (3) **Cūrṇi (600–700 CE)**, (4) Ṭīkā (or Vivaraṇa; after 800 CE)". Companion Japanese abstract (IBK 69.2, 2021, J-STAGE): "**The Cūrṇi … is a mixture of Prakrit and Sanskrit prose.**"
- **Dundas** ("The Later Fortunes of Jamāli", DOI 10.4324/9780203008539-3): on Jinadāsa's 7th-c. Āvaśyaka-Cūrṇi: "**the text switches from Prakrit to Sanskrit** to show how the orthodox teaching of *calemajje calitaṃ*… is correct."
- **Leiden study** (item 3640057): "the Prakrit prose commentary (**sporadically interspersed with Sanskrit**) called in Sanskrit cūrṇi (the equivalent of Prakrit cuṇṇi)… traditionally ascribed to Jinadāsagaṇin"; cūrṇi etymology from √cūrṇ "grinding"/"flour" of explication; pratīka (catchword) system as student-orientation markers.

## Sub-topic 7 — Canonical locus of the nine-aṅga list (SuttaCentral verification)

Method: SuttaCentral bilara API `https://suttacentral.net/api/bilarasuttas/{uid}/html?lang=pli` (saved: `documents\sc_bilara_mn22_root-pli.json`, `documents\sc_bilara_an5.33_root-pli.json`).

- **MN 22 (Alagaddūpamasutta): POSITIVE.** Root Pali segments `mn22:10.2`, `mn22:10.18`, `mn22:11.2`, `mn22:11.18` read exactly:
  > "**suttaṁ, geyyaṁ, veyyākaraṇaṁ, gāthaṁ, udānaṁ, itivuttakaṁ, jātakaṁ, abbhutadhammaṁ, vedallaṁ.**"

## Sub-topic 9 — K.R. Norman, *Pali Literature* (1983), nine aṅgas discussion

Full text public on archive.org (item `PaliLiterature`) → downloaded `documents\norman_pali_literature_1983_djvu.txt`. Key passage (djvu.txt lines 1291–1339) with footnotes:

> "Except for two references to the five nikayas in later passages in the Vinaya-pitaka,[2] the usual classification of the canon found in canonical texts is that of nine angas, viz. suttaṃ geyyaṃ veyyākaraṇaṃ gāthaṃ udānaṃ itivuttakaṃ jātakaṃ abbhutadhammaṃ vedallaṃ.[3]"
> fn3: "**E.g. M I 133, 24–25.**"  fn4: "**Ps II 106, 8–28 = Sp 28, 8–29, 3.**"

> "It is clear that all Buddhaghosa knew about some of these terms was the fact that there were in his time a number of texts which happened to have the same name as an anga… it is probable that the list of nine angas did not originally refer to specific works in the canon, but was a description of various types of text."

veyyākaraṇa semantics (fn6, lines 1386–87): "**So veyyākaraṇa is commonly used of an exposition in a sermon, e.g. veyyākaranasmiṃ bhaññamāne (D I 46, 27–28)**." Also fn8: vedalla ↔ Skt vaipulya/vetulla discussion; Dipavamsa account of ninefold→nikāya reclassification (lines 1370–1375).

## Sub-topic 10 — India→SEA transmission claims (chāyā/cūrṇi/anvaya → nissaya)

**Verdict: no explicit scholarly claim found stating that the Indian chāyā technique was transmitted to Southeast Asian nissaya composition.** Direct claim: ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน. Strong adjacent evidence collected:

- **Terminological echo (best lead)** — Trent Walker, JAOS 140.3 (2020), p. 676 fn1: local bitext names include "**cuṇṇiyapad** (Siamese)" [= Thai จุลนิยบาท < Skt **cūrṇi**!] and "**prè lot prayog** (Khmer)" [prayoga/anvaya family]; also nissaya (Burmese), naṃ/trā-ai (Mon), nisrai/nissăy (Lanna/Lao/Siamese), nāmaśăbd (Lanna/Siamese). URL above; DOI 10.7817/jameroriesoci.140.3.0675.
- **Walker's comparative frame** deliberately pairs SEA techniques with Latin and Literary-Sinitic reading traditions ("parallel to approaches for reading Latin in medieval Europe and Literary Sinitic in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam") rather than Indian chāyā — notable silence.
- **India-origin chronology**: same article — earliest mainland SEA bitext = Pali-Mon inscription late 11th/early 12th c.; Pali-Burmese from late 13th c.; "several **Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts are listed in an inscription from 1442**"; oldest complete Pali-Siamese bitext 1482 Ayutthaya. Walker 2022 "Bilingualism" chapter (DOI 10.4324/9781351026666-22): three steps "selection, analysis, and presentation". New: Sripum & Walker, *Manuscript Studies* 11:1 (2026), DOI 10.1353/mns.2026.a990231 (incl. **Sanskrit-Siamese** Supritithammarachachadok kham luang).
- **Pyu-era Sanskrit gloss claim**: Moussons 26 (Brill, 2023) "Burmese Nissaya Literature…" relays Tin Lwin: a 7th-c. excavation at Srikṣetra yielded "a **Pyu nissaya, consisting of an interpretation of a Sanskrit passage**" — the closest thing to a Sanskrit-shadow→nissaya transmission claim. https://brill.com/view/journals/mnya/26/1/article-p1_004.xml
- Genre framing: McDaniel 2002 (DOI 10.1163/26659077-00504003): "The nissaya genre is unique to Laos, Burma and Northern Thailand"; Okell 1965 *Lingua* 15:186–227 "Nissaya Burmese"; Ruiz-Falqués PhD 2015 (SOAS): Pagan-era commentaries "usually composed in the form of nissaya", but no grammatical nissayas attested from Pagan period.
