## Sub-topic 2 — The nissaya catalog (pp. 106–109): COMPLETE LIST

**CRITICAL CONTEXTUAL CORRECTION:** The numbered catalog at pp. 102–109 of Bode is NOT a freestanding bibliography of Burmese nissayas; it is **the continuation of the list of texts donated to the Sangha in a dated INSCRIPTION of A.D. 1442** (B.E. 804), copied by Bode as "APPENDIX TO CHAPTER III / AN INSCRIPTION OF A.D. 1442". Framing quote:

> "The inscription mentioned on p. 50 is among those collected by Forchhammer at Pagan. It is dated B.E. 804 (1442 A.D.), and commemorates the bestowal of various gifts on the Order by the Governor of Taungdwin and his wife. Together with a monastery, garden, paddy-lands, and slaves, the pious donors offered a collection of texts, of which a list is given." ([PLB] lines 5283–5288, printed p.101)

> "Besides helping to fix the chronology of many Pali works and giving some indication of their importance, it gives us another clue well worth following up." ([PLB] lines 5290–5293)

> "References to Forchhammer's List indicate that MS. copies of the works in question are in the Bernard Free Library at Rangoon." ([PLB] lines 5312–5329, printed p.102)

## Sub-topic 3 — Sagaing monasteries producing Burmese nissayas, seventeenth century

Verbatim ([PLB] lines 3090–3137, printed pp.54–55):

> "The theras of Sagaing at this period seem to have taken the lead in Abhidhamma studies. One of these, the thera Tilokaguru, toiled for many years at tlkas [ṭīkās] and supplementary tikas (anutlka) on various texts. After dealing very thoroughly with the Dhatukatha he composed a tika on the Yamaka. But his great feat was a tika on the Patthana, the most important book of the Abhidhammapitaka.
>
> Tilokagaru [sic] is but one example. **The Sagaing monasteries also produced a number of Burmese nissayas (interpretations or paraphrases) on Abhidhamma texts during the seventeenth century.** But it is not easy to distribute these works aright among their several authors, whose Pali names are but an indifferent help to accuracy. There were at least four Ariyalamkara's noted for scholarship. The monk mentioned in the Sasanavamsa as the second Ariyalamkara (pupil of the great thera who was 'equal to Tipitakalamkara') is probably the scholar of whom Oldenberg remarks that the Burmese are indebted to him for the version of a great number of Pali works. Those ascribed to this Ariyalamkara are : (1) Interpretations of the AtthasalinI of Buddhaghosa, the Sankhepavannanii of Saddhammajotipala, the AbhidhammatthavibhavanI of Sumangala, and the Vibhanga of the Abhidhammapitaka. (2) A Pali tlka on the Kaccayanabheda, entitled Saratthavikasinl. (3) Ariyalarpkara [sic] was careful to add a Burmese version to what we should nowadays call his revised edition of Kaccayana's grammar."

Place detail ([PLB] lines 3139–3151):
> "'This work was done mostly in the Dakkhinavana vihdra, or Monastery of the Southern Grove, near the Rajamanicula cetiya at Sagaing. Ukkamsika had built four monasteries, one on each side of his famous pagoda, and presented them to theras learned in the sacred texts. Another grammarian in residence on the west side produced an edition of the Nyasa, 'adorned' (as the Pali phrase goes), and set forth with various methods of explanation. The Nyasa was taken up again in the reign of Sirinandadhammaraja-Pavaradhipatiraja (A.D. 1648) by the king's preceptor, Dathanaga of Sagaing. His commentary is entitled Niruttisaramafi j usa [Niruttisaramañjūsā]."

Contemporary vernacular translators of the same milieu ([PLB] lines 3192–3222, printed pp.56–57):
> "he [Jambudhaja] devoted himself to the Vinaya, of which he translated text and commentary into Burmese. But Maniratana, a writer of the same period, is an example of a life spent in interpreting the abstruser side of sacred learning to those who were only capable of reading the vernacular. The Sasanavamsa mentions translations by him of the following works — the AtthasalinI and SammohavinodanI (Buddhaghosa's commentaries on the Dhammasangani and the Vibhahga) and the KankhavitaranI (Buddhaghosa's commentary on the Patimokkha of the Vinaya); this last seems to have been Maniratana's only departure from metaphysical studies. Then, turning to the later exponents of the Abhidhamma, he translated the tlkas Abhidhammattha-vibhavanI and Sankhepavannana into Burmese."
> "Another thera, Saradassi, of the same place (Nayyinyua, in the Ava district)... He also translated the Nettipakarana into Burmese, not to shirk his part in opening up the Pali texts to readers without learning."

And ([PLB] lines 3241–3257, printed p.57):
> "When Mahapavaradhammarajalokadhipati came to the throne in 1651 he built some monasteries, according to the custom of his predecessors, and presented them to distinguished theras. The most eminent among these was the Aggadhammalamkara, already mentioned, who translated several Pali texts into the vernacular. He first paid the usual homage to Kaccayana, but by writing a Burmese translation instead of a Pali commentary; afterwards he translated the Abhidhammatthasangaha, and then, as if continually seeking heavier and heavier tasks, the patient scholar toiled through translations of the Matika (of the DhammasanganI), the Dhatukatha, the Yamaka, and the Patthana."

## Sub-topic 4 — Abhidhammatthasangaha: twenty-three Burmese nissayas per Pitakatthamain

Verbatim ([PLB] lines 3437–3483, printed pp.61–62):

> "The greater number of Burmese students of metaphysics have depended from early times on compendiums and manuals. The most successful of these, after the canonical Dhammasanganl, has been the twelfth-century textbook Abhidhammatthasangaha. This little treatise is a summary of Buddhist theories on mental processes, on existence and annihilation. It is a mass of technical terms needing an extensive commentary. Commentators, of course, were forthcoming. The Sinhalese theras Vimalabuddhi and Sumangala both composed tlkas, and the second, the AbhidhammatthavibhavanT, is part of the usual course of (Abhidhamma) study in Burma. Two ancient and authoritative treatises from Ceylon, the Abhidhamma vatara by Buddhadatta and the Saccasamkhepa by Culladhammapila, were studied more in the early period of Burmese scholarship than in later times.
>
> Probably **no Pali work on the Abhidhamma has been more often translated and paraphrased than the Abhidhammatthasangaha, of which the Pitakatthamain alone mentions twenty-three different Burmese nissayas.** All the most noted theras of the seventeenth century took it in hand, and it has been carefully edited by modern hands. A close analysis of the principal translations of this single little text would be an explanation, incomplete of course, but very interesting and instructive, of the true Burmese view of Abhidhamma theories..."

Footnotes attached ([PLB] lines 3458–3464): Dhammasangani "Edited iu JPTS., 1884"; Abhidhammatthasangaha "Edited by E. Müller, trans, by C. A. F. Rhys Davids"; VibhavanT references to Fausböll Cat. Mand. MSS. pp. 38, 48; Forchhammer List p. xvii; Gandhavamsa pp. 62, 72.

Seventeenth-century vernacular renderings of the Saṅgaha named elsewhere by Bode: Aggadhammalamkara "translated the Abhidhammatthasangaha" ([PLB] line 3250); Nanavara "prepared a ganthipadattha or gloss on the difficult words in that famous work" ([PLB] lines 3695–3698, p.67); modern: Aggadhammalamkara's own "Abhidhammatthasamgahagandhassa atthayojanam pi Marammabhasaya akasi" [SAS line 9446–47]; plus modern printed works listed pp.95–96 (U Tin's Sarupatthadlpani Maulmein 1883; Abhidhammatthasangahaparitta by Maung Tun Aung 1897; Abhidhammatthasangahaganthi-thit 1898 by U Tissa and U Janinda) ([PLB] lines 5059–5066).

## Sub-topic 5 — Ariyavamsa: first vernacular atthayojana author

Verbatim ([PLB] lines 2568–2608, printed pp.43–44):

> "These works were composed, according to the old scholarly tradition, in Pali ; but **Ariyavamsa was a teacher not content to write only for the learned. He stands out in the Sasanavamsa's record of literary theras as the first name connected with a metaphysical work in the vernacular. He composed an atthayojana or interpretation in Burmese of a commentary called the Anutlka on the Abhidhamma.** The Gandhavamsa attributes another work entitled Mahanissara to Ariyavamsa, but there is no mention of it in the Sasanavamsa."

Footnote 2 ([PLB] lines 2602–2604): "The work generally known as the Anutika was written by Dhammapala to supplement the original tika (of Ananda) on the Abhidhamma. See the Sasanavamsa's list of commentaries composed in Ceylon (Sas., p. 33)."

Footnote 3 ([PLB] lines 2606–2608): "I am not sure that this word should not be **Mahanissaya (chief commentary or gloss in Burmese)**; the work would probably be the atthayojana, of which mention has been made above."

Dating context: Ariyavamsa flourished at Ava under king Dutiya-min-khaung (Sirisudhammarajadhipati, began reign A.D. 1470; Sas. p. 98, "the year 842 of the Kaliyuga") — footnote at [PLB] lines 2610–2612. So per Bode: **Ariyavamsa, at Ava, c. 1470s–1490s, atthayojana (Burmese interpretation) on the Anuṭīkā of the Abhidhamma; first vernacular metaphysical author in the Sasanavamsa's record.**

Pali source confirmation [SAS] lines 7972–7976 (PTS p.98):
> "So ca Ariyavamsathero Manidipara iiama gandham Gandliabharanafi ca Jatakavisodhanaii ca palibhasaya akiisi.
> **Anutikaya pana attliayojanam Marammabhasaya akasi.**"
[OCR: "The thera Ariyavamsa composed the work called Manidipa, and the Gandhabharana and Jatakavisodhana in Pali. And he made an atthayojana (interpretation) of the Anutika in the Burmese language."]

Also relevant, Silavamsa immediately after ([PLB] lines 2590–2596, p.43): "He, like Ariyavamsa, laboured to spread religious learning by interpreting Pali texts in the vernacular. A Burmese atthayojana of the Nettipakarana, and another edifying work, the Parayanavatthu..." — Pali source [SAS] lines 8028–8029: "...bhasaya akasi Parayanavatthim ca."

Index entry [PLB] line 6452: "atthayojana (interpretation), 43."

## Sub-topic 6 — Saddhammasiri using Burmese as literary instrument

Verbatim ([PLB] lines 1444–1458, printed p.20):

> "In the work of Saddhammasiri, the author of the grammatical treatise Saddatthabhedacinta, we catch a glimpse of a culture that recalls Aggavamsa. Saddhammasiri's grammar is based partly on Kaccayana's Pali aphorisms and partly on Sanskrit authorities. **The Sasanavamsa tells us that Saddhammasiri also translated the Brihaja (?) into the Burmese language. He was probably among the first to use Burmese as a literary instrument.** If the work mentioned is the astrological Brlhaj jataka it could not have put a great strain on the resources of the Burmese idiom (even before the immense body of Pali words added later had come to the aid of the vernacular), so the feat was not a surprising one."

Footnote 4 quotes the Pali ([PLB] lines 1470–1471): "Sas., p. 75 : 'So yeva thera Brihajam nama Vedasattham pi maramma-bhasaya parivattesi.' Cf. Pitakatthamain, p. 68."

Footnote 5 ([PLB] lines 1473–1478) cites Duroiselle on Sanskrit-derived words in 10th–11th c. Burmese inscriptions, and the belief that Sanskrit was known in Burma before Pali, which shortly after importation from Thaton "n'etait connu que de l'elite des moines" (Bulletin, tome v, p. 154).

Dating: Forchhammer places Saddhammasiri and Abhaya in the reign of... ([PLB] line 1566, cut off in OCR; both are Pagan-period grammarians, 12th c. context of Narapatisithu's reign discussion, pp.19–21).

Pali source confirmation [SAS] lines 6862–6866:
> "Saddatthabhedacintam pana Arimaddananagarasamipe thitassa Khanitthipadapabbatassa samipe ekasmim game vasanto Saddhammasiri nama thero akasi. So yeva thero Brihajani nama vedasatthain pi Marammabhasaya parivattesi."
[Saddhammasiri, living in a village near Khanitthipada mountain near Pagan, composed the Saddatthabhedacinta; the same thera also rendered the Veda-work called Brihaj into Burmese.]

Legacy chain ([PLB] lines 1560–1564): "From the Saddatthabhedacinta of Saddhammasiri sprang a number of commentaries of which the best known is the Mahatika by the thera Abhaya of Pagan." — and note the 1442 inscription contains a "Saddatthabhedacintanissaya" (p.109), i.e., a Burmese nissaya on Saddhammasiri's own grammar existed by 1442.

## Sub-topic 7 — Royal translation commissions (Bodawpaya / Mindon)

*(OCR renders Bodawpaya as "Bodopaya", Mindon as "Min-don-min"/"Miii-ddn-min".)*

- Nana-bhivamsa (Sangharaja) "At the request of the king he undertook a Burmese translation of the Pali commentary on the Jataka (the Jatakatthakatha)." ([PLB] lines 4260–4262, printed p.78)

**Mindon (Min-don-min, r. 1852–1877)** ([PLB] lines 4891–4946, printed pp.92–93):
> "Pannasami succeeded his master as Sangharaja in the following reign. It is rather curious that not a single Pali composition is mentioned by him as belonging to this decade. His colleagues were nevertheless very active, especially in translating from the Pali. **The Anguttara-, Samyutta-, and Dighanikayas were translated with their commentaries. The authors of these translations were respectively Paiinajota-bhidhaja, Manijotasaddhammalamkara, and Medhabhivamsa.**"
> "This was Min-ddn-min, whose reign, lasting from 1852 to 1877, was a period of peace, good government, and general content... The king's command and example, as of old, were all-powerful..."
> "The Sarigharaja Xeyyadhamma composed 'at the king's request' a work entitled Surajamaggadlpanl. Meanwhile the old traditional learning was not neglected. **The Sangharaja had been expounding the commentary on the Majjhimanikaya to his pupils. Under his direction a Burmese translation of the commentary was prepared, embodying his interpretation of the text.** The original text of the Jataka tales was also translated at this time by the thera Medhabhivamsa."

Earlier in the same decade-chain ([PLB] lines 4860–4871, printed p.91): Neyyadhammabhivamsa "mentioned as the author of a Burmese translation of the SaddhammavilasinI, the commentary on Patisambhidamagga"; his pupil Pannasami's first work "was a translation into Burmese of a commentary on difficult passages (Ganthipadatthavannana) of the venerable grammatical work Saddatthabhedacinta. By the royal order a complete text of the Tripitaka was then engraved on stone tablets..."

Medhabhivamsa made the atthayojana-naya of the Jataka Pali in Burmese.]

## Sub-topic 8 — HOW nissayas were composed / used pedagogically (formal descriptions in Bode)

(a) **Definition given by Bode**: "Burmese nissayas (**interpretations or paraphrases**) on Abhidhamma texts" ([PLB] line 3099). Index: "atthayojana (interpretation), 43" ([PLB] line 6452). Conjectural definition: "Mahanissaya (**chief commentary or gloss in Burmese**)" ([PLB] line 2606–07).

(b) **Methodological key-passage on the genre's value** ([PLB] lines 386–393, printed p.xiii):
> "When we leave Pali and come to Burmese interpretations of Abhidhamma texts we feel that we follow our commentators in a (to us) somewhat dark adventure; but yet **the effort of these authors in their own vernacular is interesting as an effort to do more than recite the consecrated words of their ancient masters. Here is the key to the sense really given by the Burmese to Indian abstractions**, the key not to be found where they use the exotic and traditional Pali form."

(c) **Ubiquity in manuscript culture** ([PLB] lines 3312–3322, printed pp.58–59):
> "We have seen how the several books of the Abhidhamma were interpreted and paraphrased in Burmese during the seventeenth century, and we cannot doubt that the disciples living near their venerable masters in the monasteries by the Irrawaddy persevered in studying the third Pitaka. And that tradition of the seventeenth century has come down to later generations, as the most casual survey of modern Burmese literature will show. **Everyone who has seen a collection of Buddhist manuscripts from Burma must have noticed the numerous copies of Abhidhamma texts with vernacular interpretations.**"

(d) **Pedagogical scenes**:
- Devacakkobhasa "made his pupils study and recite the Patthana (we suppose in Pali)" ([PLB] lines 3290–3292, p.57).
- Silavamsa, established in a royal vihara, "there he lectured on the sacred texts. He, like Ariyavamsa, laboured to spread religious learning by interpreting Pali texts in the vernacular" ([PLB] lines 2590–2593, p.43).
- Sangharaja expounding Majjhimanikaya commentary to pupils; translation prepared "embodying his interpretation of the text" ([PLB] lines 4940–4943, p.93); Pali: "attham sissanam vacetva yatha vacitaniyamena atthayojananayam potthake aropapesi" [SAS 10860–62] — i.e., oral exposition transcribed into book form: strong evidence that nissaya/atthayojana books arose from classroom recitation.
- Motive formula repeated: vernacular rendering "to readers without learning" ([PLB] line 3215–16); "interpreting the abstruser side of sacred learning to those who were only capable of reading the vernacular" ([PLB] lines 3196–98).

(e) **Linguistic framing** ([PLB] lines 212–216, p.ix): "the psychological subtleties, and high thoughts of Buddhism have forced the Burmese language to grow, deepen, and expand continually. When Burmese was at last raised (in or about the fourteenth century) to the level of a literary language, it was by the addition of a great body of Indian words necessary to express ideas beyond the scope of that picturesque vernacular."

(f) **Vernacular religious literature growing around older texts** ([PLB] lines 2829–2837, p.48): "From the time when the Shan rule was finally demolished by Burmese kings, more favourable days began for religion. The seventeenth century saw some further changes...; **a religious literature in the vernacular, in the Burmese language itself, grew up round the older texts**, but the earlier traditions of Pali scholarship always found faithful followers."

(g) **Modern-era practice** ([PLB] lines 5041–5046, p.95): Alahkaranissaya "is an example of that care to preserve the old traditions of scholarship... The Alankaranissaya is an edition of Sarigharakkhita's Subodhalankara, with a commentary."; Chandomanjari, "a Pali treatise on metre, followed by a nissaya in Burmese (1897)" ([PLB] 5149–5150, p.97); Ledi Hsaya-daw: "Niruttidlpanl (a Pali grammar, and afterwards a Burmese nissaya on the same work)" ([PLB] 5160–61, pp.97–98).

**Negative finding:** Bode gives NO technical/formal description of nissaya word-by-word layout (no mention of interspersed-word method, path-notation, or classroom drilling technique as such). Her terms are consistently "interpretation(s)", "paraphrase(s)", "version", "translation". Formal descriptions must be sought in Okell 1965, Pruitt 1992, and Herngseng 2023 (see sub-topic 10).

## Sub-topic 9 — Local Sasanavamsa (PTS 1897 Pali text) mining: nissaya / vernacular exposition

Grep results: 26 hits for nissaya-variants. Most are Pali "nissaya" = reliance/support ("rajanam nissaya" = supported by the king; "nissayacariya" = dependence-teacher relation). Vernacular-exposition-relevant findings:

**(a) Bode's English introduction (embedded, "A Burmese Historian of Buddhism", [SAS] lines 2885–2902, printed p.56 of prelims):**
> "according to the orthodox, scriptural warrant is everything, in the settlement of religious difficulties. From the word of the ancient texts, expanded in the Atthakatha and further explained by Ukas and atthayojanas there is no appeal."
— Early explicit recognition of atthayojana as a recognized exegetical layer (Atthakatha → uka/ṭīkā → atthayojana).

**(b) Pannasami's own justification of language choice ([SAS] lines 3151–3162, metrical preface):**
> "Sihaladlpato yeva agatehi disantaram Bhikkhilhi jacito kassam Sasanavamsappadipikam / Kaman ca poranehi ya Sasanavamsappadipika Vittharavacana magga viracita vinicchaya / **Sa pana Mramma-bhasaya katatta yeva etesam Dipantaranivasinam va hati sutthuu[ttham]** / Tasma hi mulabhasaya karissami aham have / Samsanditvana gandhehi tarn sallakkhentu sadhavo ti."
[OCR reading: whereas former Sasanavamsa-appadīpika works were composed in Mramma-bhāsā (Burmese), which is difficult for residents of other lands, "therefore I shall compose it in the mūla-bhāsā (root/original language = Pali), so that the devout may examine it thoroughly together with the texts."] — Inverse evidence: by the 19th c., Burmese was the DEFAULT chronicle language, and choosing Pali required justification.

**(c) Named Burmese-language compositions (Marammabhasaya / mulabhasaya) in the Pali text:**
- Saddhammasiri: "Brihajani nama vedasatthain pi Marammabhasaya parivattesi" ([SAS] 6865–6866; cf. PLB p.20).
- Ariyavamsa: "Anutikaya pana attliayojanam Marammabhasaya akasi" ([SAS] 7976, PTS p.98).
- Silavamsa: "...bhasaya akasi Parayanavatthim ca" ([SAS] 8029).
- Jambudhaja: "Vinayapaliya atthakathaya ca atthayojanam Marammabhasaya akasi" ([SAS] 8902–03).
- Maniratana: "Atthasrdini-Sammohavinodani-Kankhavitaranl-attliakathanam Abhidhammatthavibhavani-Sainkhepavanna-na-tikanau ca attham Marammabhasaya yojesi" ([SAS] 8903–06).
- Saradassi: "Giilhatthadlpanim nama gandham Visnddhimaggaganthipadatthah ca mulabhasaya akasi. Nettipaliya ca attham Maramma-bhasaya yojesi" ([SAS] 8908–11).
- Nanathera (Nanalankara): "Padavibhagagandhara Nyasasarnvannanam Yamakasamvannanam Mahapatthanasamvannanam ca Marammabhasaya akasi" ([SAS] 9488–90) — as a newly ordained youth, i.e. Ava period.
- Aggadhammalamkara: "Abhidhammatthasamgahagandhassa atthayojanam pi Ma-rammabhasaya akasi" ([SAS] 9446–47, before PTS p.127).
- Mindon-era: Majjhimanikaya-atthakatha atthayojananaya books ([SAS] 10860–62); Medhabhivamsa "Jatakapaliya atthayojananayam Marammabhasaya akasi" ([SAS] 10865–66); Pannasami "Saddanltiya samvannanam palibhasaya akasin ti" ([SAS] 10887–88).

**(d) Monastic-dependence (nissaya) technical usage** — [SAS] lines 9842–9846 (PTS p.134), Bodawpaya-era sangha reform:
> "Atba araba pi samano nissayamuccakangavikakj vina nissayacariyena vasitum na vattati ti janitva nissayacari-yapabonakanam tberanam nissayangani nissayamuccaka-rabaiiam nissayamuccakangani paripurapetva nissitakanam nissayam ganbitva va nisidapesi."
[A novice who has not completed the nissaya-muccaka-ṅgavika (release-from-dependence duties) ought not to dwell without a dependence-teacher (nissayacariya); the king seated those released from dependence having fulfilled the groups of dependence duties, or having taken a dependence(-teacher).] — shows nissaya as canonical Vinaya dependence-term active in royal legislation; distinct from the literary-genre sense.

**(e) Ren-nat-ca-ka-ro-da place-name** ([SAS] 8541–8542): "gamam nissaya Marammavoharena Ren-nat-ca-ka-ro-da ti samaniia ahosi" — village name given "in Burmese usage" (nissaya here = "near/by"); negative for literary genre.

**(f) Pedagogical anecdote of lay exegesis** ([SAS] 7041–7054): father commands son and daughters each: "Imassa atthayojanam karohi ti" ("Make an atthayojana of this!") — riddle-sentence interpretation contest in Pagan (Arimaddana); shows atthayojana as a household exercise of textual interpretation.

**(g) Negative finding:** The Sasanavamsa does NOT use the word "nissaya" for the vernacular literary genre; it uses **atthayojana / atthayojana-naya / parivatteti / yojeti / karoti with Marammabhasaya/mulabhasaya**. Bode's "nissaya" terminology derives from the Pitakatthamain and manuscript labels, not from Pannasami's vocabulary.
