## Verified facts

- Title: *The English Hexapla exhibiting the Six Important English Translations of the New Testament Scriptures Wiclif M.CCC.LXXX, Tyndale M.D.XXXIV, Cranmer M.D.XXXIX, Genevan M.D.LVII, Anglo-Rhemish M.D.LXXXII, Authorised M.DC.XI*.
- Publisher: Samuel Bagster and Sons, Paternoster Row, London; first edition 1841; first edition printed by Wertheimer and Co.; first edition contains full 160-page preliminary "Historical Account of the English Translations" by S.P. Tregelles (later editions have shorter histories, possibly by John Stoughton).
- Layout (HathiTrust): Greek text at top of page, English versions below, arranged in six parallel columns spread across two pages. (Internet Bible Catalog: each double page contains the Greek text at the head and six translations in parallel columns underneath.)
- The six English versions are reprints with original spelling but NOT facsimiles.
- Greek base text: after M.A. Scholz (compiled 1830-1836), itself based largely on Griesbach's editions (1775-1805); includes variant readings of the Textus Receptus and principal manuscripts (per archive.org title-page transcription).
- Text exemplars used: Wycliffe from a manuscript of the Duke of Sussex; Tyndale 1534 and Great Bible 1539 from the Baptist College, Bristol; Geneva NT from 1557 first edition; Rheims from 1582 original; KJV from a black-letter 1611 copy supplied by Rev. J.H.M. Luxmoore.

### Dealer discrepancy noted

Moons Rare Books listing substitutes "Bishops' Bible (1568)" for one column; this contradicts all bibliographic sources above (title pages list Anglo-Rhemish 1582). Treat as dealer error.

## Significance for project

Monoglot comparative layout: one original language (Greek) on top, six vernacular versions beneath in parallel columns - inverts the polyglot hierarchy (originals + lingua-franca glosses) into original + six same-language historical versions. Explicitly named after Origen's Hexapla ("hexapla = six-fold/six-columned"); Bagster was known for multilingual scripture projects. Demonstrates that the "originals preserved at top, derivatives below" strategy persisted into Victorian publishing even when all columns share one language.
