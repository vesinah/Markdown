## Per-source notes

### Blom 2017, Glossing the Psalms
The target verification item from the task brief: the author name recorded from the De Gruyter Brill front matter/snippets is **Alderik H. Blom** (affiliation given as University of Oxford). The book studies thirteen glossed manuscripts including the Vespasian Psalter and the Milan Glosses, comparing Latin with Old Irish, Old English, Old Frisian, Old Saxon and Old High German across the seventh to twelfth centuries. It examines when and why scribes switched into the vernacular, how vernacular glosses served Latin study, and how glosses interact with construe marks and punctuation. Direct page fetch failed (empty output), so status remains SEARCH-VERIFIED despite the author being confirmed from degruyterbrill.com-hosted content.

### Kuhn 1959 (PMLA)
Kuhn frames the central problem for the Vespasian gloss: is it a ninth-century original or a ninth-century copy of an earlier (? eighth-century) gloss, in BL Cotton Vespasian A.i, generally regarded as Mercian. He argues for an early ninth-century date of the gloss hand ("A"), supported by his earlier Speculum xviii (1943) study of charter hands and palaeographical resemblance to the Lorica Prayer written by or for Bishop Aelwald of Lichfield (A.D. 818-830). Footnote 17 records that the Junius Psalter gloss was directly copied from the Vespasian gloss. The article was later challenged/extended by Gerritsen (1989, English Studies 70) and Pulsiano (1996, Anglo-Saxon England 25).

### Kuhn ed. 1965 (Vespasian Psalter edition)
The standard edition of BL Cotton Vespasian A.i: Roman-Psalter Latin text with the Mercian Old English interlinear translation, transcribed from MLAA rotograph no. 332 of the British Museum manuscript. Published Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 1965; xii + 327 pages; associated name Sherman M. Kuhn (1907-1991). Includes glossaries and indexes; borrow-only scan at archive.org (LCCN 63009901).

### Fernandez Cuesta & Pons-Sanz eds. 2016
De Gruyter Anglia Book Series 51 collection devoted to Aldred's interlinear gloss in BL Cotton Nero D.IV, described as one of the most substantial representatives of late Old Northumbrian. Coverage includes authorship, morphosyntax, vocabulary, sources and relationship to the Rushworth Gospels, and Aldred's cultural and religious affiliations. Chapters seen include Pons-Sanz on Aldred's multiple glosses (pp. 301-328), Michelle P. Brown on "A good woman's Son" (pp. 13-36), and Jane Roberts on "Aldred: Glossator and book historian" (pp. 37-60).

### Doyle, Wuerzburg Irish Glosses database
Codex Paulinus Wirziburgensis contains the Latin Pauline Epistles with marginal and interlinear glosses added in three distinguishable scribal hands, dated about the middle of the eighth century - among the earliest large bodies of continuous Irish text. The database presents 3,501 glosses with Irish content based on Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus vol. 1 (Stokes & Strachan 1901); further glosses were apparently lost through age and binding. The site's bibliography anchors the scholarly chain: Zimmer 1881; Bergin 1928; Breen 1996 (biblical text/sources, Romans 1-6); Kavanagh & Wodtko 2001 lexicon; Bisagni 2013 on code-switching (Peritia 24-25); de Paor 2016 new edition (Herder); O Neill 2002 on the Prima Manus hand. Related outputs seen: Zenodo dataset 5508250 and the UD_Old_Irish-DipWBG treebank (Adrian Doyle, NUIG).

### Hofman/Moran/Bauer, St Gall Priscian Glosses v2.1
St Gall, Stiftsbibliothek MS 904 was written AD 850-1, probably in Ireland, brought to the Continent between 855 and 863, and reached St Gall sometime after 888. It carries over 9,400 interlinear and marginal glosses plus roughly 3,000-4,000 construe marks, more than one-third of the verbal glosses in Old Irish - a core corpus of the Irish language. The digital edition v2.1 (2023) by Hofman, Moran and Bauer is the first complete transcription of all glosses, juxtaposed with Hertz's text of Priscian (Keil, Grammatici Latini vols 2-3); superseding Thesaurus Palaeohibernicus vol. 2, pp. 49-224 (Old Irish part) and Hofman's Sankt Gall Priscian Commentary Part 1 (Münster: Nodus, 1996, first half of glosses). The manuscript breaks off in Book 17; Book 18 is missing. Europeana gives 9,000+ glosses, of which 3,478 in Old Irish.

### Moran 2023, revised typology
Moran reviews prior typological approaches, especially Rijcklof Hofman's typology used for the St Gall Priscian glosses, and proposes a revised typology designed for portability to other gloss corpora and cross-regional comparison. Typologies are defended as tools for (a) enumerating possible functions of individual glosses, (b) quantitative characterisation of gloss collections, and (c) manuscript-to-manuscript comparison. This chapter is the current reference point for gloss-function classification alongside Wieland's older five-category scheme. His companion article "Language Interaction in the St Gall Priscian Glosses" (Peritia 26, 2015, pp. 113-42) analyses language-switching within and between glosses.

### Griffith & Stifter, Milan Glosses Project
The Milan glosses (Thes. i 7-483) are interlinear and marginal notes to and translations of a Latin commentary on the Psalms in Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, MS C 301 inf., and form the largest single Old Irish gloss corpus. FWF project P19137-G03 (2006-2011, EUR 110,364.03) under Aaron Griffith (PI) and David Stifter (director) built a corrected full text and FileMaker dictionary databases, modelled on Kavanagh/Wodtko's Würzburg lexicon. The text was revised against Best's 1936 collotype facsimile, Ascoli's 1878-9 edition, and inspection of the manuscript in Milan (Dec 2010). Ten downloadable PDFs cover Ml. 2a1-146d1 complete; publication of the printed lexicon was planned for the OeAW Praehistorische Kommission series.

### Gretsch 1999
Gretsch assigns to AEthelwold, Bishop of Winchester (963-84), two foundational texts: the interlinear Royal Psalter translation and the vast Old English gloss corpus to Aldhelm's prose De virginitate, arguing these shaped the vernacular as a medium for scholarly discourse (the "Winchester vocabulary" programme). Chapter structure covers psalters and psalter glosses in Anglo-Saxon England, the Royal Psalter's vocabulary, lexical/stylistic links to the Old English Rule, and AEthelwoldian authorship chapters. Published in Cambridge Studies in Anglo-Saxon England 25 (CUP 1999; online 2009), cited 49 times per Crossref. Her follow-up article "The Junius Psalter gloss: its historical and cultural context" (Anglo-Saxon England 29, 2000) continues the psalter-gloss chain.

### Tamoto 2013 (Rushworth/MacRegol Gospels)
Tamoto's Benjamins volume provides the first single complete edition of Bodleian MS Auct. D. 2. 19 with its entire Old English interlinear gloss (cxxxix + 339 pp.; DOI 10.1075/z.180). The Bodleian catalogue dates the Latin Gospels to Ireland (Birr), before 822, with the Old English gloss added in the tenth century. The Turin repository paper states the gloss was written in the second half of the tenth century by two scribes who name themselves in colophons as Farman (Mercian, Matthew) and Owun (Northumbrian, Mark-Luke-John); Farman's portion shows dependence on the Lindisfarne gloss. Tadashi Kotake's Medium AEvum NS44 volume separately edits Farman's gloss ("Rushworth One") against online facsimiles.

### Wieland 1983
Wieland's monograph on CUL MS Gg. 5.35 (Arator and Prudentius glosses) established the standard functional typology of glosses: five categories - prosodic, lexical, grammatical, syntactical, commentary - clarifying texts on literal and allegorical levels. He showed glosses often introduce material "irrelevant" to the lemma poem (expansion outward into general learning) and corrected the assumption that gloss function was exhausted by word-level vernacular translation. The work is Studies and Texts 61, Toronto, 1983; a Brepols Festschrift (Teaching and Learning in Medieval Europe, 2017) honours him. This typology is the explicit starting point for O'Sullivan's synthesis and is contrasted with Moran's revised St Gall typology.

### O'Sullivan, Text, Gloss, Tradition
O'Sullivan builds on Wieland to argue that glossing was fundamentally a practice of collectio keyed to lemmata, expanding the text "into a world of learning", with text and gloss transmitted together. She highlights unresolved functions of Latin-vernacular code-switching, citing Moran's demonstration that in the St Gall Priscian language instruction "does not seem to fit the bill", and Moran's observation that Irish occurs where equivalent manuscripts have Latin glosses. Parallels include mixed Latin/Old High German glosses in the Weitz tradition (Cologne Ms. 81) and Welsh glosses integrated into a continental Martianus tradition (CCCC 153). Useful bridge source connecting Anglo-Saxon, Irish and continental gloss practices.

## Cross-cutting observations (session-verified)

- Two independent scribal-authorship models recur: named glossators self-identifying in colophons (Aldred at Chester-le-Street; Farman and Owun in Rushworth) vs anonymous institutional attribution (AEthelwold's Winchester school for the Royal Psalter; the three Wuerzburg hands).
- Dating spread across the corpus: Wuerzburg mid-8th c.; St Gall Priscian MS 850-1; Vespasian gloss early 9th c. (Kuhn); Lindisfarne and Rushworth glosses 10th c.
- Gloss counts now stable in digital editions: Wuerzburg 3,501 Irish-content glosses; St Gall 9,400+ glosses (3,478 Old Irish per Europeana); Milan glosses = largest Old Irish corpus (Thes. i 7-483).
- Construe marks/symbols appear as a third annotation layer beside interlinear and marginal glosses (St Gall c. 3,000-4,000 symbols; also treated in Blom 2017).
