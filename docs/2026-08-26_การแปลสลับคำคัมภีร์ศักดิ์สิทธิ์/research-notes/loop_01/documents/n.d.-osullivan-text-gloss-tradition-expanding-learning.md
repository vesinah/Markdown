## Key extracted content

- Thesis: marginal and interlinear glosses "were not just ubiquitous but generated widely circulating gloss traditions that afford insight into the reception of key texts and transmission of learning"; the question of function was "foregrounded by Gernot Wieland" whose "essential typology ... demonstrates how glosses clarify a text on both the literal and allegorical levels".
- Argues transmission of text and glosses was intertwined (codicological/palaeographical evidence), citing Sinéad O'Sullivan CCCM 237 edition intro (pp. xxv-xxvii) and her Weitz Tradition monograph (p. 23).
- Core argument: glosses "expand outwards beyond the text into a world of learning"; collectio ("an essential constituent of early medieval scholarly practice and of medieval memoria") "was frequently at the heart of glossing"; a gloss was keyed to its lemma or tag word, "which often served as a cue or reference point for collecting".
- On code-switching: "code switching between Latin and the vernacular in early medieval glosses, the precise function of which remains unclear, as Padraic Moran has demonstrated for the Old Irish glosses in the St. Gall Priscian (St. Gallen, Stiftsbibliothek, Cod. Sang. 904, saec. IX med, probably Ireland), where language instruction does not seem to fit the bill." Quotes Moran: Irish occurrence "corresponds to Latin in equivalent glosses in other manuscripts."
- Parallel case discussed: ninth-century Welsh glosses in Cambridge, Corpus Christi College MS 153 on Martianus Capella - vernacular incorporated into an existing continental Latin gloss tradition; lemma selection identical to Latin tradition.
- Example of mixed-language glossing cited in footnote: Cologne, Dombibliothek Ms. 81 (Weitz tradition) contains glosses with Latin and Old High German words written by the same hand (e.g. BUCCULAS genus uasorum uel rantboga).
