## Extracts (OCR text, archive.org djvu.txt, checked 2026-08-25)

### p. 143 - East Syriac liturgical books incl. the Turgama
"The lessons are contained in three books ; the Kerydnd ('readings') contain the Old Testament and Acts, the Ewangeliyun, the Gospels, and the Shliha ('Apostle'), the Epistles of St. Paul. The Choir uses the Dauida (Psalter), the Hudra ('circle') containing the variable chants for all Sundays, the Kashkul ('containing all') for the week-days, and the Turgama ('interpretation'), in which are found the verses sung between the lessons, like our Gradual."
- Index entry seen: "Turgama, Nest[orian] book, 143".

### p. 277 - Coptic church: readings repeated in Arabic
"Most Coptic service books have a parallel Arabic version. The lessons in the liturgy are read first in Coptic, then in Arabic ; so their service includes three languages."

### p. ~286 - same practice described in rite walkthrough
"Each lesson is followed at once by a short verse sung (hypakoe/gradual), and is then read in Arabic."

### p. 348 - West Syriac (Jacobite): Arabic invasion of liturgy since 15th c.
"Since the 15th century, as Syriac more and more became a dead language to most Jacobites, there has been a great invasion of Arabic in their liturgy. Now the lessons, the Lord's Prayer, many pro-anaphoral prayers and hymns are in Arabic, written sometimes in Karshuni."

### p. 350 - Our Father said by all "in Arabic" (footnote: Mosul 1881 ritual edition, p. 32)

### p. 368 - Malabar Jacobites
"All talk Malayalam... Their Syriac services are like those in Latin to us."

### p. 274-275 area - Egypt: vernacular translation as origin of Coptic liturgy
"in the first centuries no one had any idea of a special liturgical language. As the faith spread to the villages of Upper Egypt the same prayers were, as a matter of course, translated into the popular language of the country."

### p. 345-346 - Antioch: St James liturgy used "in Greek or Syriac indifferently"; Orthodox kept Greek form, Jacobites Syriac.

## Notes (loop 16 relevance)
- Verifies the term Turgama (East Syriac) as a liturgical book whose name means "interpretation," containing verses between lessons - i.e., the interpretive genre tied to scripture readings.
- Documents the sacred-language hierarchy concretely: liturgical language retained while readings are rendered into the vernacular (Coptic->Arabic repetition), or replaced outright by the vernacular over time (Jacobite lessons in Arabic; Maronite usage per Sfeir).
- Does not itself attest an office-holder called turgemana translating orally during services; that specific claim remains unverified this session.
