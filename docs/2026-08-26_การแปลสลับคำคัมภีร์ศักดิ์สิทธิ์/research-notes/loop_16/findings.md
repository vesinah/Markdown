## Per-source notes

1. Muradyan 2014 (Brill): Standard current synthesis of the Hellenizing School. Classical-period translators took the sentence as the unit; Hellenizing translators rendered word by word, sometimes morpheme by morpheme. Chapter compares Classical vs Hellenizing renderings of the same Greek texts including Bible passages and biblical citations in Philo and Timothy Aelurus. Records Terian's chronology (Constantinople ca. 590-610 per Akinean; group after 570; early-6th-c. group around Step'anos Siwnec'i).
2. Terian 1980: Describes the hunaban movement from the 5th c.: translators created a deliberate "translationese" Classical Armenian, translating literally from Greek down to word order, hard to read without the Greek, yet enriching Armenian with Greek-model compounds. Confirms the scholarly frame for any "Armenian interlinear-like" technique claim.
3. Fortescue 1913: Richest single haul this session (full OCR searched). p. 143: East Syriac Turgama = book of "interpretation," verses sung between the lessons (like the Gradual). p. 277 and p. ~286: Coptic lessons are read first in Coptic, then read again in Arabic ("their service includes three languages"). p. 348: since the 15th c., among Jacobites "the lessons, the Lord's Prayer, many pro-anaphoral prayers and hymns are in Arabic, written sometimes in Karshuni." p. 350: Our Father said by all in Arabic (Mosul 1881 ritual, p. 32). p. 368: Malabar Jacobites' "Syriac services are like those in Latin to us." Egypt section: vernacular translation of originally Greek services from at least the 3rd c. Antioch section: St James liturgy used "in Greek or Syriac indifferently" before the split.
4. Brock 2006: Authoritative overview of Syriac biblical versions and their liturgical use (lectionaries included); visible fragments stress that Syriac liturgy is saturated with Peshitta phraseology and that pre-print scripture reading happened chiefly in church services. His companion article "The use of the Syriac versions in the Liturgy" (2006) is the focused treatment. Specific modern Arabic-repetition passages inside the book could not be extracted this session (access blocks).
5. Sfeir 1953: Maronite Mass handbook states the Gospel, Epistle, Our Father and about seventeen other prayers plus most responses/hymns "are said in Arabic which for the last four hundred years or more has been..." [the case]; also documents bilingual Syriac-Arabic verse alternation before the Epistle.
6. Tarchnishvili edition + Oxford page: Grand Lectionary of Jerusalem (5th-8th c.) reconstructed from Paris georg. 3, Sinai georg. 37 (982, John Zosimus), Lathal, Kala mss; Graz 7th-c. fragment and an 8th-c. Pentecost/November fragment; Kekelidze's 1912 partial edition. Garitte 1972 published the Georgian index to Jerusalem Gospel readings from Sinai geo. 30 & 38 (copied 979 by Ezra and John Zosimus).
7. CSLA E03423: "Universally acknowledged to be a version in Georgian of a Greek original (now lost)"; consensus that it reflects Jerusalem practice around 600; Greek composition dated 7th c., Georgian translation 8th c.; native title "Regulation and order of the holy hierarchs, as observed in Jerusalem."
8. Kvirkvelia 2025: New Georgian witnesses to the Jerusalem-rite lectionary keep surfacing (e.g., Ivir. georg. 59, two origins/two scripts); Old Georgian tradition transmitted the Jerusalem rite through euchologion (kurtkevani) and lectionary types. Companion 2026 Hamburg project on palimpsested Georgian Gospels spans all lectionary traditions.
9. Robert 2006: Verified directly on the Nanzan site: Bulletin vol. 30 article "Hieroglossia: A Proposal," author Jean-Noel Robert, PDF available; pagination 25-48 confirmed by CRCAO/HAL listing. Concept defined by his College de France profile: cultural-linguistic zones where the religious factor works through translation (Chinese scriptures vs Japanese paraphrase treated as religious practice by monastic poets). Sequels: hieroglossic-interaction essay (Bulletin 31); La hieroglossie japonaise (2012) and Japanese Hieroglossia (2013).
10. Collins 1992 / Uspenskij: Collins's Brill chapter opens by naming "B.A. Uspenskij's theory that there was Church Slavonic-Russian diglossia in medieval Rus'" as controversial since Uspenskij's Istorija russkogo literaturnogo jazyka (XI-XVII vv.) (1987); Vereshchagin wrote a named critique of that theory. A second citable English discussion is Collins's "Medieval Russian Diglossia" entry in the Encyclopedia of Slavic Languages and Linguistics Online (Brill).

## Verification verdicts on earlier-unverified items

(A) "Hokeakes" (Armenian interlinear renderings of Greek scripture used in services): STILL-UNVERIFIED as a term; context CORRECTED.
- Exhaustive spelling searches this session ("hokeakes", "hokeakay", "hokeak") returned no scholarly hit; the only hits for the string are unrelated (hoecake recipes). No source by Stone, Terian, Cox, Cowe, Muradyan or Calzolari seen this session uses such a term.
- What IS verified around the earlier claim: (i) before Mashtots' alphabet, scripture/liturgy were read in Greek or Syriac and orally rendered into Armenian (Koriun-derived account seen at armenianbible.org); (ii) literal word-by-word/morpheme-by-morpheme Greek-to-Armenian translation technique is real and well documented for the Hellenizing School (Muradyan 2014; Terian 1980), including on biblical texts; (iii) Grokipedia's Hellenizing School page (AI-generated, low grade, used only as a lead) speaks of "interlinear versions designed to aid Armenian students studying in Byzantine schools," but its cited anchor is Muradyan's chapter, which this session verified only for word-by-word technique, not for a service-use term "hokeakes."
- Working hypothesis for next loop: the form is corrupt - candidates include a garbled transliteration of an Armenian word (e.g., t'argmanic'-related lexemes) or a misreading of a manuscript-practice description; check Stone's own articles on Armenian interlinear/bilingual manuscripts and Bernard Outtier on bilingual psalters/gospels directly in print.

(B) Syriac oral/vernacular translation during services: PARTIALLY VERIFIED; term usage CORRECTED.
- VERIFIED: turgama is an East Syriac liturgical book whose name means "interpretation," containing the verses sung between lessons (Fortescue 1913, p. 143 and index).
- VERIFIED: readings delivered in Arabic within Syriac-rite services - Maronites: Epistle/Gospel said in Arabic for "four hundred years or more" (Sfeir 1953); Jacobites since the 15th c. have lessons in Arabic/Karshuni (Fortescue p. 348). Parallel explicit repetition practice attested for Copts: lesson first read in Coptic, then read in Arabic (Fortescue pp. 277, ~286).
- STILL-UNVERIFIED: the specific office-holder noun "turgemana" as the deacon/interpreter who translates readings aloud; searches with several spellings found only non-scholarly translator tools. Next step: Badger, The Nestorians and their Rituals (cited by Fortescue for Nestorian books) and Gabriel of Qatar's liturgy commentary (Brock, Hugoye 6:2, 2003) should be checked for targumana-role terminology.

(C) Georgian translations of Greek liturgy/scripture as sacred-language hierarchy: VERIFIED.
- The Old Georgian Jerusalem (Hagiopolite) Lectionary is universally acknowledged as a Georgian version of a lost Greek original reflecting Jerusalem practice ca. 600 (CSLA E03423); critical edition Tarchnishvili, CSCO 189/205 (1959-60), mss listed above; Garitte's 1972 Gospel index from Sinai geo. 30/38. Broader picture verified from the Pearse-rendered overview and Kvirkvelia 2025: earliest translations partly via Armenian mediation, revised against Greek through Athos (11th c.); St James liturgy and Presanctified preserved in full only in Georgian translation (per zxc-mirror summary corroborating CSCO 123/Tarchnisvili 1950 - the mirror itself not citable; cite Tarchnisvili editions directly).

(D) Robert "hieroglossia" citation and Uspenskij diglossia: VERIFIED / VERIFIED.
- Robert: Nanzan Bulletin 30 (2006), pp. 25-48, exact title "Hieroglossia: A Proposal" - citation as previously recorded is correct; concept and follow-up literature confirmed (Nanzan pages fetched; HAL/CRCAO records).
- Uspenskij: diglossia framework applied to Church Slavonic vs Russian in medieval Rus' is real and contested; citable English-language secondary discussions exist: Daniel E. Collins, "On Diglossia and the Linguistic Norms of Medieval Russian Writing," Studies in Russian Linguistics (Brill, 1992), pp. 79-94, DOI 10.1163/9789004652750_004 (Crossref-confirmed), and Collins's "Medieval Russian Diglossia" entry in Brill's Encyclopedia of Slavic Languages and Linguistics Online. Uspenskij's own key publications (1987 book; a 1983 IJSLP piece whose details surfaced only in a wiki-mirror bibliography) remain to be checked at first hand.
