# Source Extract: Jerome, Letter 57 to Pammachius (De optimo genere interpretandi)

- Author: St. Jerome (Eusebius Hieronymus)
- Work: Letter 57, "To Pammachius on the Best Method of Translating"
- Date: A.D. 395
- Type: Primary source (patristic letter); founding text of word-for-word vs sense-for-sense debate
- Access: New Advent, Church Fathers series
- URL: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3001057.htm
- Status: FULL-FETCHED (full English text retrieved this session)

## Bibliographic basis of online text

English translation by W.H. Fremantle, G. Lewis and W.G. Martley, from Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Second Series, Vol. 6, ed. Philip Schaff and Henry Wace (Buffalo, NY: Christian Literature Publishing Co., 1893). Revised for New Advent by Kevin Knight.

## Occasion (per New Advent headnote and letter body)

- Written to Pammachius in A.D. 395 after Jerome's Latin version of an Epiphanius letter (to John of Jerusalem) circulated without permission and was attacked as falsification; he was charged with not rendering word for word ("they tell the unlearned that I have falsified the original, that I have not rendered word for word"). An unnamed critic, supposed by Jerome to be instigated by Rufinus, accused him of error or deliberate offense.
- The letter defends his method "to give sense for sense and not word for word" by appeal to classical, ecclesiastical, and New Testament practice.

## Key passages retrieved

1. The famous rule, qualified for Scripture (sec. 5): "I myself not only admit but freely proclaim that in translating from the Greek (except in the case of the holy scriptures where even the order of the words is a mystery) I render sense for sense and not word for word."

2. Appeal to Cicero (Tully), who translated Plato's Protagoras, Xenophon's Oeconomicus, and the Aeschines-Demosthenes orations; Jerome quotes Cicero's prologue: "I have rendered them not as a translator but as an orator, keeping the sense but altering the form... I have not deemed it necessary to render word for word but I have reproduced the general style and emphasis... only to give him an equivalent in value."

3. Horace, Art of Poetry, cited against word-for-word rendering: "And care not with over anxious thought / To render word for word."

4. From his preface to the Chronicle of Eusebius: "If I render word for word, the result will sound uncouth, and if compelled by necessity I alter anything in the order or wording, I shall seem to have departed from the function of a translator."

5. From his preface to the Life of Antony: "A literal translation from one language into another obscures the sense; the exuberance of the growth lessens the yield." And: "My version always preserves the sense although it does not invariably keep the words of the original. Leave others to catch at syllables and letters, do you for your part look for the meaning."

6. Hilary of Poitiers praised as sense-translator: "Like a conqueror he has led away captive into his own tongue the meaning of his originals"; he did not bind himself "by the stale literalism of inadequate culture."

7. What learned critics call fidelity, Jerome says, others call "pestilent minuteness."

8. Scriptural precedent argument (secs. 7-10): the Septuagint translators, the evangelists, and the apostles themselves did not translate literally when quoting the Old Testament. Examples given in detail: Mark 5:41 adds "I say unto you" to "Talitha cumi"; Matthew 27:9-10 attributes to Jeremiah words found in Zechariah "in quite different words and an altogether different order"; John quotes Zechariah differently from both Hebrew and Septuagint ("the divergence of language is atoned by oneness of spirit"); Paul "has not rendered his original word for word, but, using a paraphrase, he has given the sense in different terms" (1 Cor 2:9 vs Isaiah 64:4). Conclusion: apostles and evangelists "have sought to give the meaning rather than the words."

9. Counter-example of extreme literalism condemned: Aquila the proselyte, "who has striven to translate not words only their etymologies as well," rendering "syllable by syllable and letter by letter" with constructions neither Greek nor Latin admits - described as "unhappy pedantry."

10. Implicit limit kept intact: despite defending sense-based method, Jerome concedes scripture is special ("even the order of the words is a mystery"), and he defends the Septuagint's churchly authority while noting its additions/omissions marked by daggers and asterisks.

## Relevance to sacred-text literalism vs comprehension

The letter is the locus classicus establishing (a) sense-for-sense translation as legitimate and ancient practice, (b) a carve-out for Scripture where word order may itself carry mystery - the seed of later literalist arguments for sacred texts, and (c) scriptural self-testimony that inspired authors paraphrased freely. Both camps in modern Bible-translation debates cite this letter.
