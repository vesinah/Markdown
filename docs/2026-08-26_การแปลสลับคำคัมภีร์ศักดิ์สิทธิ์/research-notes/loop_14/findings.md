## Per-source notes

### Jerome, Letter 57 (FULL-FETCHED)
Full English text retrieved from New Advent (NPNF 2/6, 1893). Written 395 to defend Jerome's sense-based rendering of an Epiphanius letter against charges of falsification for not translating word-for-word. Core rule stated with a scripture carve-out: "in translating from the Greek (except in the case of the holy scriptures where even the order of the words is a mystery) I render sense for sense and not word for word." Cites Cicero, Horace, Hilary; argues evangelists/apostles/Septuagint themselves translated non-literally (Mark 5:41; Matt 27:9-10; 1 Cor 2:9), while condemning Aquila's letter-by-letter pedantry as "pestilent minuteness." This letter is the founding text of the literalism-vs-comprehension debate and already contains both poles: sense-for-sense advocacy plus a sacred-text exception that later literalists invoke.

## Cross-cutting findings

1. The word-for-word vs sense-for-sense debate originates with classical rhetorical imitation (Cicero, Horace) and enters sacred-text territory immediately through Septuagint lore (Philo) and Paul's 1 Cor 14 intelligibility principle, as evidenced by Robinson's anthology structure.
2. Jerome Letter 57 (fetched in full) is the pivot document: it licenses sense-for-sense translation while carving out Scripture as exceptional ("even the order of the words is a mystery") - simultaneously the charter of comprehension-oriented translation and the seed of sacred-text literalism.
3. Nida's 1964 Brill monograph operationalizes comprehension for missions: dynamic orientation chapters ("The Dynamic Dimension in Communication," "Principles of Correspondence") inside a book whose subtitle is entirely about Bible translating.
4. Venuti's domestication/foreignization pair (Schleiermacher lineage documented via Paloposki's Handbook entry) re-describes the same axis as cultural politics; a 2025 study applies it quantitatively to Greek NT translations.
5. Appiah's thick translation offers the mediating position: retain source-context specificity but supply it through explicit scholarly framing - canonized by inclusion alongside Steiner in Venuti's Translation Studies Reader.
6. Sanneh supplies the historical/sociological claim that vernacular scripture translation transformed receiving cultures; Long's 2005 collection demonstrates that by 2005 "holy untranslatability" debates spanned all major religions, not only Christianity.
7. Bibliographic constellation verified this session: the theoretical canon (Kelly 1979, Steiner 1975, Robinson 1997 anthologizing Jerome through Nietzsche) plus the applied Bible-translation wing (Nida 1960/1964; Burke/Nida Institute presence in Long 2005).
