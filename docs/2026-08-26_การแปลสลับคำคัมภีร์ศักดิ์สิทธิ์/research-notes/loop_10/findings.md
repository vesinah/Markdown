## Per-source notes

**Tubb & Boose 2007.** Codifies the "five services" expected of a Sanskrit commentary: padaccheda (word division/resolution of external sandhi), padarthokti (word-meaning statement via synonyms, paraphrases, definitions, examples), vigraha (analysis of complex formations), vakyayojana (syntactic construing), aksepasamadhana (objection-resolution). Confirms each Vedic collection received an ancient padapatha ("word-text") exhibiting each word-isolate separately without terminal sandhi. Distinguishes two construing approaches, anvayamukhi (full prose-order rearrangement) and kathambhutini (skeleton-plus-questions). Examples drawn chiefly from Mallinatha's mahakavya commentaries and Sarikara's Brahmasutrabhasya.

**Bronkhorst 1996.** anuvyakhyana occurs only four times in Vedic literature, always in the canonical enumeration sutrany-anuvyakhyanani-vyakhyanani, implying a graded hierarchy of exegetical layers. Sankara glosses anuvyakhyanas as mantra-explanations and vyakhyanas as arthavada. Following Bhandarkar, Katyayana's varttikas would be the anuvyakhyana and Patanjali's Mahabhasya the vyakhyana to Panini's sutras. Suggests the Upanishadic list already presupposes two-layered commentary on sutra works before the Common Era.

**Kulkarni & Das 2012.** Traditional Sanskrit schooling introduces sentence analysis at age 9-10 after memorizing sabdarupa, dhatupatha, Amarakosa, using danda-anvaya (all words rearranged into prose order) or khanda-anvaya (dependency-style question method resembling Tesnierian parsing). Mimamsa sangati theory supplies inter-paragraph coherence relations (objection, example, counter-example, corollary, pre-requisite, exception). Describes the nested sutra-bhasya-tika-tippani "bhasya parampara" chain plus khandana-mandana polemic chains and prakarana treatises as the three structural trends of Sanskrit scholastic literature.

**Ganeri (n.d.).** Bhasya defined by tradition as prapañcaka (expansion) of sutra-said meaning, required to keep sutra order intact, fix the scope of terms like adi, and declare interpretive policy (Sabara's rule: words taken in ordinary senses wherever possible). Coherence built through abhidheya/prayojana/sambandha identifications and prakarana/adhikarana sectioning. Notes many original works are cast deliberately in text-plus-commentary form (karika/varttika instead of sutra), showing commentary is not merely derivative but a compositional genre that stabilizes root-text authority.

**Nyanatusita (n.d.).** Gives the emic definition: sannaya (from Pali sanna/Skt samjna), also called padartha, = word-by-word translation/paraphrase Pali-to-Sinhala in which first the Pali word is placed in Sinhalese syntactic order, then translated, sometimes with extra explanation; extensive ones called arthavyakhyanaya/vyakhyayava. Documents parallel genres: pada-anuvaya (Sinhala-syntax reordering of the Pali without translation), gaetapadaya/granthipadaya (difficult-word glosses on portions), and Southeast Asian nissaya. Notes sannayas can be as old as their Pali originals and preserve variant readings and quotations from the lost Sihala-atthakatha.

**Blackburn 1997 (+ Mrozik review; Kervan article).** Large numbers of sutra (vistara) sannayas were composed from the mid-eighteenth century amid Sangha revival and renewed Pali Sutta/Vinaya study, aimed at educating novices. Saranamkara's Sararthadipani was the first and model bilingual commentary. Used in preaching and public displays of courtly erudition, the sannayas created a new textual community spanning monks and lay men and women of all literacy levels - vernacular access expanded without displacing Pali authority.

**Wilden (ed.) EFEO volumes.** The Pondicherry program maps Tamil commentary types (Lehmann), Sanskrit-Tamil scholastic interaction (Chevillard), grammatical/poetological/literary/Vaishnava genres, and 19th-century neo-commentaries (Ebeling). Companion volume (Anandakichenin & D'Avella eds.) states that in one case the notion of commentary was extended "to include translation", and its editors work precisely on Alvar poetry and Srivaisnava Manipravalam - confirming commentary-translation fusion as a recognized South Indian phenomenon.

**Zvelebil ch.16.** Urai culture rests on the mulam/urai division with "unquestioned authority of the original text": commentators refute each other but never the original author; golden age 13th-15th centuries. Earliest extant recorded commentary is Nakkirar on Iraiyanar Kalaviyal (8th c.); four commentary types distinguished since earliest times. Genre evolution arumpadha urai -> kurippurai -> vilakkurai/virivurai; Tiruvaimoli commentary grew from 6,000 to the 36,000-line Edu. Medieval commentators' prose became the accumulator for modern written Tamil.

**Champakalakshmi 1994 (+ Sruti interview; Tamil Arts Academy).** Tevaram patikams were recovered under Rajaraja I (identified with Apayakulasekhara in the Tirumuraikanta Puranam legend) with Nampi Antar Nampi organizing the seven Tirumurais; a panar-family girl preserved the pann melodies and Kattalai institutions were founded. Rajaraja appointed forty-eight oduvars at the Tanjavur Brihadisvara temple. Practitioner testimony confirms Tevaram is "considered the Tamil equivalent of the Vedas", sung in chorus like the Vedas during worship, with meaning explanations given at the mutt schools. Hymns themselves praise Siva as Vedic chanter and equate devotion with the four Vedas and Sanskrit-Tamil duality (Appar VI.301.1).

**Ciotti 2025.** Full study of the unique late-18th-c. Manipravalam vacanam of the Bhagavadgita (GOML R3507): hypersanskritised Tamil lexicon with Tamil syntax/endings ("Late Tamil Manipravalam"), written necessarily in dual Tamil Grantha + Tamil scripts, presented ritually as the Bhagavadgitamaha-mantra with a Risyadinyasa paratext marked by uniquely Srivaisnava kaimkarya intent. Contrasts literary hypotactic translation with the pedagogical pancalaksana system in IFP RE04169: avataranika plus word-by-word division, metrical-free syntactic rearrangement, and word-by-word glosses mirroring oral teaching. Manipravalam naming tracks script choice (grantha present = manipravalam; absent = tamiz).

**Goren-Arzony 2021.** Manipravalam ("gems and corals") is Kerala's emic term binding local language to Sanskrit; the Lilatilakam (late 14th c.) theorizes it through sutra+vrtti in Sanskrit with Manipravalam stanzas, applying alankara-sastra wholesale since no indigenous poetics existed. Its word typology (desi/samskrutarupa/samskrutabhava, with desi subdivided against Cola/Pandya/Telugu/Karnata languages) is iterated from Prakrit grammar, which provided the model of a non-Sanskrit yet rule-governed literary language. Argues Manipravalam is thus a sanctioned third register, not degenerate mixture; Freeman's work frames the Lilatilakam as sanctioning a regional literary language poised between Tamil and Sanskrit traditions.

## Cross-cutting findings (for downstream synthesis)
1. Shared apparatus across languages: pada-patha/pada-anuvaya (isolation + reordering), padarthokti/kurippu glossing, vigraha/compound analysis, anvaya/vakyayojana prose-order construing, and objection-resolution form one continuous intra-language (and cross-language) translation technology.
2. Authority is preserved by keeping mula recitation intact (Vedic samhitapatha with padapatha control; Pali canon; Tevaram/Divyaprabandham sung; Gita recited as mantra) while explanation circulates freely in vernacular or hybrid registers.
3. Vernacularization proceeds through sanctioned genres rather than free translation: sannaya, urai (arumpadham/kurippuram/vilakkam), nissaya, Manipravala vacanam/padi.
4. Hybrid registers (Manipravalam; Thambiranpadi in Manipravala idiom) let theological terminology stay Sanskrit while syntax serves the vernacular reader.
