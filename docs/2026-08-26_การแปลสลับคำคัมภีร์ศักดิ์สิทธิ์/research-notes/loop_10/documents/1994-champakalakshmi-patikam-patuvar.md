## Key extracts
- Discusses the patikam-singers (patikam patuvar) and the term Tevaram's obscurity: "for a general discussion on the obscurity of the term Tevaram and its etymology see Francois Gros, 'Towards ... Tevaram'".
- Recounts the recovery legend from Umāpati Śivācāriyār's Tirumuraikanta Puranam: hymns recovered by a Cola king "Apayakulasekhara (identified with Rajaraja I)" with Nampi Antar Nampi; sealed room in the Tillai (Chidambaram) temple; moth-eaten palm leaves; divine voice assuring that "the important among the hymns had been preserved"; hymns organized into "the first seven Tirumurais".
- Musical transmission detail: "the pan (tune) in which the hymns were composed by the trio was known only to a girl of a panar family in Tiruvekkattampuliyur near Chidambaram"; Kattalais (music/rhythm per metre) were established.
- Notes present-day Otuvan performance "as interpretation, with a freedom of choice from the hymns to be rendered" (citing Peterson, Poems to Siva, pp. 59-67).
- Campantar's hymns described as depicting Siva "as Vedic Chanter, as ritually pure and as the Brahmana Lord, to the Vedas" (e.g., Appar VI-301.1 where "Siva is both Sanskrit and Tamil").

## Corroborating practitioner source seen this session
- Sruti magazine interview with Sri R. Velayuda Oduvar at Dharmapuram Adheenam (https://www.sruti.com/articles/spotlight/oduvars-a-hoary-tradition-of-hymn-singing): "The Tevaram is considered the Tamil equivalent of the Vedas, even as the Vedas propounded in Sanskrit were once called Tevaram"; stone inscriptions show "Rajaraja Chola I introduced Tevaram singing in temples. He appointed forty-eight hymnodists to serve as oduvars at the Brihadeeswarar temple in Tanjavur"; sung during abhishekam; "Tevaram is also sung in chorus, like the Vedas"; on meaning-teaching: "We give enough explanation of the songs."
- Tamil Arts Academy intro page: Tevaram hymns "belong essentially to the Vedic tradition", "revered as Vedas", sung daily by oduvars in panns.

## Relevance to loop topic
Establishes both the institutional history (Chola restoration of a Tamil corpus to liturgical parity with the Sanskrit Veda) and the living practice (oduvar singing plus oral explanation) through which Tamil sacred originals stay authoritative while vernacular meaning is transmitted alongside Sanskrit Vedic recitation.
