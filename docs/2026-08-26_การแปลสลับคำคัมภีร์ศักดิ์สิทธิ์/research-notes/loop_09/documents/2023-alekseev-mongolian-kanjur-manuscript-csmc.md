## Extracted facts

- The first surviving edition of the Mongolian Kanjur comprises a total of 883 works, prepared in manuscript form in 1628-1629 under the last khan of Mongolia, Ligdan (1588-1634), by "a relatively small team of translators" who made extensive use of earlier translations.
- Mongol reception of Buddhist canonical texts began under the Yuan dynasty (1280-1368); translation activity declined after its fall and recommenced under Altan Khan of the Tumed (1508-1582); compilation of the whole Kanjur reportedly completed under Altan's grandson Namudai Secen Khan (1586-1607) per some Mongolian sources; that version has not survived.
- Ligdan Khan's redaction produced the luxury "Golden Kanjur," written in gold on blue paper; only a small number of volumes survive, kept at the library of the Academy of Social Sciences of Inner Mongolia in Hohhot; plain "black" copies were written alongside or later in the 17th century.
- The only complete and probably oldest "black" manuscript is at St Petersburg State University Library (shelfmark Ms. Mong. 1-113), discovered/acquired in 1892 by Alexei M. Pozdneev through postmaster N. Gomboev for 4,500 roubles.
- Physical description: 113 chapters in pothi format bound in 100 bundles between wooden boards; Chinese paper glued in two layers (paper mulberry and bamboo fibres); folios 68.5 x 23.5 cm; reed pen and black Chinese ink within ruled frame 57.5 x 15.5 cm; volume numbers marked with Tibetan letters; red fabric tags give section, volume number, and page counts.
- Layout/authority markers: majority of titles given first in Sanskrit and Tibetan (transcribed/transliterated with Mongolian letters) and only then in Mongolian; titles or initial lines in or highlighted with red ink; colophons name patron and translation committee.
- Structure: order of sections/works corresponds to Ligdan Khan's version generally, not strictly to any known Tibetan canon edition; numerous duplications are different Mongolian translations of the same Tibetan works.
- Dating evidence: hands characteristic of manuscripts produced in first half of the 17th century in Southern Mongolia; orthography likewise.
- Beijing blockprint edition of the Mongolian Kanjur made 1718-1720 under Kangxi became the basis of later tradition and differs structurally from the St Petersburg manuscript.
