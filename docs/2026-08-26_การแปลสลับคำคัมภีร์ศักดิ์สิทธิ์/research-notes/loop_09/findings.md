## Per-source notes

1. Tsetan 2024 (Tibet): The sGra sbyor bam po gnyis pa = Madhyavyutpatti ("Middle Vyutpatti") is an imperial translation manual issued as royal decree (bkas bcad). Tabo monastery fragments (Spiti, Himachal Pradesh) lead the author to date composition to 783 CE under Trisong Detsen (r. 755-797); he compares a Tabo version (decree 783) with a Tibet Museum (Lhasa) version (decree 814), illustrating each dharma bsgyur ba'i thabs ("method of translating the dharma"). Its bibliography anchors the field: Ishikawa 1990 critical edition (Toyo Bunko); Panglung 1994 new Tabo fragments; Scherrer-Schaub 2002 diplomatic analysis of the imperial decrees (JIABS 25); Simonsson 1957; Verhagen 1994/2001/2015; Hahn 2007; Penpa Dorjee 2011 Sarnath edition.
2. Grokhovskiy n.d. (Tibet): The Mahavyutpatti in the Bstan 'gyur (Derge vol. co, ff. 1-131a4) was compiled by Indian panditas and Tibetan lotsawas under Khri lde srong btsan and Ral pa can in the early 9th century and contains 9492 lexical units under 277 thematic headings (Ishihama & Fukuda edition). The Madhyavyutpatti (= sGra sbyor bam po gnyis pa, Derge ff. 131b1-160a7) is its commentary: an introduction prescribing adherence to "the authoritative system of established Sanskrit-Tibetan translation equivalents," plus word-and-meaning commentary on 413 Sanskrit lexical units under 50 headings (170 + 243 entries) and a colophon. Simonsson translated colophon/introduction/selections; Verhagen treated it as the earliest Tibetan scholarship on Sanskrit.
3. Samye Institute wiki (Tibet): Quotes the Mahavyutpatti's introduction naming the translation team - Indian preceptors Jinamitra, Surendrabodhi, Silendrabodhi, Danasila, Bodhimitra with Tibetan translator-scholars Ratnarakshita, Dharmatashila, Jnanasena, Jayarakshita, Manjushrivarman, Ratnendrashila - commissioned to translate, define, and list the terminology of Great and Small Vehicles. States the team corrected errors, restored omissions, and reduced verbose renderings of older translations. Gives a worked sGra sbyor-style etymology: Sanskrit buddha analyzed ("awakened from the sleep of delusion"; "intelligence unfolds") to justify Tibetan sangs rgyas. Claims Mahavyutpatti has over 9500 terms. Note: attributes the compilation gathering at Samye to Sadnalegs (Thride Songtsen), which conflicts with other sources seen this session (see Open questions).
4. Alekseev 2023 CSMC (Mongolia): The first surviving Mongolian Kanjur edition (883 works) was produced as manuscript in 1628-1629 under Ligdan Khan by a small translation committee reusing earlier translations; the luxury "Golden Kanjur" (gold on blue paper) survives only in fragments (Hohhot), while the complete oldest "black" copy (113 chapters, pothi format, 100 bundles, Ms. Mong. 1-113) sits at St Petersburg State University Library, bought via Pozdneev from N. Gomboev in 1892 for 4,500 roubles. Authority-preserving layout: most titles given first in Sanskrit and Tibetan (transliterated in Mongolian script) before the Mongolian title; rubrication; colophons naming patron and committee. Kangxi sponsored a Beijing blockprint Kanjur in 1718-1720 whose structure diverges from Ligdan's. References Heissig 1957/1962/1973 on the organization of the Kandjur translation, Kollmar-Paulenz 2002, Uspensky 1997 (reconstructed catalogue of Tibetan title equivalents).
5. Alekseev 2021 BSR (Mongolia): The Ratnakuta section of the 1628-1629 Mongolian manuscript Kanjur appears to have been translated en bloc in those years; it lacks eight sutras and misplaces one text; textual elements preserved from the Tibetan source(s) tie it to the Them spangs ma and Western Tibetan Kanjur recensions - evidence that Mongolian translators carried specific Tibetan canonical lineages into Mongolian. Reference list adds Yampolskaya 2018 (eight distinct Mongolian translations of the Astasahasrika Prajnaparamita) and Kollmar-Paulenz 2017 state-of-research.
6. Berzin n.d. (Mongolia/Qing bridge): Chronology of Tibetan-to-Mongolian translation: first text = Bodhicaryavatara by Chokyi Ozer under Yuan Wuzong (1308-1311); bulk of Kangyur under Altan Khan (1507-1582); completion 1628-1629 (Ligdan); Kangxi blockprint revision 1718-1720; Tengyur 1742-1749 under Qianlong with Changkya Rolpe Dorje supervising; Manchu Kangyur translated from Chinese 1772-1790. Changkya's Dag-yig mkhas-pa'i 'byung-gnas (1741-1742) is a two-column parallel Tibetan-Mongolian lexicon in eleven doxographic chapters including brda gsar-rnying (old/new terminology), with translator guidelines in its preface.
7. Song Yifeng 2021 (Qing/Manchu): Manchu translations of the Five Classics and Four Books served as examination texts and textbooks in Eight Banners Official Schools; framed as a tool for Bannermen to master Han academic/moral canons and to narrow Manchu-Han cultural distance for governance. References document imperially decreed editions (Qinding fanyi wujing sishu; Qianlong 1768 Wu Ying Hall Yuzhi fanyi Shijing, Manchu-Chinese) and Yamazaki Masato's distinction between old and new waves of Manchu Shijing translation.
8. Theobald 2013 (Qing/Manchu): The Qingwenjian series grew from the 1673-1708 Manchu mirror into Qianlong-era multilingual redactions: Zengding (1771), Sanhe qieyin (Manchu-Mongol-Chinese with reciprocal fanqie transcriptions), Santi (adding Tibetan), Siti (four languages: Manchu-Mongolian-Chinese-Tibetan), Wuti (five, adding Turki). The Wuti qingwen jian (finished ca. 1790, printed not before 1805) gives for each lemma eight entries including literal qieyin and phonetic duiyin transcriptions of Tibetan into Manchu; 36 categories, 292 categories/556 sub-categories, 17,052 words (+1,619 supplement = 18,671). Modern access: Minzu Press facsimile 1957, Tamura Jitsuzo 1966 yakukai, Corff 2013 Harrassowitz.
9. Theobald 2010 (Tangut): Fan-Han heshi zhangzhong zhu compiled by Gule Maocai at the Western Xia court of Renzong (r. 1140-1193), finished 1190; rediscovered at Khara-Khoto by Kozlov in 1909 as a 37-page butterfly-bound woodblock print. Its bilingual preface argues that mastering both languages is necessary to administer a Tangut-ruled state with Chinese subjects. Format is cross-transcribed (Tangut words glossed in Chinese characters and vice versa), arranged encyclopedically in nine sections with human activities taking nearly half the book (incl. Buddhism). Edition history: Soviet facsimile, Luo Fucheng 1924, Ningxia facsimile 1989; scholarship cited incl. Nie Hongyin 1987 on the phonetic notation symbols.
10. kepping.net (Tangut scholarship): Ksenia Kepping (1937-2002), world-renowned Tangut linguist born in Tianjin; memorial volume "Last Works and Documents" (St Petersburg 2003) hosts her late papers as PDFs, including "Tangut Ritual Language" (Leiden STLC 1996) and "The Famous Liangzhou Bilingual Stele: a new study" (Tangut-Chinese bilingual inscription), plus collaborative work with Gong Hwang-cherng on Tangut translations of Chinese literary texts (Zhuge Liang's "General's Garden"; Han Wudi's "Autumn Wind"). These directly document Tangut bilingual/translation practice, though no Pearl-in-the-Palm-specific study by Kepping was seen this session.
11. Arakawa 2023 (Tangut scholarship): Surveys Kychanov's foundational role and the Tangut-Russian-English-Chinese Dictionary (Kyoto 2006, co-edited with Arakawa), whose unique character-index supports script-shape research. Reference list maps the Japanese/Russian tradition: Nishida Tatsuo's Seikago no kenkyu (1966, vol. II), his dictionary articles (1989, 2001) and posthumous Shinron (2012); Sofronov's Grammar (1968); Kychanov's Khara-Khoto corpus work and Wenhai baoyun studies; Li Fanwen 2008; Nie Hongyin 2021; Gong Hwang-cherng 2003.
12. DILA/DDBC (Qing quadrilingual canon): The Manchu Canon was ordered by Qianlong in 1772 and finished ca. 1790; ca. 732 works in 108 boxes plus a catalog box in four languages (Manchu-Chinese-Tibetan-Mongolian); only twelve sets printed; woodblocks reprinted 2002 (20 copies). The project supplies a digitized image archive (Heart Sutra, Diamond Sutra cases), trilingual catalog, and a Manchu-Chinese-Sanskrit(-Mongolian) Buddhist-term glossary.

## Cross-cutting observations (all sourced above)

- Standardization as imperial policy recurs in all four polities: royal decrees fixing Sanskrit-Tibetan equivalents (783/814 bkas bcad); Ligdan Khan's translation committee and later Kangxi/Qianlong sponsorship; Qianlong's imperially compiled multilingual mirrors; the Western Xia court's officially styled bilingual glossary with a statecraft rationale in its preface.
- Authority of the sacred source language is materially preserved by keeping it visible: Sanskrit/Tibetan titles transliterated ahead of Mongolian titles in the Kanjur manuscripts; Sanskrit etymologies rehearsed inside Tibetan lexical commentaries; reciprocal phonetic transcriptions (qieyin/duiyin; Tangut-Chinese mutual transcription) so the prestige language remains legible to learners of the vernacular.
- Commentary genres enforce equivalence: the sGra sbyor/Madhyavyutpatti comments on 413 normative entries (word and meaning), functioning as a meta-commentary on the Mahavyutpatti; Changkya's lexicon preface legislates translator qualifications.

## UNVERIFIED this session

- "Interlinear Mongolian-Tibetan manuscript formats": searched repeatedly; no scholarly source describing true interlinear (line-between-lines) Tibetan-in-Mongolian or Mongolian-over-Tibetan layout was retrieved. Closest verified facts: transliterated Sanskrit/Tibetan titles preceding Mongolian titles (Alekseev 2023); Uspensky 1997 reconstructed Tibetan title equivalents; Yampolskaya 2015 on pothi foliation. Treat the interlinear-format claim as UNVERIFIED pending direct manuscript sources.
- Kepping's and Nishida's specific studies OF the Pearl in the Palm: their broader Tangut oeuvre is verified (kepping.net; Arakawa reference list), but Pearl-in-the-Palm-attributed studies by them were not seen this session.
- tshig don as a generalized early "word-by-word" commentary genre beyond the Madhyavyutpatti's 413-entry word-and-meaning commentary: searches surfaced mainly Longchenpa's Tshig don mdzod (Treasury of Word and Meaning, Seven Treasures), which is a different, later doctrinal genre. Genre-level claims about early translation-period tshig don literature remain UNVERIFIED here.
- 'grel ba ('grel pa) commentary-tradition specifics: no dedicated source fetched; only generic commentary scholarship (Verhagen 2015 "Tools of the Trade"; Hahn 2007) appeared in bibliographies.

## Open questions

1. Which decree date governs the sGra sbyor's normative core - 783 (Tabo, per Tsetan 2024) vs early 9th century under Khri lde srong btsan/Ral pa can (Grokhovskiy; Samye wiki)? Needs Scherrer-Schaub 2002 and Ishikawa 1990 checked against the Tabo and Lhasa witnesses.
2. Did any Mongolian Kanjur manuscripts employ genuinely interlinear Tibetan glosses, or only prefaced transliterated titles? Requires inspection of St Petersburg Ms. Mong. 1-113 folios and Yampolskaya's pothi-layout work.
3. What exactly did Kepping and Nishida publish on the Zhangzhongzhu (if anything)? Candidate leads not yet opened: Nie Hongyin 1987 school; Arakawa's phonology papers; BabelStone's Tangut catalogue webpages listing Zhangzhongzhu editions.
4. How did the Wuti qingwen jian's duiyin/qieyin transcriptions of Tibetan interact with monastic vs administrative usage of Tibetan in Qing rule?
5. Is there a continuous lineage between the 783/814 imperial decrees' translation procedures and later 'grel ba/tshig don commentarial practice on translated tantras, or is the continuity a retrospective construction?
6. The Pearl in the Palm's last section devotes large space to Buddhism-related vocabulary - how much Buddhist terminology entered Tangut via Chinese calques vs via Tibetan-language monastic channels?
