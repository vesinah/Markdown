1. Jezek 2022 (full text extracted in documents/)
=====================================================
Author: Vaclav Jezek
Title: "Ethiopian exegetical traditions and exegetical imagination viewed in the context of Byzantine Orthodoxy"
Journal: HTS Teologiese Studies/Theological Studies 78(1), a7997 (2022). DOI: 10.4102/hts.v78i1.7997
Status: Open access; PDF + extraction saved.

KEY PASSAGES:
- Etymology of andemta: "The term Andemta is perhaps derived from the Amharic word andem,
  '(another) one, which introduces an alternative opinion' (Pedersen 1995:4), or as Villa specifies,
  'The Amharic word andem, meaning "and (there is) one (who say s)"' (from which the term andemta is
  derived)' (Villa 2019:6)." Pedersen compares andem to Hebrew formulae such as "and there is one who
  says / another interpretation".
- Villa 2019 on t rgwame vs andemta: "Commentaries are subdivided into t rgwame and andemt commentaries.
  The former represent an early stage of the Ethiopian traditional exegesis. They are in Ge'ez and are
  attested from the fourteenth century onwards... The decline of the t rgwame corpus is linked to the rise
  of the andemt commentaries. The Amharic andemt corpus, originating in the Gondarine Age and committed to
  writing only in recent times, intends to explore in depth the true meaning of a given text, verse by verse."
- Cowley 1988 as comparativist study aligning Ethiopian exegesis with Antiochene/Jewish material;
  Cowley quote: "I am unconvinced that it is possible to construct a system, the application of which
  reveals the meaning of the text" (Cowley 1988:374).
- Andemta Genesis 1 characterized by Cowley 1988:262 as containing "the interpretation of words, theology,
  natural science, cosmology, calendrics, and homiletic application".
- Suggests comparison with Byzantine catenae as future work ("More studies are needed to assess the role
  of Byzantine biblical commentaries in the form of catenae and compare them with the Ethiopian tradition").

3. British Library EAP336 - "Preserving the lay bet andemta"
=====================================================
URL: https://eap.bl.uk/project/EAP336
Facts captured:
- Digitisation project for andemta (Ge'ez-Amharic commentary) manuscripts of the lay bet exegetical
  school; codices kept in private/monastic collections in Eastern Gojjam and Southern Gondar.
- Outcome statement: "The project digitised 54 manuscripts from 8 personal collections, covering more than
  85 Ge'ez-Amharic commentaries of different books from the four sections of Ethiopian Exegesis: Old
  Testament, New Testament, Patristic Works and Monastic Canons & Writings."
- Notes intra-tradition rivalry lay bet ('lower house') vs tach bet ('upper house') andemta.
- Copies deposited: British Library, EOTC Patriarchate Library Museum, National Archive and Library of
  Ethiopia, Debre Dimah St. George monastic library, University of Hamburg.

4. Getatchew Haile - concrete publication data (Target C)
=====================================================
(a) Getatchew Haile, "An 'Old' Amharic Commentary on the Genealogy of Christ (EMML 4751, fols. 7r-8v)",
    in Getatchew Haile, Ethiopian Studies in Honour of Amha Asfaw (New York, 2017), pp. 141-172.
    ISBN 978-0-9706663-8-3. Full text on ResearchGate (publication 324455180) and Scribd.
    Per Villa's review (Aethiopica 21 [2018], 247-249): the commentary is "inserted as a prefatory note in
    an eighteenth-century Four Gospels manuscript, is rife with linguistic archaisms and expands the corpus
    of Old Amharic texts with one additional document." Review also confirms the volume collects thirteen
    unpublished articles by Haile, many editing EMML manuscripts.
(b) Getatchew Haile, "Some Notes on 'A Text in Old Amharic' of Roger Cowley", Bulletin of the School of
    Oriental and African Studies 43.3 (1980): 578-580. (Listed on ResearchGate profile; also cited in
    Nosnitsin's 2018 Afrikanistentag slides bibliography.)
(c) EMML catalogues by Haile (e.g., vol. VIII, 1985) document Amharic commentary texts; vHMML record for
    EMML 4751 (Boru Sellase monastery, Wallo): "Texts in secondary hands: Commentary on Matthew 1, in
    Amharic" - i.e., Haile's cataloguing work is itself primary documentation of vernacular commentary MSS.
CAVEAT for verdict: searches surfaced no article-length study by Haile dedicated to the andemta corpus as
such; his relevant contributions are editions/cataloguing of Old-Amharic exegetical texts and manuscripts.
A selected bibliography of his ~150+ publications exists: A. Bausi & T. Rave, "A Selected Bibliography of
the Publications of Getatchew Haile", in McCollum (ed.), Studies in Ethiopian Languages, Literature, and
History: Festschrift for Getatchew Haile, Aethiopistische Forschungen 83, Wiesbaden: Harrassowitz, 2017,
pp. 609-619 (JSTOR j.ctvckq4mn.31).

5. Herngseng 2023 (nissaya vs sannaya; Target E) - PDF saved from Brill (open access)
=====================================================
Ven. Pannabhoga Herngseng, "Burmese Nissaya Literature: A Mainstream Monastic Education Learning Method
and its Salient Features", MANUSYA: Journal of Humanities 26.1 (2023), pp. 1-23. DOI: 10.1163/26659077-25010027.
KEY PASSAGES (from Brill page):
- "According to McDaniel (2008, 119), a similar genre of Pali literature also developed within Theravada
  Buddhist countries, i.e., in Sri Lanka, as sannaya, and in Thailand and Laos as nissaya, namasadda, and
  vohara..."
- "The Sutanipata-sannaya, a Pali-Sinhala word-to-word translation written in the 12th ce[ntury], is the
  earliest example of a nissaya, or sannaya literary genre found in Sri Lanka." (citing Pruitt 1992, 278)
- Pruitt 1992, 278: "Sinhalese Sanne and Burmese Nissayas have been important in Pali studies in the West."
- Braun 2013: nissaya = Burmese text that translates/explains Pali works "often on a systematic word-by-word basis".
- Genre spectrum per McDaniel: nissaya/namasadda = close word-for-word glosses; vohara cites longer passages
  (12-25 words) with extensive creative vernacular commentary; Burmese nissaya typically cites 4-10 words first.
- Also cites Walker (2020, 678) on Pali-Burmese nissayas extant from late 13th c.; Walker's related article:
  "Indic-Vernacular Bitexts from Thailand: Bilingual Modes of Philology, Exegetics, Homiletics, and Poetry,
  1450-1850" (pan-Theravada 'bitext' technology).
NOTE: This is Theravada-internal comparison (Burma/Sri Lanka/Thailand/Laos). No Ethiopia link.
