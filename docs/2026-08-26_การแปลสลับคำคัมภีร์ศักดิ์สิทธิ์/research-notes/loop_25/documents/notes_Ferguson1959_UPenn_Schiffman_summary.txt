VERBATIM DEFINITION QUOTED BY SCHIFFMAN:
"DIGLOSSIA is a relatively stable language situation in which, in addition to the primary dialects of the language (which may include a standard or regional standards), there is a very divergent, highly codified (often grammatically more complex) superposed variety, the vehicle of a large and respected body of written literature, either of an earlier period or in another speech community, which is learned largely by formal education and is used for most written and formal spoken purposes but is not used by any section of the community for ordinary conversation."

SCHIFFMAN'S ENUMERATION OF FERGUSON'S CRITERIA (post-1959 research variables):
1. Function - functional differentiation fundamental; H and L for different purposes; using H in L domain or vice versa felt odd.
2. Prestige - H more valued; H = 'great' literature, CANONICAL RELIGIOUS TEXTS, ancient poetry, public speaking.
3. Literary heritage - literature all in H; H thought to be THE language; L sometimes denied existence.
4. Acquisition - L mother tongue/home; H learned through schooling.
5. Standardization - H strictly standardized (grammars, dictionaries, canonical texts by native grammarians).
6. Stability - persists centuries/millennia; L gains domains occasionally; note "perhaps linked with religion" factor for stability of Arabic and South Asia diglossias.
7. Grammar - H more complex (tense, gender, agreement, syntax).
8. Lexicon - largely shared but differentiated; H has vocab L lacks and vice versa.
9. Phonology - shared systems or H subset/superset; two system types described.
10. Diglossia vs standard-with-dialects - in diglossia NO ONE speaks H as mother tongue.
11. Distribution across families/space/time; most involve literacy; oral diglossias conceivable.
12. Genesis conditions: (a) body of ancient/prestigious literature in H that linguistic culture wants to preserve; (b) literacy restricted to elite; (c) time needed to develop; religion-linked stability.
