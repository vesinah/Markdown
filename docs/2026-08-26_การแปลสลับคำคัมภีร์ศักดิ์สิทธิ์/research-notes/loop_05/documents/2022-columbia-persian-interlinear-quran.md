# Source extract: The Quran in East and West (Columbia University Libraries online exhibition)

- **Institution:** The Burke Library, Columbia University Libraries - online exhibition "The Quran in East and West: Manuscripts and Printed Books", section "Qurans > Manuscripts"
- **Type:** Institutional digital exhibition (curatorial catalogue text)
- **URL:** https://exhibitions.library.columbia.edu/exhibits/show/quran/qurans
- **Status:** FULL-FETCHED (this session)

## Key facts extracted

1. RBML MS arab. and pers. Smith or. 222: colophon signed by Muhammad Muhsin al-Isfahani, dated Ramadan 1154 / November 1741 (India?). Symmetrical double-page opening (sura 1 opposite start of sura 2). Two scripts distinguish the languages: Arabic in black naskh, Persian translation in smaller red nasta'liq written between the lines.
2. Curatorial framing of doctrine: "At a first glance a Persian translation of the Quran appears as a blatant violation of the Islamic dogma of the inimitability of the Arabic Quran's perfect language."
3. Counter-evidence given immediately: "Persian glosses of the Quran are preserved in tenth-century Quran manuscripts and in early Persian translations of Arabic Quran commentaries."
4. Dating problem acknowledged: "The point at which Persian interlinear translations of the complete Quran became fairly common is a matter of debate, since most of the extant Arabic-Persian Quran manuscripts were copied after 1500 CE."
5. Burke Arabic MS 4: Ottoman Qur'an, colophon signed Isma'il b. Ibrahim, dated 1175/1761-1762; compact octavo volume without patron evidence; the exhibit concludes that after 1500 CE nicely illuminated Qur'an copies were staples of workshop production for "the middlebrow book trade".
6. Damaged Qur'an manuscripts are not discarded but restored or recycled into apotropaic objects (talismans, framed leaves), because restoration was usually cheaper than a new copy.
7. Ottoman colophons in these volumes are written in Arabic, not Ottoman Turkish, switching to diwani script - showing continued Arabic-language prestige even in Ottoman production contexts.
