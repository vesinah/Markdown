# Source extract: "Zaferani Quran" - oldest dated Persian-translated Quran manuscript

- **Source:** Tehran Times (Culture section), "Ancient Persian-translated Quran unveiled at Iran National Library", 29 July 2026
- **Type:** News report on a scholarly facsimile publication event

- **Status:** FULL-FETCHED (this session)

## Key facts extracted

1. A high-quality facsimile edition of the "Zaferani Quran" was unveiled at the National Library and Archives of Iran (jointly with Farhang-e Siadat Publications); project took nearly eight years, using advanced color calibration.
2. The manuscript is preserved at Tehran's Reza Abbasi Museum and was copied in 546 AH (1151-52 CE) by Abolfazl Zaferani in the city of Rey; researchers identify it as the earliest surviving complete and precisely dated Persian-translated Quran identified to date.
3. Format: interlinear - the Persian translation is written in smaller script between the lines of the Arabic text. The translation is closely associated with the Quranic commentary of the 11th-century scholar Abu Bakr Atiq Nishapuri (Surabadi).
4. Manuscript scholar Morteza Kariminia led the publication effort; Emadi Haeri described the translation as an independent scholarly tradition rooted in early Iranian Quranic exegesis and highlighted early scholars' legitimisation of translating the Quran into Persian.
5. Linguist Ali Ashraf Sadeghi framed translation limits in terms relevant to i'jaz debates: "A perfect, hundred-percent translation between two languages is practically impossible"; Arabic and Persian words rarely have perfect one-to-one matches; ancient translators should not be criticized since they picked the best equivalents available for their era.
6. Mohammad Jafar Yahaqi (in a recorded message) placed the manuscript within the broader history of Quran translation in Iran, describing the 6th century AH as a golden age of Persian Quranic exegesis and translation.
7. Beyond religion, the manuscript is treated as a linguistic repository preserving early Persian vocabulary and forms that have disappeared from modern usage, and as evidence for Seljuk-period translation practice.
