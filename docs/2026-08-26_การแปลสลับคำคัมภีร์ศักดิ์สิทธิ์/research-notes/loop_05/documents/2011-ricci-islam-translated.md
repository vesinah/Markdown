# Source extract: Islam Translated (Ronit Ricci, 2011)

- **Author:** Ronit Ricci (Hebrew University of Jerusalem)
- **Title:** *Islam Translated: Literature, Conversion, and the Arabic Cosmopolis of South and Southeast Asia*
- **Year:** 2011
- **Type:** Scholarly monograph
- **Publisher:** University of Chicago Press (South Asia Across the Disciplines series); re-published in India by Permanent Black
- **ISBN:** Cloth 978-0-226-71088-4; Paper 978-0-226-38053-7; eISBN 978-0-226-71090-7; LC classification PJ813.R533 2011; 335 pages
- **URLs:** https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/I/bo11274031.html ; https://archive.org/details/islamtranslatedl0000ricc
- **Status:** SEARCH-VERIFIED (publisher page and archive.org record with table of contents retrieved this session)

## Key facts extracted

1. Method: Ricci traces one text - the Arabic *Book of One Thousand Questions* - from its Arabic original into Javanese, Malay, and Tamil adaptations between the sixteenth and twentieth centuries, using it to map connections among Muslims across South and Southeast Asia ("the Arabic cosmopolis").
2. Chapter 2 is titled "On 'Translation' and Its Untranslatability" - the book directly theorises vernacular Islamic translation as a practice that does not displace Arabic authority. The conclusion is "The Arabic cosmopolis of South and Southeast Asia".
3. Thesis (publisher's description): literary translation and religious conversion were historically interconnected forms of globalization, mutually dependent and creatively reformulated in societies transitioning to Islam.
4. Awards listed by the publisher: AAR Best First Book in the History of Religions (won); Harry J. Benda Prize (won); AAR Award for Excellence - Textual Studies (shortlist).
5. Review excerpts on the publisher page stress Ricci's command of Javanese, Malay, Tamil plus Arabic, Hebrew and Latin sources; a review in *Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde* calls it "a valuable addition to the study of literary production, Islamization, and translation history".
6. A New Zealand Journal of Asian Studies review quoted on the page frames the book as "a salutary corrective both to ideas of indigenous or Southeast Asian 'uniqueness' and of derivative Arabization" - i.e., against both vernacular-isolation and pure-Arabization readings.
