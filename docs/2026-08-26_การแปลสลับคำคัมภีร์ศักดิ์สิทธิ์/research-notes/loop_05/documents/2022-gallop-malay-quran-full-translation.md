- **Author:** Annabel Teh Gallop (British Library; Lead Curator, Southeast Asia)

- **Type:** Institutional blog post (extracted from a forthcoming chapter: Gallop, 'Qur'an manuscripts from Southeast Asia in British collections', in *Malay-Indonesian Islamic studies: a Festschrift in honor of Peter G. Riddell*, ed. Majid Daneshgar and Ervan Nurtawab, Leiden: Brill, 2022)

## Key facts extracted

1. Royal Asiatic Society ms Arabic 4 is described as "the only known single-volume Qur'an with a full interlinear translation in Malay"; it has been fully digitised in honour of Professor Peter Riddell (Riddell 2001; Riddell 2017).
2. Provenance inscription: "Presented by Admiral C. M. Pole, Bart., June 19, 1830". Sir Charles Moris Pole (1757-1830) served on the East Indies Station 1773-1778, the most likely period of acquisition.
3. Physical layout: each page contains nine lines of Arabic in a careful wide hand, with the Malay translation "sloping beneath in a much smaller, very fine, hand"; red surah headings set in rectangular panels; verses separated by small gold roundels outlined in black (a feature noted as very rare in Southeast Asia); laid paper probably of Asian manufacture.
4. Dating: internal evidence (Asian paper, unusual graphic layout, gold verse markers, Pole's service dates) supports an early 18th-century date, when the sultanate of Banten flourished as a centre of Islamic scholarship.
5. Textual identity: the RAS Malay translation is "almost exactly the same" as the identical Malay translations in two 18th-century Banten Qur'ans, PNRI A.51 and W.277 (National Library of Indonesia), studied by Ervan Nurtawab (2020, *Indonesia and the Malay World* 48/141: 169-189, DOI 10.1080/13639811.2020.1724469).
6. Nurtawab's argument (as summarised by Gallop): Qur'an manuscripts with interlinear translations should be regarded as *tafsir* because they were created with explicit intent to explicate meaning; the Banten translations contain interpretive elements and presentation of variant views beyond pure translation, composed independently of 'Abd al-Ra'uf's *Tarjuman al-Mustafid*. A worked example (Q 18:11) shows shared wording plus minor variation between A.51 and RAS Arabic 4, including a gloss "some say their sleep lasted 309 years".
7. Banten concentration: two of the only four other known Qur'an copies with interlinear Malay translations (all multi-volume) come from Banten, as do three of the eleven known Qur'an manuscripts with Javanese (interlinear) translations - highlighting Banten's association with this pedagogical tradition.
8. Chronological frame borrowed from Riddell: mid-17th century Malay world producing Qur'an manuscripts, verse translations, and surah-level exegesis; second half of 17th century the first full Malay commentary (*Tarjuman al-Mustafid* by 'Abd al-Ra'uf, Aceh); the next major identified Malay exegetical work appears ~250 years later (*Tafsir Nur al-Ihsan*, Muhammad Said bin Umar of Kedah, 1925-1927). Interlinear-translation manuscripts fill this long "interregnum".
