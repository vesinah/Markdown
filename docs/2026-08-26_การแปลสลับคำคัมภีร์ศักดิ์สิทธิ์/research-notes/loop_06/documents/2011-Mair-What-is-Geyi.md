# Extract: Victor H. Mair, "What is Geyi, After All?"

- Journal: China Report / journal per SAGE listing journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/000944551104800203 (2011); paywalled - abstract-level verification only
- Status: SEARCH-VERIFIED via SAGE abstract + secondary quotations (Muller DDB entry citing Mair; Sharf quotation). Full text NOT fetched.
- Type: peer-reviewed article

## Key facts seen

- Abstract: geyi 格義 is usually rendered "matching concepts" and treated as a translation technique whereby Buddhists borrowed Daoist terms for Indian ideas; Mair "thoroughly debunks that notion": geyi was actually "a very short-lived attempt to deal with numbered lists that came to China from India in great profusion during the early medieval period", and its misreading is purely a matter of modern scholarship.
- Quoted conclusion (via Wikipedia-cited excerpt seen in results, attributed to Mair): "geyi...was not a translation technique at all but an exegetical method, and that it was by no means restricted exclusively to drawing upon Daoist texts".
- Robert Sharf quoted: ko-i is "a red herring" for sinification studies; recourse to the familiar was ubiquitous throughout Chinese Buddhist history.
- Relevance: corrects the older Wright/Zurcher-era textbook picture of early translators systematically substituting wu 無 for sunyata etc.; important background for assessing claims about early translation strategy before the transliteration-vs-translation debates crystallized in wubufan.
