## Key facts seen in excerpts

- Frames the translator's dilemma for the Lotus Sutra dharani chapter: dharaṇī language sits "on the knife's edge of intelligibility" - some Indic words, some glossolalia-like, no syntax binding them.
- European reception parallels: Burnouf (1852) refused to translate and printed the syllables in Devanagari; Kern (1884) read feminine vocatives as invocations of a great goddess; Hurvitz (1976) declined to translate because meanings are obscure, words "some pure Sanskrit and some just mumbo-jumbo."
- Chinese contrast: the two earliest Lotus translations took opposite approaches - Dharmaraksa (286 CE) TRANSLATED the dharanis word-for-word into Chinese sense-phrases (quoted example: 奇異所思意念無意...), while Kumarajiva transliterated sound only; Jizang later defended transliteration with the argument that "in chanting them the Brahmanical language cannot be altered"; Fachong's Ushnisha-vijaya commentary (per Copp 2012) offered a synesthetic rationale (dharani as water/light/wind/nectar).
- Argues medieval exegetes (Daosheng, Zhiyi, Jizang onward) never settled one authoritative mechanism for dharani efficacy; multiple strategies coexisted; cites Ronald Davidson on historical pragmatics of dharani (attend to surrounding ritual instructions rather than emic claims).
