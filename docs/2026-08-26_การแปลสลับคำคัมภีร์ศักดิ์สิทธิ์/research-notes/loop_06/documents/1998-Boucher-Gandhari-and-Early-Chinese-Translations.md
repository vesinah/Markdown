# Extract: Daniel Boucher, "Gandhari and the Early Chinese Buddhist Translations Reconsidered: The Case of the Saddharmapundarikasutra"

- Journal: Journal of the American Oriental Society 118.4 (Oct-Dec 1998), pp. 471-506
- Status: FULL-FETCHED (full text at buddhism.lib.ntu.edu.tw/FULLTEXT/JR-EPT/daniel.htm)
- Type: peer-reviewed journal article

## Key facts / findings

- Challenges the "Gandhari hypothesis" (Bailey 1946; Brough; Bernhard 1970) that most early Chinese translations derive from written Gandhari originals; argues the fundamentally ORAL/AURAL nature of the Chinese translation process has been underweighted.
- Method: instead of transcriptions, examines mistranslations caused by Prakritic phonological confusions in Dharmaraksa's 286 CE Zheng fahua jing (T263), using Karashima 1992 line-by-line collation.
- Documented confusion types: long/short vowels (bala strong/foolish); aspiration loss g/gh, d/dh (ghanta/gandha; darune/dharma/druma); bhoti/bhonti misread as bodhi; bhuta/buddha; th/d; m/v; double translations where two readings coexist (lokavidu rendered "sage-father of the world" = -vidu + -pitu; svakara/agara "good causes/rooms"; pratyaya-eka-buddha hybrid; yana/jnana "vehicle/knowledge" daohui).
- Conclusion from such evidence: recitation mediated by semilingual intermediaries (e.g., Nie Chengyuan named in colophons); Dharmaraksa fluent per hagiography but the team dynamics show limited Sanskrit grammar on the Chinese side; pronunciation habits possibly Tokharian-influenced.
- Section III reconstructs the Chinese translation process (oral recitation -> transcription -> oral rendering -> drafting -> polishing), citing colophons of the SP translation.
- Source text judged "a very mixed and layered text [...] already in a hybrid language"; calls for studies that "unpack the philological clues contained in these mongrel documents" (pp. 501-503).
- Bechert caution quoted: orthography-based conclusions about pronunciation must be treated skeptically.
