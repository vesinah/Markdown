## Per-source notes

1. Fanyi mingyi ji (FULL-FETCHED): Song glossary by Fayun of Jingde Temple, Suzhou; 7 juan, 64 sections, 2,040+ transliterated entries citing 400+ works; Tiantai-leaning explanations; sections on translators. Its juan-1 entry under Bhagavan preserves the earliest extant full five-bufan text (secret/dharani; polysemy/Bhagavan-six meanings per Fodi lun; no-Chinese-equivalent/jambu tree; deference-to-antiquity/anuttara-samyak-sambodhi since Moteng; reverence/prajna vs shallow zhihui). Zhou Dunyi's 1157 preface quotes it in abridged form.
2. Demieville 1952 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): annotated translation of Dunwu dasheng zhengli jue (Pelliot 4646) + historical commentary + APPENDIX of Indian dossier fragments (Kamalasila Bhavanakrama I analyzed via Chinese version; Bhavanakrama III translated from Tibetan by Lamotte). Council ca. 792-794 at bSam yas; Mo-ho-yen vs Kamalasila; verdict: clash driven by conceptual opposition (subitism vs gradualism), not mere language difference. 1987 corrected reprint; 1984 Chinese translation by Geng Sheng.
3. Wright 1953 (bibliographic): classic chapter on Chinese language and foreign ideas in Studies in Chinese Thought, pp. 286-303; volume indexed under "Chinese language - Translating". Content details UNVERIFIED this session.
4. Zurcher 1959 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): standard history of early Chinese Buddhism; mined early translations as evidence of Luoyang vernacular; rejected some An Shigao attributions later re-examined by Nattier; his geyi definition ("elucidating Buddhist terms, notably numerical categories, with notions from traditional Chinese philosophy") survives Mair's critique as the cautious formulation.
5. Murata 1975 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): earliest rigorous Japanese re-examination; notes Zanning names the doctrine without content; observes Cien zhuan and Xuanzang-team records contain no mention of five bufan; tests categories against Lotus dharani chapters and Heart Sutra mantra; concludes the rule largely reflects old-translation continuity rather than a distinct Xuanzang program; problematizes bufan since sound-copy is itself a form of translation.
6. Boucher 1998 (FULL-FETCHED): oral/aural model of early translation; Dharmaraksa's 286 CE Lotus translation shows Prakritic phonological confusions (bhoti/bodhi, bhuta/buddha, double translations like lokavidu -> "sage-father"); semilingual intermediaries (Nie Chengyuan) mediated recitation; source text a hybrid layered transmission; caution against written-Gandhari assumptions.
7. Boucher 1996 dissertation (SEARCH-VERIFIED): full study of Dharmaraksa's translation idiom; basis for the 1998 article and Asia Major 2006 follow-up.
8. Nattier 2008 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): attribution criteria for Han/Three Kingdoms translations combining external catalogue evidence (Dao'an, Sengyou) and internal lexical/stylistic evidence (transcription habits, prosody); famous reattributions (T361/T362 swap per Harrison); silent-exclusion method; Addenda & Corrigenda maintained by author.
9. Chen Jinhua 2005 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): chu/yichu in CSZJJ means start-of-translation or whole process, not completion, revealing staged workshop procedure (enunciation -> oral translation -> draft -> revision) in the earliest extant East Asian catalogue.
10. Yang & Li 2021 (SEARCH-VERIFIED): corpus test on Vimalakirtinirdesa shows XUANZANG violated all three testable categories of his alleged rule (bhagavan rendered Shizun except at sutra opening/closing), while KUMARAJIVA better fits it; concludes five transliterations are an inaccurate later recapitulation, probably not Xuanzang's own proposal; notes Kukai's complaint about Tang mantra pronunciation fidelity.
11. Overbey 2019 (SEARCH-VERIFIED excerpts): dharaṇī language poised between sense and nonsense; Burnouf/Kern/Hurvitz parallel refusals to translate; Dharmaraksa translated spells word-for-word vs Kumarajiva transliterating; medieval exegesis never settled one mechanism of spell efficacy (Jizang: chanting requires Brahmanical sound unchanged).

## Cross-cutting synthesis (session-grounded)

- The wubufan doctrine as commonly cited (via Zhou Dunyi 1157) is a Southern Song LEXICON entry, not a Tang treatise: no statement of the five categories exists in any work of Xuanzang or his immediate circle (Murata 1975; Yang & Li 2021; Fang Guangchang relaying Cao Shibang 1979). Earliest naming-without-content: Zanning c. 988-1000 era Song gaoseng zhuan.
- Corpus linguistics (Yang & Li 2021) reverses expectation: Kumarajiva conforms to the "Xuanzang" rule better than Xuanzang himself, who reserved transliterations for ritual frame positions (sutra opening/closing) while translating meanings in body text - i.e., selective reverence, not blanket non-translation.
- Transliteration practice had its own history independent of the doctrine: Dharmaraksa TRANSLATED dharanis (286 CE), Kumārajīva first TRANSLITERATED them to preserve sonic efficacy (406 CE), Xuanzang and Amoghavajra produced further transcriptions; only the oldest Zheng fahua jing translated them among Lotus versions (Brill ch.; Murata; Overbey).
- Authority of the Sanskrit original operated on two registers: sonic efficacy of retained Indic sounds (mantra/dharani must not be altered in chanting - Jizang; Kukai's complaints about phonetic drift) and philological fidelity to increasingly hybrid Middle Indic sources (Boucher; Karashima-based studies).
- Bilingual glossaries institutionalized Sanskrit authority: from yinyie phonetic works (Xuanying, Huilin, referenced inside Fayun) to Fayun's Fanyi mingyi ji (2,040+ headwords, variant renderings, translator sections) - the very genre that preserved and canonized wubufan.
- Translation procedure was workshop-based and oral/aural: recitation by foreign monk, mediation by semi-bilingual intermediaries, drafting and polishing by Chinese literati (Boucher colophon evidence; Chen Jinhua on chu/yichu stages).
