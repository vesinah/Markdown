## Abstract (as published on the Helsinki portal)

"This chapter gives an overview of the main issues concerning the Syrohexapla. It discusses what is known about its origin and what can be said about its Greek source text(s) based on both the colophons and on textual studies. The chapter looks at the Syrohexapla first as a translation in its own right reflecting the features of 'mirror translation' and the translation's original purpose. However, for LXX scholars the Syrohexapla is particularly interesting for its connection to the Hexapla. Therefore the chapter summarizes the research on the Syrohexapla's text and on the marginalia's role and value as text-critical witnesses to the Hexapla and to the Hexaplaric recension."

## Related session evidence from the ORA (Oxford Research Archive) PDF surfaced in search results

- "The Syrohexapla (Syh) is the Syriac rendering of Origen's revised Greek Septuagint text, carried out in 616/7 CE near Alexandria (the Enaton). The figure associated with this major undertaking is that of Paul, bishop of Tella, but this does not exclude the possibility that other translators were [involved]."
- The translator rendered the revised LXX text "replete with text-critical (Aristarchan) signs that marked adjustments to the form of Exodus found among Jews."
- Well-known Peshitta renderings were retained in the Syh for familiar items; for less common items Paul of Tella "may have been the first to try to render the Greek names into Syriac," with alternative readings of Aquila, Symmachus, and Theodotion placed in the margins after being given distinct Syriac renderings.
- The Harklean New Testament is described as emerging "from the same milieu and period" as Syh (616 CE at the Enaton), likewise aspiring "to be a very close rendering of the Greek biblical text."

## Relevance to loop topic

Frames the Syro-Hexapla as a "mirror translation": a deliberately subservient rendering whose purpose was access to the authoritative Greek original plus its variant apparatus in a language Syriac readers could parse, i.e., vernacular access without displacing the sacred-language original.
