## Key facts (session-verified)

- "The first use of Coptic, once it established itself as a stable linguistic medium and achieved the status of a literary language, was to translate the Scriptures from Greek, an effort that is now almost universally regarded as an identity operation, rather than a means of making biblical works comprehensible to those who did not have access to Greek (Funk 2012)."
- This demonstrates "the extent to which the identity of late antique Egypt was bicultural and bilingual, as any form of appropriation implies comprehension."
- Scripture was translated "in a first phase, more or less concurrently, in different places and into several dialects: Sahidic, Bohairic, Akhmimic, Lycopolitan, Oxyrhynchite (Mesokemic), and Fayyumic, with Sahidic likely having a slight temporal precedence (third/fourth century)."
- In the fifth century a new phase produced the standard Sahidic Bible. A parallel Bohairic operation was promoted in the seventh/eighth century by the Monastery of Saint Macarius; these later translations were made directly from Greek without Sahidic intermediation and based on a Vorlage from a different manuscript tradition; they carry longer titles and are more faithful to the Greek tradition.
- "There are no Coptic manuscripts that contain the complete Bible"; NT fully preserved in Sahidic and Bohairic via integrated fragments; OT survival estimated 70% Sahidic, 60% Bohairic (Takla).
- "Deciding on what was useful to translate and what should be left in its original language was a creative activity in itself" — some Greek works were deliberately left untranslated while others were appropriated.
- Shenoute of Atripe elevated Coptic to the rank of literary language for original composition; his corpus was arranged under his direction with apparent prohibition on dissemination outside his federation.
- The last phase of Coptic literature reworked texts "for the preservation and defense of Coptic cultural and religious identity, and also for liturgical use," coinciding with pervasive Arabic — "an extreme and anachronistic resistance in an Egypt which, by then, had become predominantly arabophone."

## Relevance to loop topic

Directly theorizes translation as identity operation rather than mere comprehension aid — a counterweight to purely functional accounts of vernacular access to sacred-language originals.
