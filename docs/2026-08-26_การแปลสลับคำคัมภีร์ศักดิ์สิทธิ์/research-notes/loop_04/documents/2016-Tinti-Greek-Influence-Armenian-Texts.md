## Key facts (session-verified)

- Distinction between the earliest "Classical" translations from Syriac and Greek ("aimed to satisfy the immediate needs of the Armenian Church... Bible, literature concerning ritual and Church history, commentaries, apologetic, canonical, and hagiographical works") and Hellenising translations ("theological, philosophical, grammatical, rhetorical, and generally specialised writings... a conscious effort of transferring Greek scholarly knowledge into an Armenian context").
- Strict adherence to the letter makes some Hellenising texts "useful witnesses for reconstructing the underlying Greek text, even when the latter is lost, but are often so obscure as to be virtually incomprehensible to anyone who is not familiar with Greek."
- On interlinearity: "some versions display a remarkable adherence to their source, so much so that, especially in some passages, the text actually looks like an interlinear translation from Greek. However, that seems to be more the exception than the rule."
- LEWY (1936: 16): even "'interlinear' versions initially conceived as school aids could eventually have achieved a greater degree of autonomy than their authors expected, being actually understood by Armenians and studied without reference to the original Greek text." PARAMELLE (1984: 69): such versions soon became incomprehensible, explaining textual corruptions and emergence of scholia.
- TERIAN (1982: 175): the "Hellenising School" name "stands for a school of translators," with no proof a structured movement existed; prototypical production dated roughly mid-5th century to first three decades of the 8th century.
- Footnote 18 documents oral sacred-language access before written translation: the earlier technique reflected "the practice of reading the sacred texts in Greek or Syriac and simultaneously translating them into Armenian during the liturgy" (lecture hétéro-épique; citing SARKISSIAN 1993: 201), transitioning toward techniques influenced by the written page.
- The Psalms, most used in liturgy, "would have been known almost by heart to Armenian authors, many of whom were clergymen"; the Bible itself exerted linguistic influence on original Armenian writing.

## Corroborating session sources on the Armenian Bible

- Jinbachian (2004, UBS PDF): before Mesrop's alphabet, "the languages of instruction in these schools and the Church liturgy [were] in Greek or Syriac"; Lazar P'arpec'i on the clergy: "Wasted their days in learning the Syriac language... Hence, the Armenians could understand nothing"; Koriwn reports Mesrop invented the alphabet in Edessa after prayer and fasting, began translating at Samosata starting with Proverbs; two-stage model — first translation (possibly from Syriac) before Ephesus 431, then thorough revision based on Greek manuscripts brought from Constantinople; the 22 canonical OT books correspond to the number of Hebrew letters; deuterocanonicals added after 431.
- ISBE article: Scripture read "in some places in Greek, in others in Syriac, and translated orally to the people"; final version completed c. 434-436 from Septuagint per Origen's Hexapla; La Croze styled it "Queen of versions."
- L'Harmattan open-access chapter: initial translation c. 406 as an official undertaking under Maštoc' with oversight of Sahak; Syriac prototype detected in Gospels, Psalter, Ecclesiasticus, Hebrews; most books reflect the Lucianic/Antiochene Greek text type.

## Relevance to loop topic

Armenia shows the full hieroglossic sequence: foreign sacred languages (Greek/Syriac) with oral vernacular glossing; national alphabet and canonical translation; then hyper-literal interlinear-style translations used as keys back to the Greek original.
