## Key claims (session-verified)

- "Boris Uspenskij assumes that the 'Third South Slavic Influence', which began in the 1640s, resulted in the speedy decline of diglossia in Russia and its replacement with bilingualism."
- Under Uspenskij's model, "While in diglossia H and L serve as varieties of one language, in bilingualism Church Slavonic is a foreign language for Russian speakers."
- Bunčić's counter-thesis: "Church Slavonic remained a variety of the Russian language with high prestige until the 1760s or 1780s," evidenced "among other things" by "Lomonosov's inclusion of Church Slavonic into his Theory of the Three Styles."
- The second half of the 17th century and first half of the 18th century are a transitional period "during which the essential features of diglossia were preserved, but the distribution of H and L gradually turned from a strictly diaphasic model into a predominantly diastratic one." Bunčić names this situation "diastratic diglossia."

- Keipert also lists Zhivov: "Rol' russkogo tserkovnoslavianskogo v istorii slavianskikh literaturnykh iazykov" (1988, 49-98); Iazyk i kul'tura v Rossii XVIII veka (1996); "O tserkovnoslavianskom iazyke" in Pletneva & Kravetsky, Tserkovno-slavianskii iazyk (1996, 12-23).
- Zhivov, Language and Culture in Eighteenth-Century Russia (Academic Studies Press): traces growth of the vernacular literary language "from the 'hybrid Slavonic' of the late seventeenth century through the debates between 'archaists and innovators'"; chapters cover Petrine language reform, normalization via the Theory of Three Styles, the "slavenorossiiskii language," and "secularization of Slavonicisms."

## Relevance to loop topic

Supplies the sociolinguistic model (Uspenskij's diglossia; Bunčić's refinement) under which Church Slavonic functioned as a hierogloss-like sacred register within Russia, with a hierarchy of styles mediating between sacred bookish language and vernacular.
