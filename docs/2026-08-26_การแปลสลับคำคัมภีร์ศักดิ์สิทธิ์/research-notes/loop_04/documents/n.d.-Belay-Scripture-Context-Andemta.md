## Key facts (session-verified)

- "The Ethiopian andəmta interpretive tradition uses Scripture in dialogue with the Ethiopian context through translating Ethiopic (Ge'ez) into Amharic and presenting different interpretations and applications of the text."
- The andəmta corpus "derives its name from the repeated use of 'andəm,' meaning 'and (there is) one (who says),' to introduce different interpretations" (cited from Lee 2011, 40).
- "The Ethiopian Church has possessed Scripture in the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) language since the sixth century (Ullendorff 1997, 33)." "The coming of the Nine Saints after Frumentius facilitated the translation of the Bible from Greek to Ethiopic (Ge'ez)," though dating is debated.
- Two-step method: first directly translating Ge'ez into Amharic faithfully; second explaining via cross-reference to other texts and stories. The mode of exegesis "is an Amharic commentary on the Ethiopic (Ge'ez) text... containing many alternative comments as options to give meaning to different horizons."
- Interpreters are called "the four-eyed" because they consider different horizons of readers; interpreters "stick strongly to the source text."

## Corroborating session sources on the andəmta

- ebrary.net chapter ("The EOTC's exegetical tradition"): the andəmta has "conventionally been given prioritisation" over Church Fathers in the traditional curriculum; students master the Gospels andəmta before patristic study; described as "a translation and clarification of the Ge'ez texts of Biblical, certain patristic and liturgical books"; format = "the Ge'ez is followed by Amharic headings (direct translation) and then by the commentary or explanation (fiṭṭata)"; commentators employ story-telling, direct quotations, explanation, conditional "if" sentences; corpus of four parts (Old Testament, New Testament, Books of the Scholars, Books of Monks); generally unattributed — attributed to the Holy Spirit ("Our andəmta has no references, no names"); considered unalterable; one teacher reports "for one verse you may find more than ten levels of interpretation... there is a permanent interpretation beyond which no one can pass"; Cowley found a commentator may say 'and one' up to 16 times.
- Roger Pearse blog (2012): "The Andemta commentary is an explanation in Amharic of passages in the Ethiopian biblical, patristic and liturgical books, themselves written in Geez. The commentary does discuss textual variants and emendations... The Geez OT is based on the Septuagint, rather than the Hebrew text." Points to British Library Endangered Archives project 336 digitizing lay bet exegetical tradition codices (70-75 codices) in Eastern Gojjam and Southern Gondar.
- Journal of Jewish Studies article (Sage, DOI 10.1177/0951820714528628): The Ethiopic Andəmta Commentary on 1 Enoch 6-9; "The commentary tradition reflects the outlook of seventeenth-century Gondar... although it undoubtedly contains much older material"; includes related commentary on Ps 82(81).7 with a Midrash ha-Gadol parallel.
- Academia.edu thesis abstract (Andəmta on Genesis): standardized pattern "text, translation, and commentary"; up to 15 successive interpretations for a single text; prominence "from the Gondärine period (16th-18th centuries AD)"; formalization placed around 832 E.C. under King Dəgnažän, with contributions from Mämhər Esdros.

## Relevance to loop topic

Ethiopia's flagship case: Ge'ez Bible preserved as fixed sacred original; Amharic andəmta supplies vernacular translation-plus-commentary access with multiple authorized interpretations, taught before any other exegesis.
