## Key facts (session-verified)

- "Sixteenth century psalter texts are amongst the oldest literary evidence in (old) Romanian which survive today... they fall within a specific category of textual witnesses: non-original literary texts, which are the result of a word-by-word translation from what was then considered an authoritative source."
- "Most biblical texts copied or printed in Romanian in the 16th century are thought to go back to Slavonic sources, used both for translating and revising religious texts."
- Church Slavonic psalter tradition: by the 11th century at least three distinct text varieties existed, with at least three more added around the 14th century; scholars Francis Thomson and Catherine Mary MacRobert distinguish Redactions I-V. "Redaction III became standard in South Slavonic early printed Psalters, e.g. the Cetinje and Goražde Psalters, [while] the East Slavonic printed tradition started from Redaction V."
- In parallel with psalter-text redactions, "translations of commentated Psalters were made as well: the commentaries of Hesychius (also known as the Pseudo-Athanasian commentary) and of Theodoret of Cyrrhus."
- Greek nominalized infinitives caused persistent translation problems across centuries: early redactions preferred finite constructions introduced by вънегда and да; from Redaction III onward these were replaced by infinitival constructions as literal translations of the Greek.
- Finding: "Redaction III was employed for the translation of the psalter text into Old Romanian," supporting derivation from a Slavonic source identifiable with Redaction III.
- Textual genealogy: PH (Hurmuzaki) on one hand and PS, PC1, PCb on the other "belong to two different textual traditions"; whether they trace back to one ancestral translation or two redactions remains unresolved. The study covers PH, PS, Coresi's bilingual 1577 psalter, and the c. 1588 edition, with Voroneț considered marginally due to lacunae.

## Relevance to loop topic

Shows word-by-word translation technique flowing hieroglossically from Greek through Church Slavonic into Romanian, including a commentated-psalter strand parallel to the plain text — the same layered access pattern seen in the Syro-Hexapla margins and the andəmta commentary.
