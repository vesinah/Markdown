## Per-source notes (concrete facts)

### 1. Salvesen — Syro-Hexapla (GEDSH)
Origen's Hexapla was a six-column synopsis (Hebrew, Greek transcription, Septuagint, Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion) with text-critical signs marking the LXX column against rabbinic Hebrew; the original kept at Caesarea likely perished mid-7th century and only 9th-10th-century Psalm folios remain. Paul (Pawlos) of Tella translated the Hexaplaric LXX column literally into Syriac in 616-17, adding short readings from the other Greek versions; because Greek copies dropped Origen's signs, the Syro-Hexapla is a primary witness to Hexaplaric material and to fragments of Aquila/Symmachus/Theodotion. Reception was mainly West Syrian, with rediscovery by Timotheos I, use by Isho'dad of Merv, influence on Ya'qub of Edessa's OT revision, and occasional readings leaking into Peshitta manuscripts.

### 2. Liljeström — The Syrohexapla (Oxford Handbook chapter)
The chapter treats origin and Greek source(s) via colophons and textual study, characterizes the translation as "mirror translation," and assesses marginalia as witnesses to the Hexapla and Hexaplaric recension. The ORA excerpt confirms execution in 616/7 CE at the Enaton near Alexandria, with Paul bishop of Tella as associated figure but possible other translators; the Exodus translator handled Origen's Aristarchan signs, retained known Peshitta terms, coined new Syriac renderings for unfamiliar cultic items, and placed differentiated renderings of Aquila/Symmachus/Theodotion in margins. The Harklean NT arose from the same Enaton milieu with the same hyper-close aspiration to the Greek.

### 3. Robert — Hieroglossia
Defines hieroglossia as relations between a founding language (hierogloss) and languages ontologically/theologically dependent on it (laoglosses), which either borrow massively or rebuild their conceptual vocabulary upon the model tongue; laogloss innovation counts as exegesis of the hieroglossic original rather than true novelty. Explicitly lists Christian hieroglossic traditions "Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, and even Slavonic" plus Ethiopia among Eurasian cases; developed from Japanese Buddhism (Sanskrit-Chinese-Japanese). Offers ten research categories including exegesis in another language, vernacular access to sacrality, and purism. Attribution note: session sources credit Jean-Noël Robert with coining the term; Uspenskij's verified contribution is the diglossia framework (see note 4).

### 4. Bunčić — Diastratic diglossia
Reports Uspenskij's thesis that the Third South Slavic Influence (from the 1640s) dissolved Russian diglossia into bilingualism, making Church Slavonic effectively foreign. Bunčić argues Church Slavonic remained a high-prestige variety of Russian into the 1760s-1780s, citing Lomonosov's inclusion of Slavonic in the Theory of Three Styles; late 17th-early 18th century = transition from diaphasic to diastratic distribution ("diastratic diglossia"). Session context adds Keipert's bibliography of Uspenskij's works (Kratkij ocherk 1994 etc.) and Zhivov's Language and Culture in Eighteenth-Century Russia (hybrid Slavonic -> slavenorossiiskii -> secularization of Slavonicisms), plus the 1990s Kochetkov liturgical-language dispute where traditionalists called Slavonic "our verbal icon."

### 5. Camară — Slavonic sources of the oldest Romanian psalters
Sixteenth century yields four manuscript psalters (Hurmuzaki ca. 1491-1516; Voroneț 1551-1558; Scheian 1573-1578; Moldavian 1573-1585) and four prints (Săulescu c. 1568 lost; 1570; 1577; 1589) — the oldest Romanian language monuments. Version A behind Hurmuzaki+Voroneț derives from a Slavonic psalter related to the Belgrade Psalter (13th c.), later revised toward the Oxford Serbian Psalter (14th c.) to yield version B behind Scheian, Moldavian, and the prints; Psalm 110's title in PH interprets Hallelujah like the Vienna Psalter gloss, proving use of a commentated Slavonic psalter. Crucially for interlinear practice: PH contains crossed-out Slavonic sequences that Mareș attributed to the copyist drawing on "the Slavonic interlinear text" of a bilingual antigraph.

### 6. Burlacu — Infinitivals Greek-Slavonic-Romanian
Sixteenth-century Romanian psalters are word-by-word translations from an authoritative source (Church Slavonic); MacRobert's redactional framework RI-V shows Greek nominalized infinitives rendered finitely early and infinitivally from Redaction III onward, and the Romanian tradition follows Redaction III (the South Slavonic standard of Cetinje/Goražde prints). Commentated psalters (Hesychius/Pseudo-Athanasius; Theodoret) circulated in parallel. PH versus PS/PC1/PCb form two textual traditions whose common ancestor remains undetermined.

### 7. roPsalt project page
Confirms no consensus on who initiated the Romanian translation (Orthodox, Hussite, Catholic, Reformer candidacies), a Church Slavonic prototype "yet unidentified" with multiple revisions, four manuscripts and three printed survivors, several bilingual; gives shelf marks (PH ms. Rom. 3077; PV ms. Rom. 693; PS ms. Rom. 449; PC ms. Rom. 3465; prints CRV-16/19/78/19A). The Voroneț Psalter layout per the search-surfaced description: Slavonic phrase first, then Romanian rendering.

### 8. Coresi prints
The 1570 Brașov Romanian Psalter (printed 6 Feb - 27 May, initiative of Reformed bishop Pavel Tordași) carries Coresi's epilogue lamenting that all languages have God's word except Romanians, quoting 1 Cor 14 on five intelligible words. The 1577 Slavo-Romanian Psalter repeats the epilogue with added purpose clause ("sa va fie de intelegatura si gramaticilor") — extracted from the Serbian Slavonic psalter into Romanian; the crestinortodox study ties bilingual editions to priests' declining command of Church Slavonic, and the Uadventus study states the bilinguals were printed "to teach the Slavonic language through Romanian." Șerban Coresi issued a second bilingual edition c. 1589 dependent on 1577; activity 1559-1583 under Benckner's patronage.

### 9. Feder — Coptic OT versions update
Oldest stratum: Coptic glosses added to Greek biblical texts (BM EA 10825 Greek-Coptic glossary to Hosea-Amos, Mesokemic, 3rd-4th c., verso of a c. 200 CE land register). Sahidic Standard Translation (later 4th c.) became the canonical Bible of native Egyptian Christians during the 5th century; pandect Coptic bibles never existed; dialect versions split short-lived vs long-lived. Classical Bohairic probably created early 7th c. (bridged by Kellia inscriptions), became the Church's only Coptic Bible from the 12th c. alongside Arabic, with a reduced OT canon and lectionary Wisdom/Sirach pericopes transposed from Sahidic Vorlagen; CoptOT blog adds White Monastery scribes translating Bohairic lectionary passages directly from Sahidic while Coptic knowledge declined but texts stayed read aloud.

### 10. Buzi — Coptic literature update
Scripture translation into Coptic is "almost universally regarded as an identity operation, rather than a means of making biblical works comprehensible to those who did not have access to Greek" (Funk 2012). Multiple dialect translations ran concurrently with slight Sahidic precedence; the 7th/8th-c. St Macarius Bohairic project translated directly from Greek with longer titles and greater fidelity to Greek tradition; choosing what to translate and what to leave in Greek was itself creative. Late Coptic production served preservation of religious identity and liturgy amid arabophone Egypt.

### 11. Belay (+ corroborating andəmta sources) — Andəmta tradition
Andəmta names an Amharic commentary corpus (four sections: OT, NT, Books of Scholars, Books of Monks) generated by repeated 'andəm' ("and there is one who says") introducing alternative interpretations after a direct Amharic heading translation of the fixed Ge'ez text followed by fiṭṭata explanation. The Ethiopian church has possessed Ge'ez scripture since the sixth century (Nine Saints, post-Frumentius; Ge'ez OT based on the LXX). It is generally unattributed — credited to the Holy Spirit — treated as unalterable, allows up to ten-plus (Cowley: sixteen) interpretive layers per verse, and precedes patristic study in the curriculum; endangered lay bet codices (70-75) of Eastern Gojjam/Southern Gondar are being digitized (BL EAP 336). Prominence tied to the Gondärine period (16th-18th c.).

### 12. Getatchew Haile profile cluster
Foremost Ge'ez scholar, 150+ publications, MacArthur Fellow (1988), first African recipient of the Edward Ullendorff Medal; principal cataloger of EMML from exile (joined project 1973), describing thousands of manuscripts in ten published catalogues including the oldest texts of virtually every book of the Ethiopic OT and the complete Enoch. Special focus: mid-15th-c. controversies of Zar'a Ya'qob and the Estifanosites. Festschrift: Studies in Ethiopian Languages, Literature, and History, ed. McCollum, Aethiopistische Forschungen 83 (Harrassowitz 2017) with Bausi-Rave bibliography; posthumous Aethiopica 24 (2021) piece edits an Aksumite/Zagwe-period Matthew-commentary fragment (EMML 7229 fols 122r-123v).

### 13. Tinti (+ Jinbachian corroborations) — Armenian case
Pre-alphabet Armenia heard scripture in Greek or Syriac with simultaneous oral Armenian translation in liturgy (lecture hétéro-épique, Sarkissian via Tinti fn. 18; P'arpec'i on unintelligible Syriac); Mesrop's alphabet (Edessa, c. 405) enabled an official translation begun at Samosata with Proverbs and revised after Greek manuscripts arrived post-431 (Jinbachian; ISBE dates completion c. 434-436 from LXX per Origen's Hexapla; Syriac prototypes detected in Gospels/Psalter/Ecclesiasticus/Hebrews). Hellenising school translations (mid-5th to early 8th c.) are so literal they "look like an interlinear translation from Greek," useful for recovering lost Greek but nearly incomprehensible without it; Lewy notes such school-aid interlinears gained autonomous life; Paramelle links their obscurity to corruption and scholia growth. ### 14. Zhivov/Uspensky context (folded into notes 4 and table)

## Cross-cutting observations (session-grounded)

1. Four distinct vernacular-access technologies recur across traditions: columnar synopsis with variant apparatus (Hexapla -> Syro-Hexapla margins; Harklean NT), interlinear/literal service glossing (Armenian Hellenising aids; Slavonic interlinear layer behind Hurmuzaki), alternating/bilingual page layouts in print (Voroneț layout; Coresi 1577/1589), and commentary-as-translation (andəmta).
2. Translation is repeatedly framed as exegesis of an untouchable original rather than replacement of it: Robert's definition; Funk/Buzi's identity operation; the andəmta's Holy-Spirit attribution and immutability; Coresi's otveat framing.
3. Access vernaculars themselves sacralize over time (Bohairic displacing Sahidic; Church Slavonic's three-style prestige), producing stacked hieroglossia (Ge'ez -> Amharic; Slavonic -> Romanian; Greek -> Coptic -> Arabic).
4. Declining clerical command of the sacred language is an explicitly documented driver of bilingual apparatus (Coresi's 1577 epilogue context; CoptOT White Monastery observations).
5. Commentated source-texts travel with translations: commentated Slavonic Psalter behind Hurmuzaki (Ps 110 title), Theodoret/Hesychius catena tradition, andəmta's multi-layer interpretation — apparatus preservation parallels Origen's signs in Syh.
