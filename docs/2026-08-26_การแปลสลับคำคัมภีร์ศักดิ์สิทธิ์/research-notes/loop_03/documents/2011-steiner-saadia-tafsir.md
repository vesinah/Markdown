## Extracts (publisher description, verbatim)
- "In his youth, R. Saadia Gaon (882-942 CE) dreamed of publishing a new translation of the Torah for Arabic-speaking Jews to replace the overly literal ones in vogue at the time. It would be a proper translation, conforming to the tenets of both traditional Judaism and contemporary philosophy - not to mention the canons of Arabic grammar and style."
- "Saadia's interest in this project was not purely academic. Rabbinic Judaism was under attack from Karaite and Muslim polemicists eager to win new converts, and Saadia felt that a new Arabic version of the Torah was needed to counter the attack. His dream was realized with the issuing of the Tafsir, the most important Jewish Bible translation of the Middle Ages."
- "Steiner traces the history of the Tafsir - its ancient and medieval roots, its modest beginnings, its subsequent evolution, and its profound impact on the history of biblical exegesis."
- Manuscript sources named: "a Christian Arabic translation of the Pentateuch from St. Catherine's Monastery in the Sinai Desert" (argued to be Hunayn b. Ishaq's "lost" ninth-century Arabic version, whose philosophical rendering of Genesis 1:1 Saadia later adopted), and "a Judeo-Arabic annotated translation of Genesis from the Cairo Genizah" (argued to be "a page from a copy of the first edition of the Tafsir prepared while Saadia was a student in Tiberias").

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- Steiner's account makes the Tafsir a deliberate replacement project aimed at pre-existing overly literal Judaeo-Arabic versions — i.e., comprehensibility was contested within Arabic-speaking Jewry itself.
- The apologetic driver is explicit: countering Karaite and Muslim polemic, making the Arabic tafsir an instrument of rabbinic authority, not merely convenience.
- The book's evidence base (Genizah + Sinai manuscripts) shows how textual criticism reconstructs the drafting history of an authoritative vernacular translation.
