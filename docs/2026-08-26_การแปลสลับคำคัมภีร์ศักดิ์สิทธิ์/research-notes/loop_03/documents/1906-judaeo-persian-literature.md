## Extracts

Antiquity claims and lost versions:
- "Maimonides, in the 'Iggeret Teman,' refers to the fact that the Pentateuch was translated into Persian several centuries before Mohammed."
- "the Syrian bishop Theodoret, in the fifth century, mentions a Persian Bible translation which existed in his day... This translation must have been in Pahlavi, but it has completely disappeared."

The 1319 manuscript:
- "There are, however, manuscript translations of the Pentateuch that are centuries older than that of Jacob ben Joseph Tawus, which was printed in the sixteenth century. Joseph b. Moses, the writer of MS. Brit. Mus. Or. 5446, which contains the Pentateuch, finished his work on the 24th Adar, 1319. He was probably also the translator."
- "After this comes... a translation contained in the Vatican, Paris, and St. Petersburg manuscripts of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries... Moreover, it is closely connected with the Targum of Onkelos ('Paris Cat.' p. 7)." Guidi judged on linguistic grounds it was made "in Kurdistan or in one of the border provinces," though the Vatican manuscript came from Laristan.

Printed tradition:
- "The third translation chronologically is that... by Jacob ben Joseph Tawus, published in the so-called Constantinople Polyglot (1546) and incorporated, in Persian transcription with Latin translation by Thomas Hyde (1657), in vol. iv. of the London Polyglot."
- Simeon Hakam of Jerusalem published "a carefully punctuated translation of the Pentateuch" in five volumes (Jerusalem, 1901-03); "in order to satisfy the adherents of the traditional translation, he very often inserts in brackets and in smaller print the rendering of certain words as approved by the traditions of Bokhara."
- "Simeon's statement as to the lack of written Bible translations among the Persian Jews is confirmed by the fact that Adler's collection contains only one manuscript of the Pentateuch (B. 61), dated 1776."

Lexical glosses:
- "Lexical glosses accompanying the Bible text were especially popular among the Persian-speaking Jews as an aid to the study of the Bible." Examples: Joseph b. Moses cites a "Master Abu Sa'id" who wrote explanations of difficult words in all twenty-four books; Adler B.1 "Perush ha-Millot, Persian translation of the difficult words and passages of the Bible"; B.49 "A vocabulary of difficult words in the Pentateuch with explanations in Persian."
- The 1319 translation "is in individual passages accompanied by explanations in Persian."

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- Judaeo-Persian scripture translation is documented as a chain: pre-Islamic/Pahlavi-era claims, a dated 1319 copy (BL Or.5446) whose scribe was likely also the translator, fifteenth-sixteenth-century manuscripts close to Targum Onkelos, then print (Constantinople polyglot 1546; London Polyglot 1657 via Hyde).
- The glossary habit (perush ha-millot) shows comprehension delivered word-by-word against Hebrew lemmata, paralleling Ladino le'azim glossaries.
- The Bukhara traditional renderings inserted in smaller print in Simeon Hakam's edition show an oral community standard coexisting with — and constraining — the written translation.
