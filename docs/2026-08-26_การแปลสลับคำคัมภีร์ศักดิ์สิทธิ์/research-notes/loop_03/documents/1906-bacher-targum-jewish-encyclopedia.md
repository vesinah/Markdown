## Extracts

On the office and its holder:
- "The professional translator of the text of the Bible in the synagogue was called 'targeman' ('torgeman,' 'metorgeman'; the common pronunciation being Meturgeman; see Meg. iv. 4). His duties naturally formed part of the functions of the communal official ('sofer') who had charge of Biblical instruction (see Yer. Meg. 74d)."
- "Early in the fourth century Samuel ben Isaac, upon entering a synagogue, once saw a teacher ('sofer') read the Targum from a book, and bade him desist. This anecdote shows that there was a written Targum which was used for public worship in that century in Palestine, although there was no definitely determined and generally recognized Targum, such as existed in Babylonia."

Performance rules:
- "The reader was forbidden to prompt the translator, lest any one should say that the Targum was included in the text of the Bible (Ulla in Meg. 32a)."
- Judah ben Ilai: "whosoever rendered a verse of the Bible in its original form was a liar, while he who made additions was a blasphemer (Tosef., Meg., end; Kid. 49a)."
- "In Sifre, Deut. 161 the Targum is mentioned as a branch of study intermediate between the Mikra and the Mishnah."
- "Excepting the Scroll of Esther... only one person might read the Targum, as the Pentateuch or prophetic section also was read by a single person. Even a minor might read the Targum..."
- Passages read but not translated (e.g., Gen. 35:22) or neither read nor translated (Num. 6:24-26; II Sam. 11-13).

Text history:
- "The Pentateuchal Targum, which was made the official Targum of the Babylonian schools, was at all events committed to writing and redacted as early as the third century, since its Masorah dates from the first half of that century."
- Attribution: "The Targum to the Pentateuch was composed by the proselyte Onkelos at the dictation of R. Eliezer and R. Joshua" (Meg. 3a); the Prophets targum attributed to Jonathan b. Uzziel; "no authorized Targum to the Hagiographa" since this portion furnished no sidrot for public worship.
- Disuse: anecdotes of reprehended erroneous translations (y. Ber. 9c on Lev. 22:28; y. Bik. 65d on Ex. 12:8 etc.).

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- Bacher's article is the classic consolidated account of the meturgeman office: an official function attached to the communal sofer, performed orally from memory, with the written scroll kept visually separate from the translation.
- The prohibition against reading the Targum from a book in public worship, and the rule that reader may not prompt the translator, are both mechanisms for keeping translation clearly distinct from scripture — authority is protected by maintaining the boundary.
- R. Judah b. Ilai's dictum ("liar ... blasphemer") defines the targumic middle path: neither pure literalism nor free addition.
- The article also documents the shift from Palestinian plurality of targums to a single official Babylonian redaction (Onkelos) with its own Masorah by the third century.
