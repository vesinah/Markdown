## Extracts

Scale and genre:
- "The Ze'enah U-Re'enah is the most popular Yiddish book ever published, having been through at least 275 printings since its first, in the beginning of the seventeenth century. To date, two hundred and forty editions in Yiddish, and thirty-four full and partial translations, paraphrases, and summaries, in seven languages, have been identified."
- "The Ze'enah U-Re'enah is not a 'Yiddish translation of the Humash written for women.' Rather, it is a sophisticated anthological commentary that draws on more than a dozen of the major medieval Jewish biblical commentaries and the whole corpus of Talmudic and Midrashic literature."

Author and title:
- Author: "Rabbi Jacob ben Isaac Rabbino of Yanov," died spring 1623; six leading rabbis (first two signatories Samuel Edels [Maharsha] and Joel Sirkes [Bach]) issued an approbation at the Council of the Four Lands meeting at the Lublin fair in 1623.
- "The correct title of the book is Hamisha Humshe Torah 'im kol ha-haftaot bi-leshon Ashkenaz [Five Books of the Torah in the Language of Ashkenaz]"; "Ze'enah U-Re'enah" (Song of Songs 3:11) is a subtitle.
- 1622 title page (Basel/Hanau): "...in every Torah portion... all of their words that are closest to the plain meaning of the text and brought them into this work in the language of Ashkenaz. Since the usefulness of this work for men and women..."

Audience and structure:
- Intended audience stated explicitly as "'Anashim v'e nashim,' men and women."
- On layout of early editions (e.g., 1711 Amsterdam): "there are no chapter and verse citations. The commentary is not divided by verse, comment, or paragraph. It is one running paragraph... Each comment begins with the first word or two of the sentence or phrase being commented upon." "It also assumes that the reader knows the text of the Humash, since the commentary does not translate or paraphrase the text of the Humash."

Relation to shnayim mikra:
- "There is a tradition that one should review weekly Torah portion, twice in Hebrew and once in 'Targum.'... However, Rabbi David ha-Levi of Lvov [Lemberg], author of the most important Ashkenazi commentary on the Orah Hayyim section of the Shulhan Arukh, writes in his commentary that a man who is not fluent in Aramaic may substitute a Yiddish work on the Humash, 'like the Ze'enah U-Re'enah.'"

Publication history:
- Three lost editions, Krakow and Lublin, first decade of the 1600s; earliest surviving edition Basel/Hanau 1622 (two volumes; two copies known: JTS New York and Bodleian Oxford); stable text through the eighteenth century; first East European edition Shklov 1786; major nineteenth-century textual corruptions (Chone Shmeruk); Frankfurt University Library digitized twenty-three pre-1800 editions.

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- The work's mechanism is Hebrew-verse-anchored Yiddish commentary: Hebrew lemmata open each Yiddish discourse, which mixes translation with midrashic and commentarial material — an interleave rather than a plain translation.
- Faierstein documents that halakhic authority explicitly admitted this Yiddish work as a substitute for Aramaic targum in shnayim mikra review (Rabbi David ha-Levi), making the Tsenerene a functional heir of the meturgeman for Ashkenaz.
- Turniansky's essay (My Jewish Learning, reprinted from JWA) adds independently: oldest extant edition 1622 Hanau (frontispiece says Basel), over 210 editions, sources dominated by Midrash (esp. Bereshit Rabbah), Talmud, Rashi and interpreters, Bahya ben Asher; preference for peshat combined with derash; haftarot rendered as consecutive translation with sporadic Hebrew quotations.
