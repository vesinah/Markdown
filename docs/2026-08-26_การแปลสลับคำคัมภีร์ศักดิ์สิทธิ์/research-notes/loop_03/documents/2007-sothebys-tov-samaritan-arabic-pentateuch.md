## Extracts

Sotheby's lot 31 (catalog description):
- "325 leaves, 387mm. by 275mm... double column (right-hand Samaritan-Hebrew, left-hand Arabic), 35-7 lines, in dark brown ink in a Samaritan square bookhand without vowel signs."
- Binding inscription: "let none touch it but the pure."
- Provenance: "The Samaritan community in Damascus."
- "Rothschild's list contains no other complete extant manuscript of a parallel Samaritan-Hebrew and Arabic Pentateuch, whether medieval or modern." Related items listed: a fragment sold Sotheby's 1960; one sixteenth-century Arabic Pentateuch, Uppsala University Library Ar. vii h.3; five Arabic Pentateuchs Paris BnF Ar. MSS 3, 5-8; another fragment Berlin Staatsbibliothek.
- Context quote from Damascene chronicler Dimashi (c. 1300) on Damascus Samaritans' ties with Muslims, offered by the catalog to explain the manuscript's creation.

Tov, "Proto-Samaritan Texts" (Editions and Translations section):
- "The Samaritan Pentateuch was translated into Aramaic, Arabic, and perhaps also Greek... these translations reflect the Samaritan tradition of reading, supplementing the unvocalized text of the Samaritan Pentateuch."
- Editions named: von Gall, Der hebraische Pentateuch der Samaritaner I-IV (Giessen 1914-1918); Abisha scroll colophon claims "thirteenth year after the entrance into Canaan" though "probably written in the thirteenth century"; Sadaqa brothers' parallel-column Jewish/Samaritan edition (Jerusalem 1965); Giron Blanc, Pentateuco hebreo-samaritano Genesis (Madrid 1976, Cambridge Add.1846 dated 1100 CE); Brüll's and Tal's editions of the Samaritan Targum.
- "An Arabic translation from the eleventh century was published by P. Kahle, Die arabischen Bibelübersetzungen (Leipzig, 1904). For a partial, critical edition and an extensive discussion, see H. Shehadeh, The Arabic Translation of the Samaritan Pentateuch, Prolegomena to a Critical Edition, unpubl. diss., Hebrew University (Jerusalem, 1977)."
- "The oral tradition of reading of the Samaritan Pentateuch, running parallel to the written vocalization of MT, has been recorded by several modern scholars, see especially Ben-Hayyim."

Florentin & Tal (Open Book Publishers, 2025):
- The translation is "based on a grammatical analysis of each and every word in the text according to its oral pronunciation, informed by examination of the Samaritan translations into Aramaic and Arabic as well as other Samaritan and non-Samaritan sources"; MS Nablus 6 (copied 1204 CE) represents the version; differences from Masoretic marked in red with explanatory apparatus.

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- The Damascus parallel-column manuscript demonstrates materialized bilingualism in the Samaritan tradition: Samaritan Hebrew on the right, Arabic on the left, in the same hand — scripture and vernacular locked together on the page.
- Tov frames the Samaritan Aramaic and Arabic translations as supplements to an unvocalized consonantal text, i.e., they carry the community's oral reading tradition — translation functions as vocalization-plus-gloss.
- Shehadeh's critical edition project on the Arabic translation of the Samaritan Pentateuch was cited this session via two independent pages; a further snippet (seen only within a Wikipedia search result, hence not citable here) attributed the oldest Samaritan Arabic layer to an adaptation of Saadia Gaon's Tafsir later revised toward the Samaritan text — treat that specific claim as UNVERIFIED pending non-Wikipedia confirmation.
