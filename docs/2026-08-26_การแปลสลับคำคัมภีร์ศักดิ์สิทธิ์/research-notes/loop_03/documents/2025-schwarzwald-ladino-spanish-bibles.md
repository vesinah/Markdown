## Extracts

On the Constantinople Pentateuch (CP) 1547:
- Front page transcription: "Hamisha Humshey Tora ketubim biktab Ashuri, 'im Haftarot vehamesh Megillot ... Targum hamiqra belashon Yevani ulshon La'az ... Kostandina, Defus Eliezer ben Gershom Sonsino" ("The five Chumashim of the Torah, with Haftarot and the five scrolls ... the translation of the Bible in the Greek language and Ladino, Constantinople, Sonsino Print House, [year] 307 [1547]").
- Layout: "The CP edition includes the Hebrew text at the center of the page, with Ladino and Judeo-Greek translations in Hebrew vocalized letters on either side of the Hebrew text. On top of the page, Onkelos' Aramaic translation is printed in Hebrew vocalized letters, and at the bottom, Rashi's explanations are presented in smaller non-vocalized square letters."
- "It is worth noting that the Haftarot and the five scrolls are absent in the text, presumably not printed despite the intentions of the printers."

On the term Ladino:
- "Researchers have assigned the term Ladino to refer to the distinctive rigid word-for-word translations of liturgical texts into Judeo-Spanish."
- "During the Middle Ages, there existed an oral Jewish tradition of translating the Bible in religious services, following the old practice of shnayim miqra ve'ehad targum... Bible translation was also integrated into the Jewish educational system, where children were taught to read the Bible in Hebrew, receiving word-for-word explanations in the local language."
- Late-thirteenth-century glossaries with le'azim ("foreign words") "reflect the vernacular Spanish language of the Jews" and suggest "a tradition of explaining the Bible using the local language word-for-word."

Fidelity features of Ladino vs medieval Spanish Bibles:
- Proper names transliterated from Hebrew (Yehudah, Mosheh, Yarden, Yerushalaim) while medieval versions hispanize (Juda, Moisen, Jordan, Jerusalen).
- God's name rendered יי (yy) in Hebrew script / A. (= Adonay) in Latin script; Elohim consistently "el Dio," never Dios/el senor.
- "While Ladino translations faithfully adhere to the Hebrew syntax word-for-word, the medieval translations exhibit variation"; medieval versions add copulas, reorder clauses, insert explanations (e.g., Arragel's glosses).
- "Ladino texts used the traditional proper names and God's name... preserved some old words in the translations such as omillar and refrained from changing word order or adding any explanations."
- "The Bible held sacred value for the Jews, preventing them from making changes in the scriptures. This reverence extended to Ladino translations, maintaining a strict adherence to the original Hebrew text."

Ferrara Bible 1553 (contrast case):
- Title page: "BIBLIA En lengua Espanola traduzida palabra por palabra de la verdad Hebrayca..." published by Yom Tov Atias and Abraham Usque; Latin letters only, two columns, no Hebrew text; Jewish version dedicated to Dona Gracia, Christian version signed with their Christian names (Jerónimo de Vargas y Duarte Pinel).

Later tradition:
- Full Bible in Hebrew script only 1739-1745 by Abraham Asa; lists of Ladino Bible translations in Cohen's Thesaurus of the Ladino Book.

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- CP 1547 is a five-column polyglot page per column-layout described above: Hebrew core flanked by Ladino and Judeo-Greek, Onkelos overhead, Rashi beneath — the printed page itself encodes the hierarchy (vernaculars beside, Aramaic targum and classic commentary framing).
- Schwarzwald ties the rigid calque style of Ladino directly to oral synagogue/school translation customs descended from shnayim miqra practice — comprehension was delivered word-by-word against the fixed Hebrew.
- Supporting bibliographic items surfaced: Moshe Lazar (ed.), Ladino Pentateuch (Constantinople, 1547), Labyrinthos, Culver City CA, 1988, limited edition of 250 copies, ISBN 0911437460/0911437479 (HathiTrust/NLI records); Amigo Espada 1983 monograph on the Pentateuco de Constantinopla; NLI/RAMBI record "The Judeo-Greek and Ladino columns in the Constantinople edition of the Pentateuch (1547): a linguistic commentary on Gn 1:1-5."
