## Contextual extracts (Oxford Bibliographies "Targum" entry, McNamara & Flesher)
- "Targums are Jewish Aramaic translations of books of the Hebrew Bible. The targumic genre combines literal renderings of the biblical text with additional material, ranging in size from a word to several paragraphs."
- "Most Targums were composed between the 1st and 7th centuries ce, the Rabbinic period."
- "Targums enabled Jews to understand the Hebrew Bible in their study and worship, where weekly Hebrew Bible readings were accompanied by Aramaic translation, probably provided by the written Targums we now possess."
- Dating scheme given there: "the mostly literal Targum Onqelos was composed during the 1st or 2nd century ce by Judeans and was later adopted and revised by Babylonian Jews"; "the Palestinian Targum was written in Galilee by the early 3rd century ce, combining literal translation with much additional material."
- The entry lists a section "Targum, Synagogue, and School" among its contents.

## Related item surfaced
- A 2026 Brill open-access chapter "Chapter 1 Introduction" in The Liturgical Targum engages Flesher and Chilton directly ("Flesher and Chilton, The Targums, Introduction"), showing the book remains a reference point for debates over whether targums are word-for-word translations or liturgical compositions.

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- The volume is positioned by its publisher and by Flesher's institutional bio as the standard one-volume scholarly introduction to all the targums, replacing century-old surveys.
- Its framing (per Oxford Bibliographies, co-authored by Flesher) treats the targums as worship-and-study instruments accompanying weekly synagogue readings, with a two-track typology: near-literal Onqelos versus paraphrastic Palestinian tradition.
- Chilton's involvement connects the project to his earlier work on targum as synagogue performance (see intertextual.bible references to his "From Aramaic Paraphrase to Greek Testament").
