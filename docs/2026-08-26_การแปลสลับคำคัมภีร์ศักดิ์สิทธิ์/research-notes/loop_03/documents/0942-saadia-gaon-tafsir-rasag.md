## Extracts

Sefaria About panel:
- "Tafsir Rasag — Rav Saadia Gaon's influential 10th-century translation of the Torah into Arabic using Hebrew characters."
- "Author: Saadia Gaon. Composed: c.918 - c.942 CE. Translation and commentary on the Torah by Saadia Gaon. ('Tafsir' is an Arabic term meaning 'Biblical commentary')."

Yeshivat Har Etzion:
- "According to his own words in this introduction, the main aim of Rasag was to translate the Torah into the spoken Arabic of his world, in order to make it approachable for everyone."

Saadiah's own programmatic statement (Colby course PDF):
- "[This work is] solely a translation of the plain text of the Torah, made accurate on the basis of the knowledge of reason and tradition."

Chabad.org 2016 report (on Yemenite usage and layout):
- "Judeo-Arabic is an endangered language... There are a few hundred Yemenite Jews who read Rasag's translation every Friday as part of their review of the weekly Torah portion..."
- On a modern handwritten edition: "features vowelized Hebrew verses (in large type), followed by Arabic translation (in smaller type), formatted to be used in the weekly review of the Torah portion as per the ancient custom of chanting the original Hebrew two times and a translation once."

Sameh Hanna 2023 abstract:
- "Sa'adia's tafsir strategies effected a paradigmatic shift in the Arabic translation of the biblical text... Specific focus is laid on issues around anthropomorphism and the use of idiomatic Islamic expressions. The article also reflects on the reception of Sa'adia's work and the impact it had on later generations of translators and readers."

Synagogue use of Arabic as targum (Salevsky 2018 PDF at translation.bible):
- "From the ninth or tenth century onwards it was also common in the synagogues to read the Arabic translation of the Bible as a targum (cf. Kaufmann 2005, 979)."

## Notes (facts seen this session)
- The Tafsir is dated c. 918-942 CE on Sefaria; it was written in Arabic but in Hebrew letters, and its stated aim was the spoken Arabic of the day.
- Continuity with targum practice is direct: Yemenite communities fold the Tafsir into shnayim mikra ve-echad targum review (Hebrew twice, translation once), with printed/handwritten layouts physically interleaving verse and translation.
- Scholarship this session (Hanna 2023; also references there to Vollandt's works on Judaeo-Arabic Pentateuch translations and to Goldstein 2009 on Muslim polemics) treats the Tafsir as a strategic re-founding of Jewish scripture in Arab-Muslim intellectual idioms while denying any change to the plain text.
