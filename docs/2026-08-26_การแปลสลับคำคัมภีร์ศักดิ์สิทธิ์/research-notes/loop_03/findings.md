## Per-source notes

### 1. Mishnah Megillah 4:4 and 4:10 (Posen Library; mishnah.org)
The mishnaic rules institutionalize alternating recitation: a Torah reading must be at least three verses, but only ONE verse at a time may be given to the translator (meturgeman); for Prophets up to three verses may be given, unless they constitute three separate parashiyyot, in which case one by one. The stated reason is that the translator renders from memory ("metargem al peh") and could err; error matters more in Torah because mistranslation could cause halakhic error, while Prophets are less exacting (Bartenura cites Isaiah 52:3-5 as three single-verse sections). Skipping is permitted in Prophets but not across subjects in Torah, and the reader may never leave the congregation waiting after the translator finishes. m. Megillah 4:10 controls content: Reuben's incident read but not translated; Tamar translated; first half of the golden calf episode both, second half untranslated; priestly blessing and David/Amnon neither read nor translated. Companion rabbinic texts displayed by Posen: t. Megillah 3:20-31 (one reads, one translates) and y. Megillah 4:1, 74d (translator forbidden to perform leaning on a pillar).

### 2. Bacher, "Targum" (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1906)
The synagogue translator was a professional called targeman/torgeman/meturgeman, whose duties belonged to the communal sofer charged with biblical instruction; a fourth-century anecdote has R. Samuel b. Isaac ordering a teacher to stop reading Targum from a book — showing written targums existed in Palestinian worship while authority demanded oral delivery. The reader was forbidden to prompt the translator "lest anyone say the Targum was included in the text of the Bible" (Meg. 32a). R. Judah b. Ilai defined the genre's middle path: whoever renders a verse literally "is a liar," whoever adds "is a blasphemer." The Babylonian Pentateuchal targum was redacted in writing by the third century (its Masorah dates to the first half of that century); Onkelos was the official Babylonian targum, Jonathan ben Uzziel's covered the Prophets, and no official Hagiographa targum existed because Ketuvim furnished no weekly lections.

### 3. Intertextual.bible, "Meturgeman"
Defines the meturgeman as an official communal translator-interpreter rendering scripture into Aramaic when read aloud, "not strictly word-for-word," often adding brief explanation or clarification; the office preserved understanding once Hebrew ceased to be widely spoken and fed the interpretive traditions of the Targums. The entry anchors the term in concrete verse pairs (e.g., Onkelos Genesis 28:16, Neofiti Genesis 3:15). A Salevsky 2018 paper surfaced in search adds that from the ninth-tenth centuries synagogues also read the ARABIC translation as targum (citing Kaufmann 2005), showing the office outliving Aramaic.

### 4. Flesher & Chilton, The Targums (Baylor UP, 2011)
Per publisher page: Waco TX, 2011-06-30, 575 pages, ISBNs 9781602583825/9781602583894. Flesher's university bio calls it "the first book-length introduction to the Targums in more than a century" and credits his research on the sources of the Palestinian Targums with reshaping the field's concept of the Pentateuchal targums including Onqelos; he co-founded the International Organization for Targumic Study and the Journal of the Aramaic Bible (now Aramaic Studies). The companion Oxford Bibliographies entry (McNamara & Flesher) frames the genre as literal renderings plus additions from a word to paragraphs, mostly 1st-7th centuries CE, with weekly synagogue readings accompanied by Aramaic translation "probably provided by the written Targums we now possess," dating Onqelos to 1st-2nd-century Judeans later revised in Babylonia and the paraphrastic Palestinian Targum to Galilee by the early third century. A 2026 Brill open-access chapter (The Liturgical Targum) still argues against their framing, showing the book remains the debate's reference point.

### 6. Seidman, Faithful Renderings (U Chicago Press, 2006)
Publisher description: reads translation history through Jewish-Christian difference and vice versa; a theological-political analysis of why Christianity's dependence on a rival religion's scriptures "haunted" Western translation theory. Topics include accuracy battles over Bible translations, medieval use of converts from Judaism as translators, censorship of anti-Christian passages in Jewish texts, and Holocaust testimony translation; opens with the author's grandfather acting as a strategically unfaithful "double agent" translator (p. 2 per secondary citation). Reviews quoted (Boyd-Taylor, Conway-Jones, Boys, Kaufmann) confirm its standing as a history of Jewish approaches to fidelity in translation. This supplies the loop's theoretical frame: vernacular rendering under conditions where fidelity itself is confessional.

### 7. Steiner, A Biblical Translation in the Making (HUP, 2011)
Harvard UP page: publication 2011-06-20, 198 pages, ISBN 9780674033351, Harvard University Center for Jewish Studies imprint. Description: young Saadia (882-942 CE) aimed to replace "overly literal" Judaeo-Arabic versions then in vogue with a translation conforming to traditional Judaism, contemporary philosophy, and the canons of Arabic grammar and style; the project answered Karaite and Muslim polemicists seeking converts, making the Tafsir "the most important Jewish Bible translation of the Middle Ages." Evidence base includes a St. Catherine's Monastery Christian Arabic Pentateuch (argued to be Hunayn b. Ishaq's lost ninth-century version whose philosophical Genesis 1:1 Saadia adopted) and a Cairo Genizah Judeo-Arabic annotated Genesis argued to be a leaf of the FIRST edition drafted while Saadia was a student in Tiberias.

### 8. Saadia Gaon's Tafsir Rasag (Sefaria and satellites)
Sefaria dates the work c. 918-942 CE and glosses "Tafsir" as Arabic for biblical commentary; the text is Arabic in Hebrew characters. Saadia's own introduction states the aim was the spoken Arabic of his world "in order to make it approachable for everyone" (Yeshivat Har Etzion), and his programmatic line claims it is "solely a translation of the plain text... made accurate on the basis of the knowledge of reason and tradition" (Colby course PDF). Yemenite Jews still read the Tafsir every Friday within the shnayim mikra review; modern editions reproduce the interleaved layout — vowelized Hebrew verse in large type, Arabic below in smaller type — "as per the ancient custom of chanting the original Hebrew two times and a translation once" (Chabad.org 2016). Sameh Hanna (2023, abstract) argues the Tafsir effected a paradigm shift in Arabic Bible translation, focused on anthropomorphism avoidance and idiomatic Islamic expressions, shaping all later translators.

### 9. Faierstein, "A Guide to the Ze'enah U-Re'enah" (In geveb, 2019)
The Ze'enah U-Re'enah is "the most popular Yiddish book ever published": 275+ printings, 240 Yiddish editions plus 34 translations/adaptations in seven languages. It is NOT a plain Yiddish Humash translation but an anthological commentary weaving over a dozen medieval commentaries (title page names Rashi, Ramban, Rabbenu Bahya, Hizkuni, Zeror ha-Mor, Toldot Yitzhak) with Talmud and Midrash; its real title is Hamisha Humshe Torah im kol ha-haftaot bi-leshon Ashkenaz, with "Ze'enah U-Re'enah" (Song 3:11) as subtitle. Early editions run as one unbroken paragraph per portion, each comment opening with the Hebrew lemma words being discussed, presupposing readers who already know the Humash — i.e., Hebrew verse anchored, Yiddish exposition interleaved. Halakhically, Rabbi David ha-Levi (Taz) ruled that a man not fluent in Aramaic may substitute this Yiddish work for targum in the weekly shnayim mikra review. Author: Jacob ben Isaac of Yanov (d. 1623), endorsed by Maharsha and the Bach; earliest surviving edition Basel/Hanau 1622 (three lost Krakow/Lublin predecessors); audience stated as "men and women."

### 10. Schwarzwald, "Ladino and Spanish Bibles" (Brill, open access)
Scholarship reserves "Ladino" for rigid word-for-word liturgical translation into Judeo-Spanish, descended from medieval oral practice tied to shnayim miqra ve'ehad targum and from school word-for-word explanations (documented via late-thirteenth-century le'azim glossaries). The Constantinople Pentateuch of 1547 (printed by Eliezer ben Gershom Sonsino) arranges each page as a five-part polyglot: Hebrew text centered, Ladino and Judeo-Greek translations in vocalized Hebrew letters flanking it, Onkelos in vocalized letters above, Rashi in smaller non-vocalized square letters below — though the advertised Haftarot and five scrolls were apparently never printed. Linguistic analysis shows Ladino fidelity markers versus medieval romanceadas: transliterated proper names (Yehudah vs Juda), divine name rendered יי/A., Elohim as "el Dio," strict Hebrew syntax calqued, no added explanations. The Ferrara Bible (1553, Atias & Usque) is the Latin-letter counterpart without Hebrew. Full Bible in Hebrew script came only with Abraham Asa (1739-45); Moshe Lazar's 1988 Labyrinthos facsimile (limited 250 copies) remains the standard reprint of CP 1547.

### 11. Judaeo-Persian literature (Jewish Encyclopedia, 1906)
Tradition claims deep antiquity: Maimonides' Iggeret Teman says the Pentateuch was rendered into Persian centuries before Mohammed, and Theodoret (fifth century) mentions a (now vanished, probably Pahlavi) Persian Bible. The oldest extant dated witness is BL Or.5446, completed 24 Adar 1319 by Joseph b. Moses, likely also the translator; fifteenth-sixteenth-century Vatican/Paris/St Petersburg manuscripts follow, closely connected to Targum Onkelos and judged by Guidi to originate in Kurdistan/border regions. Jacob ben Joseph Tawus's translation appeared in the Constantinople Polyglot (1546) and was reprinted with Hyde's Latin in the London Polyglot (1657). Word-level glossaries (perush ha-millot) were "especially popular" aids, and Simeon Hakam's 1901-03 Jerusalem edition inserted traditional Bukharan renderings in brackets/smaller print beside his punctuated translation — evidence of an oral community standard constraining the written text.

### 12. JSTOR Judeo-Persian items
"Judeo-Persian Bible Translations: Some Sample Texts" records that at the dawn of European Persian studies the Tawus Judeo-Persian Pentateuch from the 1546 Constantinople polyglot was among the very first Persian texts studied and excerpted for early dictionaries. "Another Judeo-Persian Pentateuch Translation: MS HUC 2193" describes the interleave architecture explicitly: each verse headed by its initial Hebrew word followed by the full Judeo-Persian translation, with each weekly portion followed by its haftarah translation. ### 13. Samaritan Pentateuch with parallel Arabic (Sotheby's; Tov; Florentin-Tal)
Sotheby's lot 31 (Western Manuscripts L07240, 2007): a unique complete vellum manuscript, 325 leaves, double column with right-hand Samaritan-Hebrew and left-hand Arabic in Samaritan script, from the Damascus Samaritan community, binding inscribed "let none touch it but the pure"; catalog notes no other complete parallel Samaritan-Hebrew/Arabic codex is recorded, with related fragments in Uppsala, Paris BnF, and Berlin. Tov summarizes that the Samaritan Pentateuch was translated into Aramaic, Arabic, and perhaps Greek, these versions reflecting the community's oral reading tradition supplementing an unvocalized consonantal text; he lists von Gall's 1914-18 edition, the Abisha scroll (colophon claims 13th year after entrance into Canaan; probably 13th century CE), Kahle's publication of an eleventh-century Arabic translation (Leipzig 1904), and Shehadeh's 1977 Hebrew University dissertation toward a critical edition of the Arabic. Florentin & Tal's 2025 English translation (Open Book Publishers) bases itself on MS Nablus 6 (1204 CE) and on oral pronunciation informed by the Aramaic and Arabic Samaritan translations. A snippet claiming the oldest Samaritan Arabic adapts Saadia's Tafsir was seen only inside a Wikipedia result and is therefore UNVERIFIED here.

### 14. Shnayim mikra ve-echad targum (Berakhot 8a; OC 285)
Berakhot 8a (Steinsaltz/Sefaria): Rav Huna bar Yehuda in the name of Rabbi Ami — complete one's portions with the congregation, "twice and the translation once," with long life as reward (Peninei Halakha). Post-talmudic authorities widened what fills the "targum" slot: Rosh (Berakhot 1:8) and Tur (OC 285) accept Rashi as substitute; the Taz accepts a Yiddish work like the Ze'enah U-Re'enah. The private-study rite thus preserves the ancient verse-plus-rendering unit even after public targum recitation lapsed in most communities (persisting in Yemen).

## Cross-cutting observations (grounded strictly in this session's pages)

- One recurring architecture appears across fifteen centuries: fixed Hebrew unit + vernacular rendering immediately attached (oral alternation in m. Megillah 4:4; interlinear columns in CP 1547 and MS HUC 2193; lemma-plus-exposition in the Tsenerene; verse-over-translation layouts in modern Saadia and shnayim mikra editions).
- Authority is guarded by separation rules: translator performs from memory, may not read from a book, cannot be prompted, and certain verses are ring-fenced from translation entirely (m. Megillah 4:10).
- Comprehensibility is tiered to stakes: one verse at a time for Torah because legal error is feared; three for Prophets where looser accuracy is tolerated.
- Each vernacular wave reuses the same institutional slot: Aramaic meturgeman -> Arabic tafsir read "as targum" (9th-10th c. onward) -> printed Ladino/Greek/Persian polyglot pages (Constantinople 1546-47) -> Yiddish commentary substituted for Aramaic by halakhic ruling (Taz).
- Translation style itself encodes theology: anti-anthropomorphism in Onkelos and Saadia; word-for-word calque fidelity in Ladino as reverence; anthological commentary (Tsenerene) rather than bare translation in Ashkenaz.
