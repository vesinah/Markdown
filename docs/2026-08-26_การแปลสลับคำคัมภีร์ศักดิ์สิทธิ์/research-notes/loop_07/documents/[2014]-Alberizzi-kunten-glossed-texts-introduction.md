## Key extracts seen this session

- Japan is described as having "the best established tradition among all the East Asian cultures which developed strategies to interpret written Chinese based on their non-Sinitic vernaculars"; kanbun kundoku 漢文訓読 has frequently been interpreted as a kind of translation by Western scholars.
- Japanese scholars reject construing kundoku as translation because it "does not produce a parallel text in the target language" and "leaves the Chinese original actively present in the reading context, using it as a 'visual' outline to facilitate comprehension."
- Mechanics as described: small phonograms (kana) added to right or left of a lemma supplied inflectional endings; conversion from SVO (Chinese) to SOV (Japanese) syntactic order indicated by dots, keys and other paragraphematic marks "commonly known as kunten 訓点, inserted on the lower side of a character."
- Oldest extant glossed manuscript in Japan named as the Zokukegonkyō ryakuso kanjōki 続華厳経略疏刊定記 (commentary to the Avatamsaka sutra), transcribed by layman Chikyō 智鏡, collated at Tōdaiji in Enryaku years 2 and 7 (783 and 788); glosses in white and vermillion pigments, white marks used for punctuation only, vermilion ones for reordering syntactic order.
- Buddhist vs secular glosses differ: "The discovery of remarkable differences in the way of notating Buddhist texts in Chinese as well as the secular Chinese classics is one of the most important contributions" of kundoku studies; variations among Buddhist schools and lay scholars' families reflect different ways of reading.
- Vernacular reading spread varied across the Sinosphere: established as the "official" method in Japan, narrower development in Uighur Central Asia, gradually abandoned in Korea after initial adoption; traces found in Vietnamese documents per some scholars.
- Modern kunten research traced to 1903 and the activities of the Kokugo (national language) survey tradition; glosses are provisional notations jotted quickly in interlinear space, linked to writer skill level, errors common.
- Eight-century predominance of mark-based glossing due both to inadequate development of pure phonographic writing and to advantages of quick, small-sized notation.
