## Key extracts seen this session

- Abstract: "The earliest examples of translation in Japanese are found in glossed Sinitic Buddhist texts rendered into the vernacular via kanbun kundoku ('Sinitic script, vernacular reading')."
- Chapter studies early Heian (794-1185 CE) narrative techniques in a 9th-century Japanese rendering of the Golden Light Sutra parable of Prince Mahasattva feeding a starving tigress with his own body; focuses on morpho-syntactic representation of tense, aspect, modality, mirativity mostly left to context in the Sinitic source.
- Argues vernacular glosses on early Heian Buddhist texts are evidence of acts of translation performed for a domestic audience unable to fully access source texts in Chinese.

## Companion paper by same author seen this session (SEARCH-VERIFIED)

- "Evidence in Heian Buddhist Kundokugo Narration" (Proceedings of the Association for Japanese Literary Studies, Brandeis journals site): compares evidential auxiliaries in Heian fiction vs Buddhist kundokugo. Key points captured: in Heian secular narrative keri frames narration while ki marks witnessed facts; in Buddhist kundokugo renditions (early 9th c. Saidaiji Golden Light Sutra; late 11th/early 12th c. Ryukoin Lotus Sutra) keri is absent from narrative clauses and ki governs framing because "the authority of the religious text is taken for granted"; quotes Guest (2013) that semi-standardized calques between characters and Japanese words made kundoku renderings "a distinctive style... not expected to conform exactly to any other style of classical Japanese"; quotes Steininger (2017): kundoku "cleaves closely to the original text" representing meaning "through a limited, formalized Japanese register while maintaining the structure of the original."
- Related OSU dissertation by same author: "Tense, Aspect, and Modality in the Creation of Narrative Structure: Early Heian Japanese Translations of Sinitic Buddhist Texts" (OhioLINK acc_num osu1627040325555703), analyzing auxiliaries ki, keri, tsu, nu, ari, tari in early Heian kundokubun.
