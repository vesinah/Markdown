## Key extracts seen this session

- Argues for terminological care in Anglophone scholarship: shift toward "Literary Sinitic," "Sinograph/Sinogram," reflecting the transnational reach of Sinographic writing beyond present-day China ("Sinographic cosmopolis," term attributed to Ross King).
- Defines Literary Sinitic as largely synonymous with Literary Chinese (wenyanwen), "a conservative written standard grounded ultimately in Old Chinese (600 BCE-0 CE)".
- Kundoku made it possible that "readers of Literary Sinitic prose need never have conceived of themselves as reading in a language that was anything other than 'Japanese'"; warns against conflating orthography with language.
- A kundoku register "will be noticeably different from vernacular Japanese registers of any time period", excluding many ordinary Japanese lexical elements while admitting phraseologies found nowhere else in the language.
- Kundoku methods widely taught today "do largely represent conventions current in the late nineteenth century"; techniques of many premodern schools of kundoku have been lost to history.
- Any complete kundoku system permitting both decoding and encoding "will run up against challenges that make departures from vernacular Japanese essentially inevitable" because vernacular Japanese cannot be fully inscribed logographically with Sinographs alone.
- Quotes Hashimoto Shinkichi's definition of kanbun as "Japanese writing that follows the rules of Chinese writing" (a species of Japanese literary language).
- Quotes Brian Steininger (2017): poems by Bai Juyi and Sugawara no Michizane can be characterized as "Chinese" from script vantage yet read aloud are equally "Japanese."
