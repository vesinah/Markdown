## Per-source notes

**Okell 1965.** Foundational linguistic analysis of "nissaya Burmese" as a register systematically adapted to a foreign (Pali) grammar and syntax; Lingua 15:186-227. Full title confirmed independently by Pannabhoga's reference list and by the SOAS Digital Collections scan slug (item 529303), resolving a mis-attribution risk: that scan is Okell's article. Per Pannabhoga's summary (p. 7), Okell reads nissaya as linguistic analysis with special emphasis on Pali grammatical features, arguing Burmese absorbed Pali morphology/syntax under double pressure of nissaya literature and Pali grammar study since the late Pagan period.

**Pruitt 1994 (+ 1986-87 articles; 1992 IJL study).** Meticulous demonstration, building on Tin Lwin and Okell, of "the extraordinary range of grammatical particles and abbreviation systems employed in a single type of Pali-Burmese bitext" (Walker 2020 fn.2). Precursor trilogy in Cahiers de l'Asie du Sud-Est 19/21/22 (1986-87) edits a Bibliotheque Nationale Patimokkha nissaya linguistically. His 1992 lexicography-domain article documents that Shwe Zan Aung and Pe Maung Tin translated the Abhidhammattha-sangaha (1910) and Atthasalini (1920) into English for the PTS through the nissaya method - i.e., Western PTS translations are themselves nissaya-mediated.

**McDaniel 2002 (FULL).** Declares the nissaya genre "unique to Laos, Burma and Northern Thailand"; begins by the 15th c., most prevalent 1720-1880. Thai/Lao designations: nissaya, nissai, nisai sab, khan, nisrai, wohin; some manuscripts carry double titles ("Wohin Hii Sip Chiit Nisrai Hii Sip Chat"). Defines services: repetitive word-glossing; some nissayas function as bilingual dictionaries (nisrai wohan/nisai sab colophon titles); von Hinuber suggested the name reflects texts that "lean on" Pali. Argues nissayas are teacher's handbooks/student guides for explaining Pali in sermons, never read verbatim; ties the term to the Vinaya nissaya (five-to-ten-year dependence of novice on acariya/upadhaya, Mahavagga refs). Names dozens of Lanna/Lao manuscripts (Chiang Mai Siitmon Nissaya; Vientiane Panchakanipit Nissaya; Wiangchan Phrakaeo Nissaya etc.).

**Hundius (FULL).** Frames the "Tham Script Domain"/"Greater Lao Cultural Domain" (Lan Na-Lan Sang-Khengtung-Sipsongpanna) sharing one Mon-derived script vs khom-script Siam. Documents the 1835-36 Kancana Mahathera copying campaign at Luang Prabang for Wat Sung Men (Phrae): over 2,800 phuk including "various rare works written in Pali and nissaya texts." Sets a research agenda explicitly including "the relationship between monolingual Pali versions of non-canonical Buddhist Jatakas, their nissaya or wohan versions and their secular literary adaptations" across the Lao cultural domain. Quantifies PLMP preservation: about 368,000 phuk inventoried, about 830 monasteries, about 1,000 microfilm rolls (1992-2002).

**McDaniel DLLM paper (FULL).** Supplies his Harvard dissertation title: "Invoking the Source: Nissaya Manuscripts, Pedagogy and Sermon-making in Northern Thailand and Laos" (Harvard, 2003). Attributes Lan Na's vernacular rise partly to "the Burmese and Lao penchant for composing nissaya manuscripts in the vernacular"; describes the Wat Sung Men corpus as largely bilingual (Pali/Northern Thai or Lao), including "a complete Nissaya version of the Pannasa-Jataka" (quoting Hundius 1990:34-35); lists coexisting vernacular genres (nissayas, namasaddas, voharas, anisong, xalong...). Claims a shared Lao-Northern Thai episteme whose local canon differed from Siam, Cambodia and Burma.

**McDaniel 2008.** The monograph synthesis: nissaya/namasadda/vohara overlap in style, pedagogy and social use in Thailand/Laos; nissaya/namasadda give close word-for-word glosses, voharas quote 12-25-word Pali stretches with expansive creative commentary, generic nissayas 4-10 words (per Pannabhoga's digest of pp. 131ff). Coins/applies "lifting words" for this pedagogy; dates nissaya use to the 16th-19th c. Walker 2020 targets its pp. 119-60 idiosyncrasy claim for refutation. Print details confirmed via Bickner's JAS review (UWP 2008, xiii+358 pp.).

**Ruiz-Falques 2017.** Verified as a 96-page Journal of Burma Studies 21(1) article on the role of Pali grammar (Kaccayana-lineage; Aggavamsa's Saddaniti of 1154 CE as landmark) within Burmese Buddhism; builds on his Cambridge PhD (2015) "A Firefly in the Bamboo Reed: The Suttaniddesa of Saddhammajotipala and the Grammatical Foundations of Theravada Buddhism in Burma." Later outputs verified: OUP 2023 chapter "A Faultless Science" in Bronner-edited A Lasting Vision; JIPh 2022 on permanent euphonic ligatures in classical Pali grammars; 2025 chapter treating Pali grammar "as a branch of the Abhidhamma." Affiliated with Shan State Buddhist University, acknowledged by Pannabhoga.

**Walker 2018 dissertation.** Title, institution (UC Berkeley), year verified verbatim from JAOS 2020 footnote: Cambodian chanted leporellos, communal scripts, localized translations. It carries his transliteration system for Khmer/Lanna/Lao/Siamese, the critique of McDaniel's idiosyncrasy thesis (diss. 351-61, 370-73), Tai-Khmer exchange analysis (403-29), and Khmer-origin attribution for a Siamese annotation practice (diss. 335). Companion pieces verified: "Echoes of a Sanskrit Past" (Unhissavijaya in Cambodia, 2018), Numen 2024 on Khmer Pali pronunciations, Religions 2022 on Khmer nuns.

**Kirichenko chapter 2018/19.** Verified contribution to Feener & Blackburn's Buddhist and Islamic Orders in Southern Asia on eighteenth-century itineraries of the "Sihala monk" Saralanka - the monastic-network substrate of Burmese Pali learning; plus JBS 2009 (yazawin historiography) and JBS 2011 (Atula Hsayadaw monastic reform). IMPORTANT NEGATIVE RESULT: no standalone Kirichenko publication on nissaya-as-genre surfaced in Crossref, OpenAlex full-text, Internet Archive, or Wayback CDX searches this session; that attribution remains UNVERIFIED.

**Walker 2020 (FULL).** Unifying model: bitext composition = selection (citation/invention) + analysis (parsing, amplification, rearrangement, particle annotation, gloss) + presentation (philological/exegetical/homiletic/poetic). Local names: nissaya (Burmese); nam/trai-ai (Mon); nisrai/nissay (Lanna/Lao/Siamese); namasabd/sap; cunniyapad; ple yak sabd (Siamese); pre lot prayog (Khmer). Anchor datings: Pali-Mon inscription late 11th/early 12th c.; Pali-Burmese from late 13th c.; 1442 inscription listing Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts; earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext 1482; earliest securely dated Pali-Lanna ms 1552; Sanskrit-Lanna 1578; oldest Pali-Lao ms bitext = bilingual Abhidhammatthasangaha 1586/87 (PLMP 08040102007_04). Deciphers Siamese khaam hvat/tva ksien interlinear annotations on curriculum texts as the region's most abbreviated philological mode. Contests McDaniel: conventions were strict and trans-regional from the eleventh century onward.

**Pannabhoga 2023 (FULL).** Living-practice account from a Shan State Buddhist University dean: Vinaya-derived etymology of nissaya as guidance; claimed Pyu (7th-c. Sanskrit-Pyu, Sriksetra) and Pagan precursors; Tin Lwin's candidate oldest Burmese nissaya (Khuddasikkha nissaya of Mahakassapa, reign of Narapatisithu); Ruiz-Falques' caution that no grammatical nissayas are attested from Pagan. Documents the Chatthasangiti official translation as essentially de-Pali-fied old nissaya (Clark 2015), pathasesa additions in parentheses; Karunika's seven interpretive methods; classroom mechanics (teacher-zaso recitation, ane'-de margin annotation, Thanbyin Tika's padaccheda-first maxim); named nissaya authors U Budh, Neyin, Nan Kyaung, Bagara, Payagyi, Pyay, Pakokku, Janakabhivamsa (graded Bhasatika series), Ma Oo, Moe Htee; >22 nissayas on the Abhidhammattha-sangaha by the 19th c.; Mandalay vs Pakokku training methods; notes Thai nissaya weakness (no nissaya in current Thai Naktham/Pariyatti exams, per Pathompong 2016) and gambhi-nisai (Kaccayana Gambhi-nisai, Saddasaratthajalini nisai).

## Cross-cutting findings (for downstream synthesis)

1. One pan-regional technology, many emic labels: Burmese nissaya, Mon nam/trai-ai, Lanna/Lao/Siamese nisrai-nissay/namasabd/vohara(+wohan), Sinhala sannaya, Khmer pre lot prayog - all realizing selection-analysis-presentation steps (Walker's three-step model parallels Loop 10's Sanskrit five-services and sannaya pada-anuvaya apparatus).
2. Authority preserved by Pali anchoring: the Indic string stays visible (interlinear khaam hvat marks in Siam; interphrasal Pali lead-ins in nissaya; pathasesa parentheses in Chatthasangiti Burmese), while vernacular matter expands freely around it - vernacularization without renouncing the cosmopolitan idiom.
3. Pedagogical identity is constitutive, not incidental: the genre's name comes from the Vinaya's nissaya dependence relation (novice-preceptor), and both McDaniel and Pannabhoga define the texts as teacher's supports/student guides rather than translations proper; Burmese official Tipitaka translation is downstream of nissayas, not vice versa.
4. Chronological spine now citable: Pyu/Mon bilingual inscriptions (6th-12th c.), Pali-Mon late 11th/12th c., Pali-Burmese late 13th c., Sri Lankan sannaya 12th c., Burmese efflorescence 15th c. onward (peak 1720-1880), Pali-Siamese poetic bitext 1482, Pali-Lanna 1552/1563/1585/1638/1666, Pali-Lao 1586/87, Kancana's 1835-36 Luang Prabang campaign with complete Pancasa-Jataka nissaya.
5. Scholarly dispute to carry forward: McDaniel's idiosyncrasy/no-fixed-conventions position (2002, 2008) vs Walker's strict shared-convention position (2018 diss., 2020); Walker also supplies the sharper chronology against McDaniel's 16th-century start.
6. Institutional asymmetry: nissaya remains a mainstream, exam-linked living pedagogy in Burma (Mandalay/Pakokku methods, Dhammacariya syllabus, Bhasatika handbooks) while Thailand formally dropped it from monastic curricula and Laos' manuscript base survives chiefly through PLMP/DLLM microfilming.
