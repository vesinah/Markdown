## Key extracts (as reported by sources fetched/read this session)
- Pannabhoga 2023 repeatedly cites it for core claims: "McDaniel (2008, 131-136) calls this method of learning 'lifting words' in similar monastic learning institutions in northern Thailand and Laos"; "According to McDaniel (2008, 119), a similar genre... developed within Theravada Buddhist countries, i.e., in Sri Lanka, as sannaya, and in Thailand and Laos as nissaya, namasadda, and vohara"; "the nissayas (supports) were used by monastic teachers in the pre-modern period from the 16th to 19th centuries in Thailand and Laos" (2008, 6).
- Pannabhoga 2023 on genre overlap per McDaniel (2008, 131): in Thailand/Laos nissaya, namasadda and vohara genres overlap "in style, pedagogical methods and social use"; nissaya and namasaddha manuscripts show "close, word-for-word glosses", whereas voharas cite "longer passages of twelve to twenty-five words from Pali source texts and offer extensive and creative vernacular translations and commentaries"; general nissayas cite "four to ten words before offering glosses and creative asides."
- Walker 2020 fn. 4 cites Gathering Leaves pp. 119-60 as the locus of McDaniel's claim, based on Pali-Lao and Pali-Lanna bitexts, "that such bilingual compositions follow no strict conventions but rather reflect the idiosyncratic approaches of individual teachers" - a claim Walker explicitly contests.
- Review located: Ananda Abeysekara review essay "Buddhism, Power, Modernity..." (Culture and Religion, DOI 10.1080/14755610.2011.626110) and Bickner's JAS 69.2 review (DOI 10.1017/S0021911810001385).

## Relevance to loop topic
The standard English monograph for sub-topic (C): establishes nissaya/namasadda/vohara as overlapping pedagogical genres of Lao-Lanna monastic education, supplies the "lifting words" concept, and frames glossed-text education histories that Walker's stricter philology later challenges.
