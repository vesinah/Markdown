## Key extracts
- Definition: "I define bitexts as texts that are presented bilingually, with portions in one language mixed together with portions in another, typically in an interphrasal or interlinear arrangement"; vernacular portions usually give "an analytic reading" of the Pali/Sanskrit, sometimes with interpretive commentary; such bitexts "form a significant portion of all extant written material produced between the twelfth and nineteenth centuries in what is now Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, and Thailand."
- Local genre names table (fn. 1): nissaya (Burmese); nam/trai-ai (Mon); nisrai/nissay (Lanna, Lao, Siamese); namasabd/sap (Lanna, Lao, Siamese); cunniyapad (Siamesene cuṇṇiyapad); ple yak sabd (Siamese); pre lot prayog (Khmer).
- Three-step compositional model shared across Sri Lanka and mainland SEA: (1) Selection - citation of existing or invention of new Indic text; (2) Analysis - parsing, amplification, rearrangement to vernacular syntax, grammatical annotation with particles, gloss; (3) Presentation modes - philological (abbreviated), exegetical (scholastic prose), homiletic (narrative prose for preaching), poetic (verse). Pali-Sinhala bitexts omit particle annotation (2d) because Sinhala marks these morphologically.
- Chronology attested: earliest mainland example is a Pali-Mon inscription late 11th/early 12th c.; Pali-Burmese bitexts extant from the late thirteenth century; several Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts listed in an inscription from 1442; earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext dated 1482 Ayutthaya (Mahajati gam hlvang); earliest securely dated Pali-Lanna manuscript bitext 1552 (DLNTM 011903027_00); Sanskrit-Lanna 1578; oldest surviving Pali-Lao manuscript bitext = bilingual Abhidhammatthasangaha, CS 948 (= 1586/87 CE), PLMP code 08040102007_04.
- Siamese interlinear annotation system: khaam hvat / tva ksien annotations on palm-leaf curriculum texts (Kaccayanavyakarana, Dhammapada-atthakatha, Mangalatthadipani, Saratthasangaha, Samantapasadhika, Visuddhimagga, Abhidhammatthasangaha), commonest 18th-19th c., likely older; he deciphers parsing spaces, word-isolation markers, numeral/syllable rearrangement ciphers (1-4, ka/kaki/k), nominative-case particle an va, and glosses - calling it "one of the most sophisticated forms of vernacular grammatical analysis to emerge prior to... modern linguistics."
- Explicit disagreement with McDaniel: contra Gathering Leaves pp. 119-60, strict conventions existed and were shared cross-regionally from the eleventh century onward (critique elaborated in his 2018 Berkeley diss., 351-61, 370-73).
- Khmer link: many such Pali-Siamese bitexts traveled to Cambodia in the 18th-20th c.; Thai/Khmer syntactic similarity makes the same rearranged readings work in Khmer (citing Huffman, "Thai and Cambodian: A Case of Syntactic Borrowing?", JAOS).
- Conclusion: bitext techniques served learning, teaching, preaching and writing from the fifteenth to nineteenth centuries; the volume of bitextual palm-leaf/folded-paper manuscripts is "staggering" and mostly unedited.
