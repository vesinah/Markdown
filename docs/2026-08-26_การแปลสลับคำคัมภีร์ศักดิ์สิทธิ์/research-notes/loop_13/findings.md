## Per-source notes

### Doctrina Christiana 1593 (facsimile, FULL-FETCHED)
The unique surviving copy (Rosenwald Collection, LOC) contains seventy-four printed pages "in Spanish, Tagalog transliterated into roman letters, and Tagalog in Tagalog characters", opening with a Tagalog syllabary before Pater Noster through Catechism - i.e., the book itself teaches both scripts alongside prayers in two languages. LOC calls it the first book printed in the Philippines and "the first, and only, 16th-century source showing an explicit and distinctly Philippine abecedarium". Governor Dasmarinas' letter of 20 June 1593 licenses TWO doctrinas - one Tagalog, one Chinese (the latter by Juan Cobo, no copy known). Price control documents show two reales (Tagalog) vs four (Chinese).

### Wolf 1947 (FULL-FETCHED)
Wolf establishes the volume is entirely xylographic ("each page ... printed from one wood-block ... carved by hand", no conventional press), on mulberry-paper stock described via Pardo de Tavera, and reconciles contradictory testimony through Retana's four conclusions: 1593 xylographic Doctrinas; Blancas de San Jose initiated typography; the first typographer was the Chinese Christian Juan de Vera; first typographic printing 1602. Authorship analysis assigns the Tagalog text to the Franciscan "Talavera-Plasencia-Oliver" tradition, corrected under Dominicans Nieva and San Pedro Martyr, and notes Martin de Rada brought woodblock-printed Chinese books that plausibly modeled Manila xylography. The essay also documents the licensing regime (cedulas of 1556/1560; 8 May 1584 order subjecting grammars/dictionaries of indigenous languages to Bishop and Audiencia censorship).

### Rafael 1988 (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Publisher description frames the book as examining "the role of language in the religious conversion of the Tagalogs ... during the early period (1580-1705)", mapping how translation enabled both colonial regime and resistance; Crossref shows a DUP e-book chapter titled "The 'Inadequacies' of Tagalog Conversion". Full text was borrow-only/inaccessible this session, so internal arguments were NOT observed and must be treated as UNVERIFIED here. Note: Duke UP page carries a February 2026 death remembrance for Rafael.

### Javellana 1984 (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Opening sentence captured: the first datable pasyon was "a 980-verse narrative written by the master printer of the Jesuits, Gaspar Aquino de Belen"; Philippine Studies 32(3), pp. 305-321 confirmed via JSTOR and Archium landing page. Corroborating sources seen this session place Mahal na Passion ni Jesu Christong Panginoong Natin na Tola at 1703/1704, appended to a Tagalog prayer book for the dying (Mga Panalanging Pagtatagobilin sa Caloloua nang Taoung Naghihingalo), with Spanish Jesuit devotional sources (Retablo de la Vida de Cristo tradition) behind it. Aquino de Belen worked at the Jesuit press in Manila from 1703 to 1716 (LOC blog citing Rafael's reading). Later expansion: anonymous Casaysayan/Pasyon Pilapil, translated into many Philippine languages.

### Dictionarium Latino-Lusitanicum ac Iaponicum 1595 (FULL-FETCHED via NDL API)
NDL records reproduce the 1870 Petitjean reprint title verbatim, proving on-title facts: first printed at Amakusa in the Jesuit college press in 1595, derived "ex Ambrosii Calepini volumine", i.e., an adaptation of Calepino's European polyglot dictionary with Japanese added to Latin-Portuguese. Shima Shozo's study volumes (1962; Ken'an editions 1971-1977) pair the 1870 Lexicon page-by-page against the 1595 original. Orii 2015 groups it with Vocabulario da lingoa de Iapam (1603-04) and Racuyoxu (1598) as the mission's three great linguistic tools.

### Chiba, Rakuyoshu (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Chiba's article title (Arena 23) directly attributes the kanji character dictionary Rakuyoshu to Jesuit missionaries; an NDL record confirms a 1984 Sasage facsimile labeled "Kirishitan kokuji-bon" (Kirishitan movable-type edition), placing it firmly in the Kirishitan-ban corpus. Orii describes Racuyoxu (1598) as "a dictionary of Chinese and Japanese characters" essential "for reading kanji" - a native-format reference work, the reverse adaptation direction compared with the Calepino-based Dictionarium. Claims about its handakuten innovation circulate only on encyclopedia-derived pages seen this session: UNVERIFIED.

### Kishimoto 2005 (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Peer-reviewed chapter (SiHOLS 109, pp. 205-224) treating the Amakusa Dictionarium as an adaptation of Calepino's polyglot dictionary for Japan - independently corroborated this session by the reprint title-page evidence from NDL. Internal analysis (which Calepino redaction served as base; how Japanese equivalents were inserted) not read this session: paywalled.

### Orii 2015 (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
State-of-the-field essay confirming dates and functions of the three Jesuit lexicographic works, print-run data (Mesquita reported 1,300 copies of Contemptus Mundi; 1,500 of Confessionarios), the Laures numbering system, and the historiographic shift toward reading Kirishitan-ban within Iberian maritime expansion. Also notes Japanese movable-type printing began around the arrival of the Jesuit presses, coinciding with printing arriving from Korea, and that the press settled in Nagasaki until the 1614 persecution.

### Cervera 2023 x2 (Hispania FULL-FETCHED; Cauriensia SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Hispania abstract verifies the chain: Ricci published Tianzhu Shiyi in 1603 bridging Confucian ethics and Christianity; Juan Cobo's Shilu was published in Manila in 1593 introducing European science and neo-Confucian concepts - the same Cobo whom Wolf identifies as author of the lost Chinese Doctrina, tying topic (A) to (C). Cauriensia abstract adds that Ricci's accommodation aimed at Ming literati acceptance and that the Tianzhu Shiyi's sharpest philosophical attack targets Cheng-Zhu neo-Confucian ontological monism.

### Figueira 2016 (FULL-FETCHED abstract page)
Frames sixteenth-century Jesuit translations of the Chinese classics as "construction of Confucianism" and "manufacture of the figure of Confucius", and reads Ricci's Tianzhu shiyi specifically "as a work of cultural mistranslation" - the scholarly lens for how missionaries handled Confucian classical text within a Christian hermeneutic.

### Yang 2014 (SEARCH-VERIFIED, summary fully seen)
Verifies Morrison's Dictionary (Macao, East India Company Press, P.P. Thoms, 1815-1823; Part 1 radicals 1815; Part 2 Wuche yunfu alphabetical 1819; Part 3 English-Chinese 1822-23) as the first printed Chinese-English dictionary; Morrison improved inherited transliteration, invented an index matching alphabetized transliteration to characters, and loaded entries with citations from the Chinese classics; his transliteration became the basis of Wade-Giles and his structure the model for Williams (1874) and Giles (1892). Scans verified at Wellcome (public domain, 6 vols.) and archive.org.

### Lee 2021 (+ Xian 2024 companion) (SEARCH-VERIFIED, abstracts)
Lee's abstract states Morrison and Milne overcame the "practical impossibility" of Protestant Bible translation into Chinese (completed 1823), balancing doctrinal constraints, English translation influence (KJV), fidelity to Hebrew, and cultural sensitivity; case studies include Genesis creation and "sea monsters". Xian 2024 compares Morrison's and Yan Fu's translator identities and differing text-selection emphases.

### Hong 2024 (SEARCH-VERIFIED with extensive extracted body text)
Traces a direct terminological inheritance: Ricci's soul theory shaped ling 靈; Morrison's version depended heavily on Jean Basset's Catholic translation plus Emmanuel Diaz and Louis de Poirot, building a ling-rou dichotomy; the Medhurst-Gutzlaff Version readopted "Shangdi 上帝 which originated in Chinese antiquity and had been proposed in the Tianzhu Shiyi" and used Dao for logos. Documents the Term Question precisely: British delegates (Medhurst, W.C. Milne, Stronach) for Shangdi=theos/shen=pneuma vs Americans (Boone, Bridgman, Culbertson) for shen=theos/ling=pneuma; serial polemics in Chinese Repository (1848); flat-ballot vote; committee split; Wang Tao assisting Medhurst; Boone-Medhurst pamphlet exchange including the Cornell-held 1852 Boone Vindication.

### Hanan 2003 (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
HJAS 63(1): 197-239 metadata verified via Crossref/OpenAlex; JSTOR snippet indicates analysis of concrete renderings (e.g., "baptize") and Medhurst's Chinese writings; Hong 2024 cites him for Wang Tao's role in the DV. Standard treatment of the DV as literary wenli achievement; full text unread this session.

### Morrison primary scans + Boone 1852 pamphlet (SEARCH-VERIFIED)
Archive.org advancedsearch verified multiple scans of Morrison's Dictionary (e.g., b2201178x_0004 series, adictionarychin01morrgoog, wucheyunfudictio02morr); Wellcome offers the complete public-domain set. Boone's 1852 Canton-printed Vindication (Cornell Wason Pamphlets, downloadable PDF) is a primary artifact of the Ephesians 1 rendering controversy inside the Delegates' committee.
