## Key extracts seen this session

- Wolf frames four problems: physical description; bibliographical history proving it was the first Philippine imprint; authorship of the text; the actual printing.
- Survival conditions: paper was "one of the most destructible papers ever used in book production"; worms, heat, dampness, fires of 1603, war; "the only copies ... may now be lying uncatalogued in some library there [Europe]".
- Paper discussion quotes T.H. Pardo de Tavera (Noticias sobre La Imprenta y el Grabado en Filipinas, Madrid 1893): "rice paper"/China paper made from Broussonetia papyrifera (paper mulberry), alum-coated, iron-salt darkening explains the browned color of Philippine imprints.
- Precedent framing: first Mexico book (1539) generally accepted as a Doctrina in Mexican and Spanish though no copy extant; Goa Jesuit press 1557 printed St Francis Xavier's Doutrina Christao in Malay (no copy located); Lima 1584 Doctrina survives - "So the choice of this book as the first to be printed at Manila follows a widespread precedent."
- Bibliographical history chain documented: Dasmariñas letter 20 June 1593; 1593 Archives of the Indies account; Jesuit Pedro Chirino's manuscript history (dated 1610) naming Fr Juan de Villanueva OSA ("certain little tracts") and Fr Francisco de San Joseph OP ("larger things of more bulk") as the first printers; Hervas 1785/1787 printing Tagalog Ave Maria and Pater Noster from an extant copy; Adelung's Mithridates and Alter perpetuating the references.
- Retana's 1897 solution quoted verbatim (four conclusions): "(A) That the Doctrinas of 1593, though printed at Manila, were not executed in type, but by the so-called xylographic method; (B) That the initiative for the establishment of typography is owed to P. Fr. Francisco Blancas de San Jose; (C) That the first typographer was the Chinese Christian Juan de Vera at the instigation of the said Father San Jose; (D) That the first typographical printing of this Dominican author is of the year 1602."
- Wolf on authorship: title says "corrected" by priests of more than one order; editorial board reconstruction across Augustinians (Martin de Rada, first linguist of note, learned Visayan and Chinese, compiled a Chinese dictionary per Mendoza), Franciscans (Juan de Plasencia, Miguel de Talavera, Juan de Oliver - the "Talavera-Plasencia-Oliver text" tradition; Plasencia's Doctrina believed lost), Jesuits (Chirino, Henriquez - possible but not probable consultants), Dominicans (Nieva, San Pedro Martyr, Cobo).
- Chirino's chapters on the Tagalog language "reproduce the Tagalog alphabet - its first appearance in a European publication".
- Printing circumstances: Aduarte quoted on Sangleys mastering brush and chisel arts overnight; "To turn over a manuscript copy of a book to a Chinaman who had already some familiarity with the production of books in China ... was then not a matter of great difficulty"; Rada had brought back Chinese woodblock-printed books that "could have provided models for the Spaniards in the Philippines who lacked European facilities for printing".
- Juan de Vera described by Aduarte as "the first printer in these islands", a devout Chinese Christian merchant who established typography out of piety; Wolf supposes "Juan de Vera tried xylographic printing under the supervision of Nieva and San Pedro Martyr, and after some experimenting achieved typography in the time of Blancas de San Jose."
- Discovery announced just as the essay went to press: the Ordinationes Generales of the Dominican province, Binondo, 1604, printed by Juan de Vera, also presented by Rosenwald to LC - bridging the gap toward the 1610 Bataan imprints (Arte y Reglas de la Lengva Tagala; Librong Pagaaralan nang manga Tagalog nang uicang Castilla).
- Woodcut of St Dominic judged "of oriental workmanship": Chinese-style clouds, eastern-temple-like buildings.

## Notes

- Wolf's study is the foundational bibliographic treatment the topic brief asked for; it establishes xylography vs movable type as a technology transfer question mediated by Chinese craftsmen, and identifies the Tagalog text's Franciscan genealogy.
- The essay also documents licensing economics (price control at two vs four reales; cedulas of 1556/1560 requiring royal license for books concerning the Indies; 8 May 1584 order that grammars/dictionaries of indigenous languages be examined by Bishop and Audiencia) - regulatory context for missionary language print.
