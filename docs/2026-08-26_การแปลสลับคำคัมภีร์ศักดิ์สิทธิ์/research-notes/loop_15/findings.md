## Per-source notes

### Ferguson 1959 (WORD 15(2))
Verified on the Taylor & Francis landing page: author affiliation Center for Middle Eastern Studies, Harvard University; pages 325-340; issue WORD Vol. 15, Issue 2 (1959); DOI 10.1080/00437956.1959.11659702; item type "Original Articles"; publisher shows 2,101 CrossRef citations and ~80k views at fetch time. Robert's 2006 essay independently confirms Ferguson 1959 as the coiner of "diglossia". The famous internal apparatus (H/L function lists, four classic case languages) was NOT directly read this session and remains UNVERIFIED pending full-text access.

### Leipzig Glossing Rules (MPI EVA / Univ. Leipzig)
Full rules fetched: ten rules plus a standard abbreviation appendix, jointly developed by Comrie & Haspelmath (MPI EVA) and Bickel (Univ. Leipzig); revised Feb 2008, page last changed May 31, 2015; future maintenance assigned to the Committee of Editors of Linguistics Journals. Key doctrinal quotes captured: glosses give "meanings and grammatical properties of individual words and parts of words"; "Glosses are part of the analysis, not part of the data." Notation conventions verified include hyphens/equal signs (morphemes/clitics), periods for one-to-many correspondences, backslash for ablaut, angle brackets for infixes, tilde for reduplication. Its first "important reference" is Lehmann's interlinear-glossing work, establishing the modern standard's intellectual lineage.

### Lehmann (1980 Guidelines -> 1982 Folia Linguistica -> 2004 HSK chapter)
Lehmann's official publication list verifies a continuous codification track: 1980 working paper "Guidelines for interlinear morphemic translations" (Koln Arbeitspapier 37); revised as "Directions for interlinear morphemic translations", Folia Linguistica 16 (1982), pp. 193-224; and the handbook chapter "Interlinear morphemic glossing" in Morphologie/HSK 17.2 (De Gruyter, 2004), pp. 1834-1857. The Leipzig page itself uses Lehmann's Hittite examples, showing direct descent of current practice from his work. Page-number discrepancies exist across sources (199-224 vs 193-224); author's own list gives 193-224. Chapter full text blocked this session (ResearchGate 403).

### Robert 2006 Hieroglossia (Nanzan Bulletin 30)
Fully verified and full-fetched from the NIRC site: Vol. 30 (2006), pp. 25-48, based on a December 2002 Waseda lecture. Defines hieroglossia as relations between a founding language (hierogloss) and languages ontologically/theologically dependent on it (laogloss), explicitly coined on the model of Ferguson's 1959 diglossia, and states in a footnote that he considers "diglossia a subclass of hieroglossia". Cases developed include Classical Chinese in Japan (kanbun/bungo complex; Chinese textual output possibly exceeding Japanese), Arabic ("the hierogloss par excellence", born with the Qur'an, only juxtalinear glosses tolerated for some non-Arab Muslims), Latin, Hebrew revival debates, Tibetan, Tamil, Syriac, Armenian, Sumerian. Closes with a ten-heading research agenda for sacred-language studies including "the vying for sacrality".

### Pollock 2006 Language of the Gods
Publisher page verified with complete TOC: Part 1 "The Sanskrit Cosmopolis" through Epilogue "From Cosmopolitan-or-Vernacular to Cosmopolitan-and-Vernacular"; abstract confirms Sanskrit "long a sacred language" reinvented as literary/political code around start of Common Era, spread "from Afghanistan to Java", then vernacular challenge from ca. second millennium. Section anchors verified: "Vernacularization and Political Inscription" (Kannada ch.9), "Religion and Vernacularization" (ch.10), "Europe Vernacularized" (ch.11). Paperback ISBN 9780520260030, 706 pp.; awards incl. Coomaraswamy Prize 2008.

### Ricci 2011 Islam Translated
Crossref verifies University of Chicago Press, 2011, book DOI 10.7208/chicago/9780226710907.001.0001; subtitle contains "the Arabic Cosmopolis of South and Southeast Asia", confirming the concept as Islamic counterpart to Pollock's Sanskrit cosmopolis. Review records (Kaptein, History of Religions 2014) confirm pagination xvi+313. Internal content UNVERIFIED this session.

### Fishman 1967
Crossref verifies exact title, Journal of Social Issues 23(2), April 1967, pp. 29-38, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-4560.1967.tb00573.x; also anthologized in The Bilingualism Reader (Routledge). The title alone documents the canonical four-fold extension decoupling bilingualism from diglossia - the standard move enabling analysis of communities whose H/sacred variety has no native speakers. Internal argumentation UNVERIFIED this session.

### Thomure et al. 2025 Arabic diglossia review
DOAJ record captures full abstract: PRISMA systematic review, 1970-2021 literature, 927 records screened, 101 studies included, Social Sciences and Humanities Open 12 (2025). Identifies five themes: diglossic distance, achievement gap, prestige and attitudes, policy, exposure to Standard Arabic. Demonstrates that contemporary empirical work operationalizes Ferguson's model quantitatively ("diglossic distance") and treats prestige/sacral associations of the H variety as a measurable attitude dimension.

### Park 2025 Nomina Sacra
Crossref + DOI resolution verify Journal of Historical Sociolinguistics 11(1): 131-171 (2025), applying "sacred language ideology" to the scribal abbreviation of divine names (nomina sacra) in Greek manuscripts 2nd-5th c. CE. Rare explicit fusion of historical-sociolinguistic ideology theory with scripture-writing practices - adjacent to glossing as indexical sacral behavior. Full text behind bot protection; claims inside UNVERIFIED.

### Dimitrova 2024 Studia Ceranea
Abstract captured: analyzes 21 interlinear and marginal glosses in the 1473 Rila manuscript of the Athonite Slavonic translation of Chrysostom's De Statuis, grouping them into translations/explanations of Greek words, biblical references, synonyms, varia; annotator acted as editor updating language to contemporary terminology. Concrete documented instance of glossing over a sacred-text translation chain (Greek patristic -> Church Slavonic).

### Maslej & Mika 2020 LingVaria
Abstract captured: most surviving Old Polish religious texts are 16th-c. copies with incorporated interlinear/marginal glosses; proposes multi-perspective recognition methodology (semantics, syntax, rhetoric, source studies) using Old Polish apocrypha incl. Rozmyslanie przemyskie; argues proper gloss recognition changes our picture of Polish medieval religious language. Establishes glosses as embedded interpretive layers - the pre-modern counterpart to the Leipzig dictum that glosses are analysis, not data.
