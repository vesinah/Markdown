## Key extracts (from the fetched PDF)

- Definition: hieroglossia is "the sum of relations that develop between a language perceived as a central or founding element in a given culture area (this language being the hierogloss) and a language or languages that are perceived as being dependent, not historically or linguistically, but ontologically or theologically, on that hierogloss."
- The dependent language is called the "laogloss" ("vulgar tongue" or "vernacular"), "clearly considered not to be self-sufficient"; it borrows massively from the hierogloss or undertakes "reconstructing its own vocabulary, reorientating its conceptual links on the basis of the hierogloss"; innovations in the laogloss are understood "as an exegesis of the hieroglossic original, but not a true innovation."
- Coinage: he coined the term himself, inspired by Ferguson's diglossia: the neologism was "inspired as well from the neologism diglossia, coined by Charles Ferguson in 1959". He notes ancient Greek hieroglossos meant "speaking a divine or prophetic language" and claims first use of the term in this sense.
- On diglossia: "diglossia [is] a very useful category for encompassing a variety of socio-linguistic situations from the creole languages to Modern Greek. If hieroglossia is reduced to a particular aspect of diglossia ... we will land ourselves back in the same pattern of social domination"; footnote 9: "I persist in considering diglossia a subclass of hieroglossia."
- Japan case: from end of Heian until end of WWII even legal texts were written in a frozen pseudo-classical bungo; Japanese Civil Code kept classical-style language into recent decades. The bungo/modern-Japanese diglossia sat inside "a larger hieroglossic complex" with kanbun: Classical Chinese "has been in use in the Japanese archipelago longer than the Japanese language itself", and total Chinese-language textual output in Japan may have exceeded Japanese-language output.
- Arabic case: "written Arabic was virtually born with the Koran"; Koranic style considered "inimitable", pastiche-like imitation deemed blasphemy; Arabic is "the hierogloss par excellence"; "In Arabic, all the failures that are attributed to diglossia are rectified by the simple fact of hieroglossia."
- Glossing link: for Islam spreading among non-Arabic speakers, only "juxtalinear translations - glosses, so to speak - were tolerated" for certain languages; full translation not encouraged.
- Other cases named: Latin in Europe (critiquing Francoise Waquet's Le latin ou l'empire d'un signe), Hebrew and its revival (citing Paul Wexler and Shlomo Izre'el debates), Tibetan, Tamil, Mongolian, Armenian, Syriac, Sumerian.
- Closing research agenda: hieroglossia is "essentially a religious phenomenon" which "falls in the main outside the scope of sociolinguistics since there is no need for a minimum number of persons to make it worthy of study"; ten proposed research headings include: discourse of supremacy of a sacred language; exegesis performed in same or another language; translations of religious texts; shift/transfer/adaptation of religious vocabulary; "The access of a 'vulgar' language to religious status: the vying for sacrality"; liturgical and magic use; language purism and religion.
- Bibliography includes Pollock, Sheldon: "Cosmopolitan and Vernacular in History," Public Culture 12-3 (Fall, 2000), 591-626, and Pollock's The Language of the Gods in the World of Men (Chicago, 2006) - direct published linkage between the hieroglossia and Sanskrit cosmopolis literatures.
