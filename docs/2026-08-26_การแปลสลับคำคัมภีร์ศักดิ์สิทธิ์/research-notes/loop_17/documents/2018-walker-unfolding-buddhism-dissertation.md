## Key content on nissaya/bitext scholarship (ch. 6 "Bilingual Pali-Vernacular Prose," diss. pp. 317–376)

1. **Section 6.2.1 "Burmese Nissaya And Sinhalese Sannaya" (diss. 318–326).**

2. **Tin Lwin taxonomy (fn. 17):** "The Burmese tradition of bilingual texts, or nissaya, has received by far the most academic attention in Theravada studies, beginning with Tin Lwin, whose MA thesis presents four types of nissaya (verbatim, free translation, ornate, and translation with short notes)" — Tin Lwin, "A Study of Pali-Burmese Nissaya" (SOAS, University of London, 1961), 6–11, cited via D. Christian Lammerts, Cornell diss. 2010, 229–230. Also recorded: **U Nyunt Maung's alternative tripartite division** (extended gloss, condensed gloss, explanatory) per Lammerts 2010: 229; and **Pyi Phyo Kyaw's diss. (King's College London, 2014, 192–196) on the ayakauk/akauk nissaya type** presenting vernacular translation as extended Abhidhamma exegesis.

3. **Kirichenko-nissaya resolution (fn. 17):** "Other scholars, including Erik Braun, Patrick Pranke, and Alexey Kirichenko, use nissaya texts in their analyses of the history of Buddhist practices and institutions, though without specifically analyzing their linguistic features" — citing Kirichenko, "Dynamics of Monastic Mobility and Networking in the Seventeenth- and Eighteenth-Century Upper Burma," in *Buddhist Dynamics in Premodern and Early Modern Southeast Asia*, ed. D. Christian Lammerts (Singapore: ISEAS–Yusof Ishak Institute, 2015), 344–347. CRITICAL: "**Kirichenko suggests that no fewer than seven different kinds of nissaya can be distinguished in Burmese manuscripts (Kirichenko, personal communication, January 22, 2017).**" His typology is UNPUBLISHED. Walker concludes Tin Lwin's and Nyunt Maung's taxonomies "may not be extensive enough."

4. **Okell/Pruitt assessment (diss. 322–324):** "The most linguistically detailed analyses of Burmese nissaya come from the work of John Okell and William Pruitt." Long Okell quotes (Lingua 15:187, 188–189) incl. "nothing to distinguish a nissaya rendering of 1491 from one of 1910"; Walker fn. 21 concedes Okell's insistence on changelessness and inattention to nissaya-form diversity "may need revisiting." Walker's own definition: nissaya is "an approach to grammatical analysis, translation, and composition... that may be used to create philologically exact vernacular translations, bilingual texts..., or even vernacular texts that incorporate a few Pali words and may not even be based on a Pali original" (fn. 24 sketches a spectrum-of-bilingualism research agenda).

5. **Pruitt citation detail:** Pruitt, Étude linguistique de nissaya birmans cited at diss. 321 fn. 9 for Western missionary/scholar use of bilingual texts (Burnouf, Duroiselle, Childers, Rhys-Davids).

6. **Khmer terminology (section 6.2.2 onward):** Khmer *samrāy* treated as the Khmer counterpart genre with three prose forms paralleling Tai practices; Siamese yok sap (ยกศัพท์) "lifting words" tradition; contemporary ṕlè yak śăb(d) manuals footnoted (Mahāmakuṭ 2535; Puñsị̄p Insār 2553; Braḥ Mahā Ṕaṭhamabaṅ 2532).
