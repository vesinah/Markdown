## Per-source notes

### Lee Yong 2011 (kugyol research overview)
Defines kugyol as a convention for adding Korean grammatical markers at appropriate places to aid reading originally Chinese texts, and presents the standard three-way typology: eumdok kugyol (transliteration), seokdok kugyol (translation), jeomto seokdok-kugyol (translation plus point marks). Reports that kugyol studies advanced sharply after the 2000 discovery of Yogacarabhumi-sastra (Yugasajiron) material with detailed point maps scratched into Tripitaka prints (gakpil/hakpil marks). Some decipherment questions for jeomto seokdok-kugyol remain open per the abstract. Its full captured reference list maps the specialist field (Chung, Nam Pung-hyun, Kobayashi Yoshinori, Lee Seung-jae cipher-kugyol decipherment volumes).

### Jang Yoonhee 2004 (seokdokkugyol survey)
Kugyol splits into eumdokkugyol and seokdokkugyol; seokdokkugyol unpacked Chinese originals into nearly perfect Korean sentences and thus shares many features with 15th-century eonhae while explaining some of that genre's peculiar phenomena. It did not fully overcome Chinese-Korean syntax gaps - clause splitting persists versus eonhae. Exactly six seokdokkugyol witnesses are known, all Buddhist: Seok-hwaeom-gyo-bun-gi (mid-10th c.), Hwaeom-gyeong vols. 35 and 14 (12th c.), Geumgwangmyeong-gyeong vol. 3, Inwang-gyeong vol. 1 (mid-13th c. onward), Yugasajiron vol. 20 (late 13th c.). Their transcription practice closely resembles hyangchal, aiding hyangga decipherment. Companion AKS entry (SEARCH-VERIFIED): Hwaeom-gyeong-so vol. 35 uses 61 kugyol characters 4,802 times; right/left to positions encode Korean word-order reordering; example gloss supplies native case markers, negation, and causative morphology.

### Chung Jaeyoung 2010 (idu / hyangchal / gugyeol)
Cleanest typological statement found this session: idu and hyangchal transcribe vernacular Korean, whereas gugyeol serves to translate/read Chinese originals via meaning-based Korean insertions. Gugyol development sequence: to-mark reading-order indication, then symbol-gugyeol with jeomto marks, then interpretative-reading gugyeol with to marks - paralleling idu's development. Companion piece locates idu's culmination in Silla from the mid-sixth century using wooden tablets, and argues Silla idu underlies BOTH hyangchal's stem-meaning/ending-sound principle AND gugyeol's insertion of morphological affixes between Chinese sentences. Keywords include yeokdokseon (reversal line).

### Kim Ki-jong 2018 (Joseon eonhae Buddhist compilation)
Eonhae Buddhist compilation concentrated in the 15th-16th centuries; late Joseon saw reprints of old eonhae but almost no new ones (only Quonnyom Yorok and Nyeombul Bogweonmun). Early Gangyeongdogam eonhae divide into commentaries on Mahayana sutras and Zen books, matching the ordination-examination sutra-recitation curriculum; their chief purpose was MONK education. Sixteenth-century regional temple editions expanded Zen books and added esoteric sutras. Late Joseon eonhae functioned as edification books for laypeople (Eunjeong-gyeong eonhae circulation, Jijang-gyeong eonhae recut from Wolinseokbo vol. 21) - a dual character: monks' textbook (early) vs mass edification book (late).

### Kim Mu-bong 2023 (Beophwa-gyeong eonhae layout)
Beophwa-gyeong eonhae (1463, woodblock, 7 vols.) is the second Gangyeongdogam eonhae after Neungeom; base text = Kumarajiva's sutra + Gyehwan's Yaojie + Iryeo's Jizhu, with King Sejo personally adding jeongeum kugyol. Page architecture: glossed source text above; circle mark; small double-column Korean translation below; untranslated Chinese commentary inserted verbatim; text sectioned by kebun divisions. Contrast established across genres: Seokbosangjeol/Wolinseokbo print translations ONLY (freer register, sometimes excessive native paraphrase), whereas Beophwa approaches word-for-word daeyeok built on the kugyol layer - evidence of a drift from free toward literal translation as the canonical eonhae format crystallized. Same author's KCI study lists all nine Gangyeongdogam vernacular titles (1462-1467) and the shared layout conventions (large-type kugyol text, small paired-column eonhae, circle separator, black fish-tail marks flanking interpolated commentary).

### AKS entry: Neungeom-gyeong eonhae
Neungeom translation began 1449 under Sejong/Prince Suyang, completed by Sejo: movable type 1461 (400 copies), Gangyeongdogam woodblock 1462, reprinted 1472 and 1495. Workflow per royal prefaces: Sejo supplied kugyol; monk Sinmi adjudicated parsing; Han Gyeohui and Kim Su-on drafted Korean; eminent monks corrected; Sejo finalized - i.e., institutional collaborative translation with named division of labor. Style is thoroughly literal; layout shows large-character glossed source followed by paired-line translation. Orthography deliberately differs between the kugyol layer and the translation layer (tone dots and doubled clusters only in translation), and every hanja carries a Dongguk Jeongeun-standardized reading annotated by named phonology specialists. It became the formatting model for later Gangyeongdogam eonhae.

### Kim Seong Ju 2015 (Seokbosangjeol vs Wolinseokbo)
Wolinseokbo (1459) fuses King Sejong's Wolincheongangjigok songs with Sejo's Seokbosangjeol (1447) as running commentary; parinirvana content forced relocation of Seokbosangjeol vol. 11 material to vol. 21. Quantified register comparison via retention ratio: Seokbosangjeol 49 percent vs Wolinseokbo 97 percent of base-text content retained (Gangyeongdogam baseline 100 percent) - a measurable free-to-literal spectrum among 15th-century vernacularizations of Buddhist material. Companion lexical study (Jo Hyunjin 2019, Niigata) confirms same-scripture passages were rendered differently due to strict-vs-liberal choices. Structural details from sillokwiki/AKS entries (SEARCH-VERIFIED): songs first then commentary; hangul/hanja ordering reversed relative to Wolincheongangjigok; native words partly replaced by harder Sino-Korean words; Hunminjeongeum eonhae printed at the front of vol. 1.

### Ross King 2015 (against diglossia)
Proposes 'Sinographic Cosmopolis' (after Pollock) for Literary-Sinitic-using East Asia and argues 'diglossia' has little or no utility for premodern Korea's speech-writing ecology because intermediate inscriptional practices abounded. Related abstracts captured this session state the Choson inscriptional spectrum included idu and kugyeol rather than a binary of Literary Sinitic vs vernacular eonhae, and that literati notions of yeok 譯 make these practices look intralingual rather than interlingual - raising whether Literary Sinitic was even conceived as foreign. King's other work seen this session extends this frame: 'Choson-style hanmun' research history (2023), the Western Wing glossarial complex and marginal glossing as engines of vernacularization, and the edited volume Cosmopolitan and Vernacular in the World of Wen.

### Sejong Korea DB et al.: Dusiwonhae
First vernacular-rendered poetry collection: Du Fu's 1,647 poems + 16 others classified into 71 gates, translated by Yu Yun-gyeom, Yu Huibok, Jo Wi, monk Uichim on Seongjong's orders (commission autumn 1481), Eulhaeja movable type, 25 vols. Distinctive negative datum: unlike Buddhist sutra eonhae, Dusiwonhae attaches NO to/interpuncts to the original and NO hangul readings on hanja inside its mixed-script translation - poetic eonhae chose unmarked mixed-script paraphrase. A Jangseogak-based study of reused examination-paper stock shows printing cannot precede winter 1482, so the standard 1481 date may need revision. First edition survives incomplete (missing vols. 1,2,4,5,12); 1632 woodblock reprint reflects 17th-century language, making first-vs-reprint comparison a Middle-to-Early Modern Korean diagnostic.

### Nguyen 2026 (Vietnamese reversal marks)
First direct evidence surfaced this session that Vietnam possessed kundoku/seokdok-kugyeol-like gloss reading: reversal marks in Han Nom manuscripts of the Canh Phuoc Collection (Kyoto University) indicate reordering classical Chinese syntax to Vietnamese word order, alongside extensive Nom-script glossing. Abstract explicitly frames Vietnam as theorized-but-understudied parallel to kanbun kundoku and Korean seokdok kugyeol. Its full captured reference list maps the nascent field: Kosukegawa & Whitman 2018; Shimizu Masaaki 2024 on a Sino-Nom Fo shuo tian di ba yang jing manuscript; Nguyen T.T.H. 2020/2025 Japanese-language work on Vietnamese punctuation/kun-reading and Analects commentaries; Phan 2024 on the exegetical Nom particle chung in Co chau phap van phat ban hanh ngu luc; Washizawa 2019 on Sinitic-Nom particle correspondences in Truyen ky man luc. ### Arcodia & Phan 2025 (Analects bilingual corpus)
Documents a 17th-century Nom bilingual edition of the Analects (Luan ngu uoc giai within Tu thu uoc giai) that translates and annotates the Four Books into Vietnamese in Nom script WITH Literary Sinitic annotations retained - structurally parallel to Korean facing-format eonhae. Emperor Tu Duc's 19th-century 2,970-line luc bat verse rendition (court-engraved c. 1896-1907) shows monarch-led vernacularization of Confucian scripture. Negation-transfer analysis quantifies assimilation: 17th-c. translator used only native chang for 不/無/非; khong and bat enter by the 19th c.; khong dominates today. Also records (citing Nguyen T.C. 2016) that all nine major Confucian classics including the Analects were translated into Nom during the monarchal period, and dates the Han-Viet reading system to the 8th-9th centuries.

### Hoang Thi Ngo 2022 (fifteenth-century sutra giai am)
Studies a Han-dien-Nom filial-piety sutra (Phat thuyet dai bao phu mau an trong kinh) whose surviving woodblock predates 1730 but whose exemplar she dates to the LATE FIFTEENTH CENTURY - contemporaneous with Korea's earliest Hangul eonhae. Because it is sacred text, reprinters preserved the archaic gloss layer without emendation, so it preserves early Nom two-code spellings evidencing Old Vietnamese sesqui-syllabicity (bl-/kl-/ml- clusters; preglottalized initials; la-da 'hail'). Monograph includes full transcription/annotation plus a frequency lexicon of pure Nom words. Same-session adjacent items: Huong Hai thien su (1628-1715) produced ~20 Nom sutra exegeses incl. a prose Lotus-Sutra gloss (AB.488: large-print Chinese Kumarajiva text, smaller Nom translation beneath, term explanations appended); Phap Lien's 1848 verse Phap hoa quoc ngu kinh (3,214 luc bat lines, printed 1856); a colophon-dated nom Lotus edition of 1747 (Canh Hung yr. 8) in the Han-Nom library.

## Open questions

1. What exactly do the Vietnamese reversal marks in the Canh Phuoc Collection look like, and how systematic were they? (Nguyen 2026 body is paywalled; needs full-text access or library copy.)
2. Do Vietnamese gloss practices constitute a continuous tradition comparable to Korean seokdok-kugyol or Japanese kunten, or scattered manuscript habits? Kosukegawa & Whitman 2018 and Nguyen T.T.H.'s Tohoku dissertation are the key unread items.
3. Was there any Vietnamese analogue to Korea's royal eonhae bureau (Gangyeongdogam)? Nothing found this session suggests a state sutra-translation office; Huong Hai's and Tu Duc's projects appear monastic/individual or court-commissioned post hoc - needs verification.
4. Relative chronology of Vietnamese Buddhist vernacularization: Viên Thái's Nôm rendering in Co chau luc (possibly late 16th-early 17th c. over a 14th-c. prototype) vs the claimed late-fifteenth-century giai-am of Bao phu mau an kinh - can the latter dating be independently corroborated?
5. Dusiwonhae completion/printing date: does the winter-1482 terminus from reused examination paper hold across other copies, and does it change linguistic dating of the text?
6. Why did poetic eonhae like Dusiwonhae omit kugyol/to layers entirely while sutra eonhae kept them - reader audience, genre convention, or economics of movable type? Sources describe the difference but none seen this session explains it.
7. Open decipherment problems in jeomto seokdok-kugyol flagged by Lee 2011: which specific point-map interpretations remain contested?
8. Does the Nom particle chưng (Phan 2024) function as a glossing/reading aid analogous to kugyol particles inserted into Chinese syntax, or purely as a translation element in the Nôm column?
