## Per-source notes

1. **Doctrina Christiana (Lima 1584)** -- First book printed in Lima (Medina no. 1, per scan description), issued from the press of Antonio Ricardo under a special Audiencia license dated 13 Feb 1584 with Jesuit oversight (Atienza, Acosta plus two translation participants). The "Epistola sobre la traduccion" records that the council's Cartilla, Catecismos, Confessionario and Preparacion para morir were translated into "the two general languages" Quichua and Aymara, and forbids any other translation, explicitly on the model of the Church's regulation of Scripture translations. The Latin decree orders bishops to commission approved translations into all other local languages via synods. The "Annotaciones o scolios" reveal translation doctrine: sense-for-sense rather than word-for-word, a middle register avoiding both rustic provincial Quechua and obscure Cuzco refinement, and deliberately broad vocabulary in Aymara to cover many ethnic groups.

2. **Sahagun Psalmodia Christiana (Mexico 1583)** -- Title couples Psalmodia with Sermonario de los Sanctos del Ano in Mexican (Nahuatl), arranged into cantares/psalms so Indians can sing them in church areytos; printed by Pedro Ocharte, 237 pp., 4to. A Larramendi facsimile with full OCR derivatives is on archive.org. One year earlier than the Lima Doctrina; concrete Mesoamerican counterpart to topic (D).

3. **Hovdhaugen ed., ...and the Word was God (1996)** -- The founding publication of "missionary linguistics" as a named field, Nodus 1996, ISBN 3893231250. Contains Hovdhaugen's programmatic chapter "Missionary grammars. An attempt at defining a field of research" (pp. 9-22), confirmed via Zwartjes's ORE reference list. Reviewed promptly in Language 74(2) 1998 (DOI 10.1353/lan.1998.0209) and Historiographia Linguistica 24:3 (Altman).

4. **Zwartjes & Hovdhaugen, Missionary Linguistics I-VI (Benjamins SIHOLS 106/109/111/114/122/130, 2004-2021)** -- Series grew out of the First International Conference on Missionary Linguistics (Oslo, 13-16 Mar 2003). Volume I's abstract states the field premise: missionaries decided indigenous languages would be the means of christianization, hence grammars and dictionaries; papers cover Spanish, Portuguese, English, French traditions across continents (Maya, Quechua, Xhosa, Japanese, Kapampangan etc.). Volume II = orthography/phonology; III = morphology/syntax; V = translation theories/practices including Zwartjes on mise en page of translated missionary texts; VI appeared 2021.

5. **Zwartjes, Portuguese Missionary Grammars (2011)** -- First monograph on the entire Portuguese grammatical tradition outside Portugal (Tamil, Indo-Aryan languages, Japanese; Kipea, Tupinamba; Kimbundu, Sena), analyzing orthography, parts of speech, morphology/syntax, pedagogy, variation, pragmatics, and missionaries' attitudes. Standard reference (72 Crossref citations); reviewed by Mixco, Fernandes, Baxter.

6. **Zwartjes, "Missionary Grammars" (ORE 2018)** -- Defines the genre: pedagogical descriptions in missionary-colonial settings, prologue + phonology/orthography + parts-of-speech morphology + optional syntax/word lists, modeled on Greco-Latin framework but adapted to new typologies. Its reference list is a compact map of the colonial American artes (1560 Quechua Grammatica; 1586 Arte y Vocabulario... del Peru; 1603/1612 Aymara artes; 1578 Zapotec; 1595 Mexicana; etc.).

7. **Durston, Pastoral Quechua (2007)** -- Notre Dame Press monograph on how post-conquest priests attempted to "incarnate" Christianity in Quechua, codifying and imposing a single written standard based on Cuzco Quechua through translations of devotional, catechetical and liturgical parish texts; treats syncretic iconography, colonial linguistic ideologies/policies, and control mechanisms in liturgy, text organization and grammar. Spanish translation El quechua pastoral (PUCP 2019). Companion chapter "Standard Colonial Quechua" (Chicago 2014, pp. 225-243).

8. **Burkhart, The Slippery Earth (1989)** -- Arizona UP study of Nahua-Christian moral dialogue in sixteenth-century Mexico (xii+242 pp.), verified against three peer reviews (AHR 1994, American Ethnologist 1991, JAH 1990). Frames evangelization as two-way moral discourse conducted largely through Nahuatl Christian texts.

9. **Lockhart, The Nahuas after the Conquest (1992)** -- Stanford UP social/cultural history of Central Mexican Indians 16th-18th c. built on alphabetic Nahuatl documentation; four scholarly reviews verified (Gerhard HAHR 1993; Chance Ethnohistory 1993; Van Young AHR 1994; Hamnett BHS 1995). FCE Spanish edition 1999.

10. **Taylor on Molina's Quechua sermons (BIFEA 2001)** -- Edition of a Lenten sermon cycle completed at Huánuco in Feb 1649 by curate Diego de Molina: Castilian original fragment plus its contemporary Quechua translation, normalized Quechua added; manuscript partially burned in the 1943 Biblioteca Nacional del Peru fire. Taylor also published Sermones y ejemplos (IFEA 2002), a Quechua sermon/exempla collection, and edited Huarochiri texts.

11. **Ezcurra Rivero on Avendaño's sermonario (Allpanchis 2012)** -- Analyzes persuasive/pedagogical strategies ("discursive rhythm shifts", "semantic-referential licences") in Avendaño's Sermones de los misterios de nuestra santa fe catolica, Lima 1649, devised for Andean evangelization. Corroborated by Puertas Porras (Studium Veritatis 2002) and Cortés Alcaide (Trocadero 2024).

12. **Getty page on Florentine Codex** -- Confirms August 1576 production at Santa Cruz de Tlaltelolco by Sahagun with 22 indigenous artists; 12 books; "parallel columns of text in Spanish and Nahuatl"; over 2,000 watercolors; Ms. Med. Palat. 220, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana.

## Key findings

- The Benjamins research field is traceable in two steps: Hovdhaugen's "...and the Word was God" (Nodus 1996) defined missionary linguistics as a field; Zwartjes & Hovdhaugen then institutionalized it at John Benjamins (SIHOLS 106, first conference volume 2004, series continuing to vol. VI in 2021), with Zwartjes's Portuguese monograph (SIHOLS 117, 2011) as its comparative non-Hispanic counterpart.
- The Lima 1584 Doctrina Christiana (first book printed in Lima, Antonio Ricardo) institutionalized trilingual pastoral printing (Spanish-Quichua-Aymara) and an official translation monopoly: only council-approved translations could be used, justified by analogy with ecclesiastical control of Scripture translations -- direct evidence for topic (D)'s vernacular-vs-Latin policy question.
- The 1584 volume contains explicit translation theory: sense-for-sense over word-for-word, a deliberate middle register between "tosco/corrupto" provincial speech and refined Cuzco usage, and pan-Aymara lexical leveling -- i.e., standardization goals stated in the translators' own words.
- Durston's Pastoral Quechua (2007) provides the modern synthesis matching those primary statements: codification/imposition of a Cuzco-based written standard, catechetical-liturgical text production, linguistic ideology, and disciplinary performance practices.
- Mesoamerican parallels are strong: Sahagun's Psalmodia Christiana y Sermonario (Mexico 1583) shows sermonarios in Nahuatl adapted to indigenous song (areytos), while the Florentine Codex (begun 1576 at Tlaltelolco) physically displays the parallel-column Spanish-Nahuatl mise en page that Zwartjes (SIHOLS V chapter, 2014) analyzes as a teaching device.
- Burkhart (1989) and Lockhart (1992) jointly define the Nahuatl-Christian corpus scholarship: discourse/moral dialogue vs social history of native-language documents.
- In Peru, sermonarios beyond the official corpus existed at parish level: Diego de Molina's Quechua Lent sermons (Huánuco 1649, edited by Taylor) and Fernando de Avendaño's Sermones de los misterios (Lima 1649) show bilingual composition and rhetorical engineering for Andean evangelization.
