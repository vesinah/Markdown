1. Introduction





At Prohear in Prey Veng province in southeastern Cambodia
(Fig. 1), one of the richest prehistoric sites in Southeast Asia was
unearthed during three rescue excavations conducted from 2008 to
2011 by the German Archaeological Institute in cooperation with
the Memot Centre in Phnom Penh (Reinecke et al., 2009, in press).
The large Iron Age cemetery, situated in the centre of the Prohear
village, had been looted almost completely during 2007 and
probably contained more than 1000 precious metal ornaments and
unique bronze items. Nevertheless, 76 burials dating mainly from
the end of the 2nd century BC to the 1st century AD were excavated
in the area of the main road, where at least some of the graves had
remained intact.

The site is assigned to the Pre-Funan culture, which is characterised by inhumation burials with the dead placed on their back,
wrapped in a fibre mat with their clothes, ornaments and implements surrounded by a number of ceramic vessels. The burial
custom, head orientation, the arrangement of the offerings and the
pottery types link Prohear well to other known cemeteries of the
local Pre-Funan culture (Fig. 1). But in its second phase, to which
most of the burials belong, also other influences not typical for PreFunan appear. For example, stone pestles or a bronze bell were
found between the thighs, and in the richest burials the head was
deposited in a bronze drum or covered by a bronze bowl. Furthermore, offerings like 33 Heger-I bronze drums in Dong Son style e
the majority just reported by looters, bronze dishes, bracelets with
buffalo-horn-shaped ends and certain stone beads have to be
considered as imported elite goods (Reinecke et al., 2009, in press).

The quantity of precious metal ornaments is quite outstanding,
compared to the finds from other Iron Age sites (Miksic, 2011:
40e42). A total number of 93 gold and silver objects were found in
32 of the burials, while three more gold pieces were recovered in
the excavation units and 16 objects from looted burials could be

The most specific archaeological information is provided by 14

depiction of a horseman (Fig. 14:3) is very interesting in view of the
presence of non-local features. Horses are not native in Southeast
Asia, and horseman images are not found as decoration on Early
Iron Age objects in the southern part of Southeast Asia, but are very
popular in the Dian culture of Yunnan, south China. However, goldsilver finger rings with a broadened head are neither typical of Dian
nor of Han dynasty (206 BCe220 AD) or any early East Asian
cultures. Therefore, it is likely that this finger ring was made in the
tradition of workshops of Bactria or around the northwest of India,
but its shape and decoration style are still clearly different.

Altogether, the finds from Prohear give the impression that an
‘import wave’ of gold and silver objects arrived together with other
non-local artefacts. As discussed in detail in Reinecke et al. (2009:

The present knowledge on ancient Southeast Asian exploitation
of precious metal resources and trade routes is still mainly based on
the early written Indian and Chinese records. They cover the time
from the Han period to the arrival of the Europeans in Southeast
Asia in the 16th century and do not mention any gold deposits,
mining or gold production in Cambodia (Reinecke et al., 2009:
156e157), but e in context of Cambodia e refer to gold and silver
from China (e.g. Zhou, 1297/2007: 71). Nevertheless, gold deposits
exist and are presently being exploited in Cambodia (Zhou et al.,
2003; Sotham, 2004), as well as in many other regions of Southeast Asia (Bennett, 2009). Apart from the larger deposits in use
today, also smaller occurrences of gold veins or placer gold have to
be considered as resources in earlier times. There is some archaeological record of ancient gold mining from Sumatra, Laos and



central Vietnam, the latter being the closest to Prohear (Bennett,
2009: 100; Lê, 1776/2007: 282e287). Silver deposits are less
frequent in Southeast Asia, but are known from the ‘silver road’
between southwestern China and northeastern Myanmar, which
existed since the Han dynasty period (Yang, 2004; Fiskesjö, 2010)
and was connected with southeast Cambodia by the Mekong River.

In view of this background, the aim of the chemical analysis of
a first selection of gold and silver objects was to reveal information
about the types of raw material used for their production and to
establish groups which characterise the artefacts in terms of
composition and trace elements. In addition to the Prohear finds,
two gold earrings from Bit Meas, a contemporary but completely
looted Iron Age cemetery situated 8 km south of Prohear, were also
included in this study for comparison.

two from Bit Meas e were either sampled in Cambodia (31 larger
and more valuable objects) or transferred to Germany for direct

LA-ICP-MS, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with attached
energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDX) and energy dispersive
X-ray fluorescence (EDXRF) at the Curt-Engelhorn-Centre for
Archaeometry (CEZA) in Mannheim.

3. Results and discussion


3.1. Composition, alloys and gilding


The artefacts show a wide range of composition from practically
pure silver (99%) to high-grade gold (95%), electrum with about 60%
gold as the most frequent alloy (Table 4). The copper concentrations, around 0.3% in most samples, show a tendency to higher
values in the silver rich samples, but rarely exceed 1%, which is not
different to the range in native gold-silver alloys (Boyle, 1979),
although in alluvial placer gold generally lower concentrations can
be found due to the depletion of copper and also silver (Morteani,
1995; Chapman et al., 2006).
Five samples show higher contents of 3e5% and 7%, indicating
that copper was added in some way. In these hypoeutectic alloys,
the copper content is lower than the maximum solubility of copper
in the aAg-phase. They typically consist of two phases in the cast
condition, due to the rapid change of solid solubility of copper in
silver with decreasing temperature (Fig. 4). Copper increases
strength and workability and a range of properties can be obtained
by different heat treatment, which could explain an intentional
addition of even small quantities. However, copper at these
concentration levels would have no effect on the colour of the
alloys because of the high silver contents (Cretu and van der Lingen,
1999).

In five samples, tin contents of 2e4% were detected. The
regarding alloys vary in composition from 72 to 29% gold and can be
grouped into AueAgeSn and AgeAueCueSn alloys. A contamination with tin from the addition of bronze is not even probable in the
two samples with high copper content, regarding the Cu:Sn ratios
of 3:3 and 5:2, and there is no reason that the tin should have been
added deliberately. But there is a certain correlation of gold and tin,
suggesting a connection between the two elements. Usually e and
also in most samples of the present study e tin is present as minor
or trace element (Guerra et al., 1999; Brostoff et al., 2009) that
indicates that the gold was extracted from a tin-bearing placer
deposit (Dube, 2006). It seems to be an important element for the
comparison of artefact gold with placer gold (Ehser et al., 2011).
Most likely it enters the gold through joint panning of placer gold
with cassiterite (SnO2) and consequent melting under reducing
conditions (Dube, 2006). Native tin has also been reported from
gold deposits (Schmiderer et al., 2007). Considering the proximity
of the Southeast Asian tin belt, and in view of the archaeological
evidence pointing to a relation with northern Vietnam, especially
the rich tin deposits of the Yunnan province (Golas, 1999), it cannot
be ruled out that even such high concentrations of tin in gold could
be of natural origin.

The samples with high tin content are perhaps exclusively
related to foil-fusion gilding, which in contrast to common foil
gilding has not been reported from Southeast Asia to our knowledge so far (Bunker, 2008). This technique could be identified in
three objects, and the gilding foil was made from AgeAue(Cu)eSn

Scott, 2000), very scarce for the Near East (Oddy, 2000), but
unknown from Southeast Asia so far (Bunker, 2008). In many of the
studied objects, the yellow surface colour is not consistent with the
measured gold content of the alloy. As there is no evidence for foil
gilding in these objects, we suspect that they are depletion gilded
as well.

The concentrations of the platinum-group elements (PGE) and
their ratios are useful and decisive factors for gold fingerprinting
(Guerra et al., 1999; Brostoff et al., 2009). The presence of PGE
indicates that the gold was extracted from secondary deposits, i.e.
placers (Meeks and Tite, 1980), as they generally derive from
different rock types and primary deposits than gold. In analogy to
cassiterite, platinum-group minerals can be included in a gold
placer and are panned together with the gold. During melting and
processing, Pd, Rh and Pt distribute homogenously in the gold,
while Ru, Os and Ir remain as minute inclusions in the gold matrix
(Meeks and Tite, 1980; Dussubieux and Van Zelst, 2004; Brostoff
et al., 2009), whereas Ir tends to appear also outside these inclusions, showing a somewhat intermediate behaviour. As platinumgroup minerals appear in placers all over Southeast Asia (Weiser,
2002), the presence of PGE in ancient gold is not unusual and
does not delimitate the area of origin of the gold.

Based on the Pd/Pt ratios and the concentrations of Pt normalised to gold and of Bi, two main groups can be distinguished
(Fig. 10). Group I comprises the majority of the samples and is
characterised by higher Pd/Pt ratios averaging around 0.15 and by
low Ptnorm and Bi concentrations. Group II with lower Pd/Pt ratios
averaging about 0.03 shows higher concentrations of Bi and Ptnorm,
the latter being accompanied by the occurrence of minute
RueOseIr inclusions (which were excluded from the signal evaluation). Iridium concentrations also support the two groups,
whereas tin and lead appear more erratic and are not discriminating (Table 4). Lead concentrations exceed 200 mg kg <sup>1</sup> only in
two samples, which is regarded as the maximum concentration in
native gold (Guerra et al., 1999). The presence of tin again points to
an alluvial origin for both groups (see Section 3.1).

According to the silver content, the unnormalised Pt concentrations and typological aspects, three subgroups can be established within group I (Fig.11): gold (Ia), electrum (Ib) and auriferous
silver (Ic). (The term auriferous silver is used in a more general way
in this paper to refer to silver dominated alloys.)

The gold and electrum subgroups show similar ranges of Sn and
unnormalised Pt concentrations and may well be interpreted as
natural alloys deriving from placers with similar geological background. The Ia gold objects, three ribbed earrings, three cylindrical



sheets and a unique small, band-shaped open ring with ribbed
ornament, exclusively appear in this subgroup (Fig.13), underlining
its definition. They probably come from a specialised workshop,
which also provided the two ribbed earrings from Bit Meas that fit

well to the single one from Prohear. The gold object not grouped to
Ia (MA-092124), although still considered as group I, is different:
The thick, U-shaped open ring with one cut end is well burnished
and without parallels among the finds.

The Ib electrum objects in contrast mainly consist of spiral rings
and simple open rings, corresponding in style and technique to the
objects made of auriferous silver (Ic). In fact, Ib and Ic represent one
single workshop and can only be distinguished from each other
chemically, as the artefacts show the same variety of yellow to
yellowish golden surface colour probably due to depletion gilding.
But the chemical difference is interesting: subgroup Ic with higher
silver contents than Ib shows lower unnormalised Pt and Sn
concentrations. As Fig. 12 demonstrates, decreasing Au concentrations correlate with decreasing Pt concentrations between the two
subgroups, indicating that both elements were diluted by the
addition of silver, i.e. that the Ic auriferous silver was produced by
alloying high-grade silver to the Ib electrum. Most probably,
subgroup Ic represents an artificial alloy, as a geological origin is not
very likely for auriferous silver (Rehren et al., 1996).

The group II objects are mainly made from auriferous silver, but
with higher silver contents of 76e90% and heterogeneous tin and
lead concentrations. In five samples (MA-092126, MA-092140, MA100811e13), bismuth is positively correlated with silver, which at
least for these samples could be regarded as the source of the
higher Bi concentrations in this group. The gilded silver object MA090063 containing about 800 mg kg <sup>1</sup> Bi provides a good example.
The placer gold used for alloying, with its high PGE concentrations
(Table 4) and different ratio of Pd to Pt, must have come from
a deposit with geological background different to group I gold and
electrum.

Of the AgeAue(Cu)eSn alloy samples, three are closely related
to the group II placer gold source and only differ in the tin content,
if considered of natural origin, while two AueAgeSn alloy samples
are different, also from each other, in Pd/Pt ratio, Ptnorm and Bi
(Table 4). However, as in their case only one ablation was possible
on the gilding layer on the cross sections, the values have to be
considered with care and require further validation from new
samples.

Regarding style, elaboration and object type, the AgeAue(Cu)e
Sn alloy samples form a group with other group II objects, consisting of two thick spiral ring fragments, a well shaped thick open
ring, one whole and four fragmented leech-shaped earrings
(Fig. 13), to which another whole leech-shaped earring made of
unalloyed silver corresponds. Their shapes are very uniform and
well-balanced, in contrast to another leech-shaped earring
belonging to subgroup Ic: It is one of three not fragmented earrings
of this type, all found in burial 4, but resembles the workmanship of
the Ib-Ic objects and could be interpreted as a copy.

There is a second object suggesting the idea of imitation of
group II objects in the Ib-Ic workshop: One of a pair of biconical
earrings found in burial 3 (Fig. 2) differs from the other in
composition and trace elements, as well as in colour (depletion
gilding), diameter, manufacturing and elaboration, and obviously
was not made in the same workshop. While the more elaborate
silver coloured earring belongs to group II, the gilded one belongs
to a group of four objects with element patterns somewhat intermediate between group I and II, although partly closer to group I.
Typologically and technologically, three of them (Fig. 13) correspond to the artefacts of subgroups Ib and Ic, indicating that also
the gilded biconical earring was produced in this workshop. The
intermediate position of these four objects in Fig. 11 provokes the
idea that they could be the result of mixing Ib electron with the
auriferous silver of group II, perhaps indicating recycling.

The unalloyed silver objects are all related to group II and the
AgeAue(Cu)eSn alloys, either by the object type (leech-shaped



earrings) or the foil-fusion gilding. The low lead and bismuth, as
well as the high gold concentrations probably indicate that the four
objects are made from silver which was not extracted from
argentiferous lead ores by cupellation, which usually results in lead
concentrations of a few per mil to per cent in the silver (Meyers,
1999). But it cannot be ruled out that lead was removed to
a greater extent due to repeated processing, especially considering
MA-090063 and MA-092129, making it unlikely to determine the
origin of the silver. As a consequence, the interpretation of the
other trace elements is not clear. However, the (not gilded) leechshaped earring MA-092135 and the gilded spiral ring MA-090060
are very similar in composition and trace elements, while the
other two differ in the concentrations of Au, Pd, Sn and Bi.

Of all gold and silver finds, the finger ring with horseman
depiction (MA-093158) does not only stand out for its style and
type, but also for its trace elements (Table 4). With its high gold
content, intermediate Ptnorm concentration, low Bi concentration
and low Pd/Pt ratio, it is clearly different from both groups and
represents another alluvial gold source. The other two analysed
finger rings (Fig. 14) belong to the Ib electrum subgroup and to
group II, and differ much in type, style, manufacturing and
elaboration.

Although not all objects have been analysed so far and many of
the excavated burials were partly disturbed, the analysed objects
deriving from 24 burials provide first indications regarding the
relation of metal groups and contexts. The number of precious
metal artefacts in these graves ranged from one to six, the majority
containing one or two, while the outstanding “burial of the fine old
lady” (burial 4) with a complete bronze drum included 13 gold and
silver ornaments. Most of the objects of burial 4 belong to group II,
and also in the other rich burials with 5 and 6 pieces, group II is
represented. Vice versa, in the burials with fewer gold-silver
objects, group II is missing, not regarding a leech-shaped earring
fragment (MA-092126) with unsure affiliation to burial 24.
Apparently, group II is related exactly to the elite burials with nonlocal features like bronze drums, stone pestles etc. Electrum and
auriferous silver objects of subgroup Ib and Ic are distributed
equally in all graves, while the Ia gold ornaments appear only in
those with five to two ornaments, being absent not only in the
burials with only one piece, but also in the richest.


4. Conclusions


A selection of 57 gold and silver objects found during the rescue
excavations at Prohear and two from the nearby looted cemetery of
Bit Meas could be characterised successfully in terms of composition and trace elements by LA-ICP-MS and give a completely new
impression of the precious metal working in Southeast Asia during
the last century BC and the first century AD. Two main groups
representing different alluvial gold/electrum placers bearing platinum minerals and cassiterite could be established based on the
concentrations of Pt, Ir, Bi and especially the Pd/Pt ratio. The
majority of the ornaments belong to group I and most likely come
from two workshops, one specialising in certain types of gold
objects as ribbed earrings for example, and one producing mainly
spiral rings from electrum and auriferous silver. Group II is closely
related to the rich burials with non-local features, representing
different object types, e.g. leech-shaped earrings, deriving from
a technically more advanced workshop.

SEM-EDX analyses revealed two different gilding techniques so
far unknown from Southeast Asia, foil-fusion gilding of silver
objects and depletion gilding. The first is related to group II and to
special AgeAue(Cu)eSn alloys as gilding foil, which contain up to
4% tin of perhaps natural origin. Depletion gilding was observed on
one object and is suspected for many of the group I ornaments

<!-- Page 11 -->
S. Schlosser et al. / Journal of Archaeological Science 39 (2012) 2877e2887 2887



made from auriferous silver and electrum. The occurrence of this
technique is currently being examined systematically in the course
of the ongoing investigation of the Prohear finds, which also
continues the analysis of the remaining gold, electrum and silver
finds in order to complete this interesting finding.

The application of LA-ICP-MS to silver samples proved to be
useful and reliable under certain conditions. In order to select the
least corroded inner parts, it is advisable to ablate on polished cross
sections or, in case of direct ablation from the object, it should be
ascertained that enough surface material is removed. Thus, for
silver artefacts, laser ablation cannot be as minimal invasive as for
gold objects.
