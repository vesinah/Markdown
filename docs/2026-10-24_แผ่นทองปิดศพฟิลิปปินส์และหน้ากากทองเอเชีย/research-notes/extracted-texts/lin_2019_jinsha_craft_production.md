# On Craft Production and the Settlement Pattern of the Jinsha Site Cluster on the Chengdu Plain

Kuei-chen LIN


ABSTRACT


Analysis of settlement pattern and production organization of the Jinsha settlement
suggests that it was less strictly organized than the Sanxingdui settlement and that it was
inhabited by heterogeneous social groups during the early Bronze Age. Measuring and
evaluating the standardization of two types of pottery vessels (i.e., jars with small bases

[xiaopingdi guan 小平底罐] and saucers with pointed bottoms [jiandizhan 尖底盞]) reveal
that potters employed different patterns for products to be used in different contexts.
Vessels are more standardized at loci close to the sacrificial zone (where ritual paraphernalia were buried), while vessels used as burial goods tend to be smaller. Pottery
production was loosely organized, ranging between individual and nucleated workshops,
and widely distributed across loci both near the sacrificial zone and on the periphery of
Jinsha. The sacrificial zone at the center of the settlement apparently created needs that
promoted production activities. The presentation of multiple types of production in the
Jinsha core, together with religious activities and public buildings, suggests not only
multiple chaînes opératoires for objects but also communal activities and entangled social
relations. Such gatherings and driving forces enabled different social groups to redress
social tensions while promoting consensus. Crafters might have cooperated when
necessary, but they were otherwise organized in a flexible manner, maintaining at most a
loose network of production. KEYWORDS: Jinsha, craft production, standardization,
urbanization, community archaeology.


BACKGROUND


EVER SINCE SETTLEMENT PATTERNS BECAME A MAJOR TOPIC of New Archaeology in the
1960s, archaeologists working on related topics have examined physical aspects
of settlements, including scale (in terms of population or area), spatial structures,
subsistence economies, and natural environments (Chang 1968; Parsons 1972; Trigger
1967; Willey 1968). In the following decades, scholars have also raised issues concerning social identity, relationships, political order, and organization. These abstract
aspects of settlement patterns relate to the processes of materialization that give life and
meaning to objects, including production activities such as the extraction of natural

Sanxingdui 三星堆 period (early Bronze Age ca. 1700–1150 B.C.) (Chengdu Shi
2004a, 2005b; Flad and Chen 2013:99; Jiang Z. 2010). However, the two archaeological cultures, Shi’erqiao and Sanxingdui, were so alike around 1200 B.C. that
many scholars today believe that Jinsha succeeded Sanxingdui following the latter’s
decay as the region’s political center. <sup>2</sup>

Sites with Sanxingdui Culture features are rarely found beyond the Tuo 沱 river
drainage where the type site is located. Archaeological finds related to Sanxingdui
Culture seem to be concentrated around the Sanxingdui type site, a walled, wellpopulated settlement that formed a nucleated center with some signs of urbanization
(Flad 2018; Sun 2013). By contrast, sites with Baodun or Shi’eriqao cultural features
are scattered throughout the Chengdu Plain, not just nucleated at their type sites.

Two sacrificial pits at Sanxingdui exhibit remarkable achievement in the production tradition (Sichuan 1999). Since many items found at Jinsha contain images or
icons that can be traced back to Sanxingdui, Sanxingdui probably influenced the
production of precious and everyday goods at Jinsha. In addition to ceramics for daily
use, variants of some large pottery vessel types that may have been used in ceremonies
or associated feasts in Sanxingdui have been uncovered in Jinsha. The pointedbottomed saucers (jiandizhan 尖底盞) that first appeared in late Sanxingdui are
particularly common in Shi’erqiao Culture sites and are found in the Jinsha site cluster
in both everyday and burial contexts. This type of pottery vessel was perhaps the
most long-lived and representative object type throughout the Bronze Age of the
Chengdu Plain.

Even if the production and use of similar objects continued from the Sanxingdui to
Shi’erqiao periods, one should not assume that they possessed the same meaning or that
production was organized the same way over time. For instance, Jinsha people neither
burned luxury goods nor buried them in pits in one batch as Sanxingdui people did,
but instead periodically scattered them near Modi River. A transformation in the social
meanings of these goods can therefore be seen (Flad 2012). Though scholars could take
the luxury or valuable goods yielded from Sanxingdui as evidence of a centralized
management system (what might be called “the state”), whether the political form
changed between the Baodun and Shi’erqiao (Jinsha) periods is not yet clear. Even if
the form changed, it did not necessarily follow an evolutionary trajectory. Scholars
have not even agreed on when “the state” form appeared on the ancient Chengdu
Plain. This political category can only be applied after using current material remains
and spatial patterns to understand how social units (whether geographically bounded
households or quasi-consanguineal groups) were organized. Such an approach
encourages us to study the social life of such people via their material culture rather
than simply apply an absolute label to them.

The concepts of “state” and “city” have sometimes been intermingled (Cowgill
2004; Renfrew 2008; Stoddart 2014). The signs used to the identify the appearance of
a state, such as monumental architecture, specialized crafters, a writing system, artistic
expression, foreign trade, and administrative bureaucracy or ruling classes, have also
been used to define urban cities (Childe 1950; cf. Jennings 2016; Jennings and Earle
2016; Wengrow 2010). As the usual foci of polities and locations where political elites
often reside, cities are unavoidably considered centers for the demonstration of
political power. For instance, Kwang-chih Chang (1974) built on the ideas of HsiaoTung Fei (1953), who argued that a city is simultaneously an instrument by which the
ruling class enforces its power and a spatial symbol reflecting the lineage hierarchy and

Here, I focus my discussion of social life on an urban center (Jinsha) to avoid

types and achievements of craft production to the degree of political centralization.
Instead, preliminary tests on pottery production, specifically around the spatial patterns
of kilns and degrees of standardization of vessels, suggest a bottom-up organization
scheme and that social relationships within this society were by no means less complex
than the relational webs constructed in top-down planning societies.


THE SETTLEMENT TYPE: THE URBANNESS OF THE JINSHA SITE CLUSTER


AND ITS EMERGENT COMPLEXITY


Defining a settlement like Jinsha is difficult. No suitable term for this kind of site cluster
seemingly exists. As a settlement center and community, Jinsha (1000–600 B.C.) is
certainly not metropolitan in the modern sense. Following the features enumerated by
Gordon Childe (1950) and scholars such as Charles Redman (1978:216), there is no
evidence of a large population or written records that would unequivocally identify it
as a city. However, urbanness is not only about population or site size and, as Xu Hong
(2000:51) observes, we should not treat all Childe’s conditions for determining urbanness equally. One probably needs to consider them at different levels of abstraction.
The development of Jinsha and its emergent complexity as reflected in craft specialization and ritual activities do fit the definition of urbanization to a certain degree.
Evidence of communal activities also force our discussion away from the archaeology
of households toward the archaeology of communities. Here, “community” not only
denotes a type of social organization, it suggests that particular social phenomena and
activities occur at the supra- or communal household level (Stanish 1989; Ur 2014).
Forms of social organization such as communities are not simply reflected in objective
sites within a space. The space itself contains physical structures and material remains
such as residential buildings, ditches, drainages, or monumental architecture (the real
sizes of which can be estimated), as well as abstract organization and activities that
cumulatively shape social phenomena such as the orientation of architectural features
to the cosmos and the segregation of the living from the dead. Because the ways
communal spaces are shaped involve both physical and social phenomena, a community constitutes a suitable scale for assessing daily practices, social interactions, and
decision-making mechanisms (Birch 2013).

The evidence of development of such a settlement cluster might resemble that of
cities. To understand how cities emerge, scholars consider external factors along with
internal social, economic, political, and religious conditions and the interactions
among external and internal features (Cowgill 2004; Flad 2018; Liu 2006). Such
diverse approaches reveal that a variety of stimuli lead to the creation of urban centers
and that their emergence follow various trajectories. Comparing processes of urban
formation in other regions with the development of the settlements on the Chengdu
Plain suggests that they shared some attributes, but that the latter were unique in other
respects. Before addressing cases outside of this region, one must first look at the

developmental history of the settlement patterns on the Chengdu Plain from which
Jinsha took its shape.

Unlike its counterparts in the Yellow River valleys, particularly Zhengzhou and
Anyang (Jing et al. 2013; Liu 2006; Liu and Chen 2003), the sociopolitical processes in
southwestern China since the late Neolithic period have only recently attracted attention. This attention is partly thanks to increasingly comprehensive regional surveys and
the discovery of eight walled sites in the area. The type site of Baodun Culture has
nested double walls surrounding the largest (276 ha) of all walled enclosures in the
Chengdu Plain and indeed in southwestern China. Given its size and the labor required
to produce its rammed-earth walls, ditches, and large buildings, this settlement may
have attracted the largest population in the region, which might otherwise have been
only sparsely inhabited (Chengdu et al. 2000; He 2015). Despite the difficulty of
estimating the population of ancient settlements in the absence of textual records, some
attempts have been made. For example, Rowan Flad and Pochan Chen (2013:84) tried
to assess the labor required to construct the inner walls of Baodun, which totaled an
area of 60 ha. They suggest that around 4000 people would have had to work
concertedly for a month to construct these walls. Other scholars have estimated that
more than 250,000 m <sup>3</sup> of earth would have been required to build the inner walls
(Jiang Z. et al. 2000:106). Organizing the labor needed to move and shape this much
earth would in turn have demanded certain decision-making mechanisms, whether
they were permanent, temporary, or ad hoc. Other settlements emerged in the region
concurrently or slightly later than the Baodun site. For example, a large building
foundation (ca. 550 m <sup>2</sup> ) without any partitions was found at Pixian 郫縣 (Chengdu
and Pixian 2001). Buildings like this were perhaps not simple common residences but
had special uses and meanings.

Several theories have been proposed to explain the emergence of the numerous
walled sites in the region. Some scholars argue that they coincided with the evolutionary development of “chiefdoms” (guguo 古國) (Duan 1999a, 1999b). Others, such
as Pochan Chen (2004), mention the possibility of applying Colin Renfrew’s (1982,
1986) concept of “peer polities” to the walled enclosures of the Chengdu Plain. Colin
Renfrew noted that early cities or city-states might each have had a population of only
several thousand residents. The “peer polities” concept suggests a possible relationship
among walled settlements and explains why even low populations might have
demanded the construction of so many walled enclosures.

Occupation became more nucleated as the area transitioned to the Sanxingdui
period. The Sanxingdui type site had walls and expanded to 500 ha (including only the
walled enclosure) or about 1200 ha (if neighborhoods with related remains are
included). Sanxingdui seems to have been the only settlement center to remain after
the other walled settlements declined at the end of the Neolithic era. Its walled
enclosure contained a number of components, also divided by earth walls. Some
divisions are thought to demarcate workshop and palace zones. The discovery of two
sacrificial pits full of precious goods was astounding. A large building with raised floors
was unearthed in the Qingguanshan 青關山 locus near the north wall, suggesting that
this was a labor-intensive construction, though its precise nature requires further
clarification.

Although the settlement compound containing Qingguanshan is still under
excavation and many details are undisclosed, the overall settlement distribution pattern
of Sanxingdui Culture can be distinguished from the earlier Baodun Culture and later

Shi’erqiao Culture. Beyond this super-settlement, only a few contemporaneous
Sanxingdui sites have been found on the Chengdu Plain. They are all smaller in size,
suggesting that most of the population remained concentrated on the Sanxingdui site.
Apparently, settlements in this region became increasingly integrated following the
rank-size rule (Johnson 1980) as they progressed from the Baodun to the Sanxingdui
period. Based on level of public construction and the many prestige goods found in
the sacrificial pits, many scholars believe that Sanxingdui society was highly stratified
(Sun 2013; Zhao 2005). The production or import of these valued goods indeed hint at
the inhabitants’ ability to exploit natural resources and organize highly technical craft
production and long-distance exchange. Nonetheless, no further evidence of social
hierarchy (e.g., differentiation in burials or residential structures) has been found.

The number of sites drastically increased on the Chengdu Plain during the
Shi’erqiao period. Recent surveys and discoveries found during modern construction
projects have uncovered 64 pre-Qin sites, of which 79.6 percent are dated to the
Shi’erqiao period. Many of these Shi’erqiao sites were only small villages or hamlets,
however (Chengdu Pingyuan 2010; Flad et al. 2013; Jiang Z. 2015). These lessnucleated populations suggest that the region became less integrated during this period.
The sociopolitical process undergirding such changes did not seem to follow an
evolutionary route. Unlike the Sanxingdui type site, which integrated all functions, no
single Shi’erqiao settlement center dominated the region. It is possible that dual or
even multiple centers coexisted at some point in time; perhaps one was more politically
oriented and the others had more ideological or economic functions. Such differentiation of functionality is only conjectural, however, and how these centers complemented or competed with each other remains unclear.

In the long term, population growth became more obvious and gradually moved to
the city center from the foothills, according to material remains and the size of Jinsha
proper. Its growing size also led to the appearance and increase of specialized activity
areas and signs of urbanization, particularly diverse craft production (discussed below).
The segregation and aggregation of space, such as the separation of residential areas and
cemeteries, also grew clearer in Jinsha. Another contrast to Baodun and Sanxingdui
settlements is that Jinsha was a “city” not surrounded by walls. In this way it was like
Yinxu in the Yellow River valley, among other examples (Xu 2013). These un-walled
cities remind us not to assume that being enclosed in walls is a criterion for identifying
cities (von Falkenhausen 2008). However, a lack of walls or moats makes it difficult to
delineate the scope of a central settlement as well as its differentiation from and
relationships to more rural areas nearby.

This brief overview and comparison of settlement centers on the ancient Chengdu
Plain suggest that settlement patterns were neither static nor followed any developmental route. This is a salutary reminder to maintain an open view on how to define a
city. As Renfrew (2008) observed, cities existed in diverse types with differing levels
of complexity. Studying a populated place such as the Jinsha settlement from the
community perspective fills in the gap between region-level and household-level
archaeology. I refer to Jinsha as both a “community” and an “urban center” in an
attempt to bypass earlier discussions about the functionalities of individual site
components (Chengdu Shi 2003a, 2004d, 2006d). Instead, I consider the settlement
cluster to be an organic integration of different social parts.

Ann Brysbaert (2014:46) has suggested that many categories might have occurred
“in-between” the level of domestic production and permanent craft workshops. Based
on my inspection of multiple types of craft production in Jinsha, I similarly provide
alternative formulations of production organization.


PRODUCTION AS A MEANS TO SOCIAL COMPLEXITY


In Childe’s (1950) definition of urbanization, he pointed to specialized spaces and
activities that increased the complexity of the attendant societies. Specialized activities
including craft production is interconnected with the development of social complexity. Specialized crafters and bureaucrats alike relied on food surplus to do their
work. Food production per se may also involve specialized activities. Whether topdown or bottom-up, there can be more than one way to organize these activities in
correspondence with different situations and technologies. These in turn produce
divergent social meanings. For instance, the sacrificial zone at Meiyuan was apparently
used for worshipping supernatural powers through the repeated burial of a variety of
valued goods at the riverbank. Judging from the deposition of the objects, craft
products had a use that differed from the contexts of Sanxingdui; devotional activities
were also organized in a seemingly less strict way (Flad 2012). In this sense, although
the Jinsha sacrificial zone has yielded ritual implements comparable to those found in
Sanxingdui, suggesting close, successive relationships between these two settlement
centers, the ceremonies that created these deposits in Jinsha would have differed
from the ritual events that produced Sanxingdui sacrificial pits. As a result, we can
surmise that those events and objects must have acquired distinct social meanings. I
return to this issue below.

Unlike other valued goods, pottery comprised the majority of craft products and
were employed both in everyday and ritual activities, which produced differing social
values. The end products that have been uncovered nearly everywhere in the area,
along with the devices and tools that produced them, give us a glimpse into production
processes and their spatial structures. More than 200 pottery kilns, some of which
contained semi-finished products, have been recovered from successive excavations
(Chengdu Shi 2003a, 2003b, 2004a, 2004b, 2004c, 2004d, 2005a, 2005b, 2005c, 2006a,
2006b, 2006c; Chengdu and Beijing 2002; Chengdu and Xindu 2010). Temporary
bonfire-style kilns that would have left little archaeological evidence might also have
existed. Identifiable remains including fireplaces, waste and refuse, and finished or

Pointed-bottomed Saucers [jiandizhan 尖底盞]


During earlier research, I noticed that the jiandizhan type ceramic was widely
distributed across the entire Sichuan Basin, middle and southern Shaanxi, and western

Sanxingdui to the Warring States period. Like xiaopingdi guan, these pointed-bottomed

included two sets of jiandizhan (n = 12 and n = 10) along with a few vessel stands

Production Patterns and their Implications


Both types of vessels were so plentiful that I suspect they were once mass-produced and
standardized. To test for this proposed production pattern, I compared metric
measurements, texture (coarse or fine), and locus (location within site) of different
vessel batches from different contexts of deposition (i.e., ash pits, trenches, houses,
burials, kilns, excavation pits, or caches). This enabled me to detect notable differences
or patterns among each set of attributes. The contexts of samples drawn from eight loci
are listed in Table 1. Each of these loci possessed between 0 and 44 kilns. Most kilns
seem to have produced their own batches of vessels.

After measuring the rim diameter, base diameter, width, height, shoulder height,
and maximum perimeter of each vessel in centimeters, I noticed several interesting
trends. Having applied one-way ANOVA to compare the mean values of these
measurements, the results show that, of all the attributes for xiaoping guan, vessel height
seems to have varied relative to locus and texture, but not to context (e.g., ash pits,
houses, burials, kilns, and caches). Among these loci, xiaopingdi guan from Lanyuan
蘭苑 tend to be squatter and thicker, making them significantly different from the rest.
Furthermore, fine-textured xiaopingdi guan tend to be taller, more slender, and more
standardized (CV = 6.7%) than coarser wares, revealing that potters paid more
attention to the production of fine pottery. Not only was their clay better prepared, but
these jars were more carefully shaped than the coarser ones. Fine and coarse wares may
have had different uses. It is also worth noting that xiaopingdi guan found in cemeteries
tend to be smaller and thicker than in other contexts, but this tendency is not
statistically significant.

As for jiandizhan, those found at Lanyuan are generally more standardized than those
found at other loci. For instance, the CV values for rim diameter, maximum perimeter,
and height of jiandizhan at Lanyuan are 6.8%, 4.2%, and 8.9%, respectively. Given the

Potters intentionally or unintentionally regulate culturally significant dimensions
through talent, skill, or bodily memory encoded in repetitive practice and physical
gesture (Brysbaert 2014; Dietler and Herbich 1998:246; Dobres 2010; Herzfeld 2004).
The measurements for the jiandizhan suggest that not every dimension was culturally
meaningful, however. Some dimensions such as base diameter and thickness vary
greatly in size and so seem to have been produced with little care, while other dimensions demonstrate clearer patterns.

Comparison of the two types of vessels shows that xiaopingdi guan were generally less
standardized than jiandizhan in loci that produced both types. This is probably because
the category xiaopingdi guan actually lumps together two or more vessel types,
including those called pointed-bottomed cups (jiandibei 尖底杯). Jiandibei look similar
to xiaopingdi guan but differ in texture and sometimes in neck length and total height.
Most importantly, jiandibei were popular in and probably originated from the eastern
part of the Sichuan Basin and were later brought to the Chengdu Plain, possibly
through human migration (Jiang Z. 2007:400; Sun 2000:14). Coming from either end
of the Sichuan Basin, these two similar types of vessel met on the Chengdu Plain,
probably signaling the interplay of two populations. Though the material cultures of
the Chengdu Plain were disseminated widely to other places, the people who lived
here were also influenced by other groups, including their immediate neighbors and
people from distant regions. An influence from the east can be seen in the interplay of
xiaopingdi guan and jiandibei, for instance. That these similar types are often confused
reminds us of the importance of the cultural contexts in which such things were shaped
and used. The use and texture of these two kinds of objects are different enough that
they seem to have been independently invented and produced by different archaeological cultures. To lump them together, artificially producing a single type
category, is thus a mistake.

Jiandizhan, on the other hand, is a more bounded type and can be considered characteristic of Shi’erqiao Culture. The distributional scope of this vessel type signifies
Shi’erqiao’s cultural sphere. Throughout the periods in which this vessel was prevalent,
jiandizhan were produced at various scales and used in diverse contexts and acquired
multiple and variable meanings and values. The initial appearance of jiandizhan, which
exhibit a high degree of standardization, in the Sanxingdui sacrificial pits implies their
distinguished role as ritual paraphernalia. Ritual jiandizhan could be used individually
or in sets that came with vessel covers and stands. Note that the vessel covers were
shallower pointed-bottomed saucers. The smaller batch of jiandizhan found in the
Sanxingdui pits possibly served as vessel covers.

Such ritual vessels should not be confused with their counterparts made for
daily use. Their ordinary use perhaps as dining utensils singles out the culture and
idiosyncrasies of the people of Shi’erqiao. Like their xiaopingdi guan predecessors,
jiandizhan created for daily use often became the lone grave goods in burials that
otherwise bore no special burial treatment. Although they were sometimes made of
inexpensive materials, their ubiquity suggests that these vessels were among the most
important goods accompanying the dead.


Kilns and Craft Networks


Available archaeological data allows us to deduce that many loci at Jinsha contained
kilns. Nearly half of the Jinsha loci currently known contain more than one pottery

<!-- Page 14 -->

kiln. Many are associated with graveyards, but they also accompany residential
structures and public buildings. Using such kilns, crafters would have been able to form
workshops of various scales. Near two exceptionally large graveyards, two groups of
simple kilns (n = 41 and n = 44) were found within about a 1.5 ha area (Zhou Zhiqing
and Wang Zhankui, pers. comm. 18 December 2015). Such cemeteries, with the
burials oriented in the same direction, were well arranged and planned in tandem with
nearby residential units. The spatial divisions of the cemeteries related to how the
society was divided into subgroups, with different populations being buried in different
graveyards. However, neither the housing structures nor the graves suggest any
differentiation in social status, as some scholars might expect of a society that had
metallurgy. The lack of evidence for status distinction in this society suggests we should
consider possibilities for production organization other than top-down planning.

A collection of so many small kilns in a single area is a significant sign not only of
their productivity in serving a community of the deceased, but also of how pottery
production might have been organized from the bottom up by the living. We should
not forget that many small-scale pottery kilns were scattered around houses and graves
beyond the nucleated production loci. These scattered kilns, whose products were
offered to small residential units or graveyards, perhaps constituted the major production modes at the site.

Though scattered, I suspect that the crafters at these individual workshops maintained a loose production network. Crafters working individually were in close
enough proximity to observe each other’s work and exchange ideas, knowledge, and
information through daily communications. This would have enabled them to achieve
a certain consensus around fashioning the vessels, which ultimately manifested in the
production of culturally salient items such as xiaopingdi guan and jiandizhan. The wares
produced at the Jinsha settlement thus constitute a model of production transmitted
through webs of interaction and human mobility. Vessel variants in part signal
chronological transitions, but they are also signs of distribution that suggest the scope of
cultural spheres. Crafters might have cooperated when necessary, but they otherwise
served individual residential groups in a self-sufficient manner. In other words,
production was organized in a flexible way and its scale was possibly between that of
individual and nucleated workshops. This could reflect what Anne Underhill (1991)
has called a “complex household industry.” Other scholars have posited that a
“communal mode of production,” wherein production labor is organized above the
level of the nucleated family, may develop based on (fictive) lineages or shared land
(Goldstein 2000). Neither of these production models were fixed types nor were they
controlled by an imposed, institutional power. In addition, it is very likely that multiple
modes of production coexisted within any given society (Underhill 1991).


Multicraft Production at Jinsha


Production of different materials, besides pottery, also existed. They might have been
complementary, or competed with or imitated each other; they might also have
demanded the same natural resources, shared similar production processes, fulfilled
similar purposes or needs, or served as up- and down-stream producers. For instance,
extraction and reduction technologies might have been amalgamated while pyrotechnology and chemical processes or transformative technologies could have
coexisted (Li 2007; Miller 2007:37; Shimada 2007; Stone 2013).

<!-- Page 15 -->
380 ASIAN PERSPECTIVES - 2019 - 58(2)


Many loci at Jinsha, particularly Meiyuan, show evidence of multicraft
production besides pottery, including bone, horn, and stone tools and jade and
other stone ornaments. Bone and horn tools often appear alongside modified animal
bones left by butchery, with the latter being the byproducts of food production and
serving as raw materials for the former. Bone tools have often been found in association
with stone rings. Both kinds of craftwork relied on cutting and drilling techniques.
Stone and bone objects were manufactured using both stone and bone tools; they
could also have been worked with metal tools. Small, simple metal tools such as awls
are commonly found near households with ceramic, bone, and stone products. All
these daily goods and tools served households. Their concurrence would have caused
the multiple chaînes opératoires or life histories of the objects to become interlaced
(Dobres 2000:164; Edmonds 1990:56, 67; Leroi-Gourhan 1993; Read 2007:
188; Sellet 1993) and further promoted “cross-craft interactions” (Brysbaert 2014;
McGovern 1989).

Another example of up- and downstream relations is presented by the ritual bronzes
that have been found at Jinsha, although no evidence (i.e., ceramic or stone molds or
models) of loci responsible for producing the bronzes have been discovered there.
Such coexisting craft production involves “networks of knowledge” in which knowledge was shared and transmitted among crafters working together (Dobres 2014;
Rebay-Salisbury et al. 2014). As Marcia-Anne Dobres (2014) notes, several situations
need to be considered in the study of such cross-craft interactions, including the use of
different raw materials to produce the same products, the applications of different tools
or craft traditions, and the practices of different and similar chaînes opératoires to produce
different types of objects. Rebay-Salisbury, Brysbaert, and Foxhall (2014) have pointed
out that artifacts are repositories of knowledge. They are also repositories of social
relationships when produced in an interactive sphere and subject to transmission from
one hand to another. The juxtaposition of multiple kinds of materials in one locus
(e.g., Meiyuan) further reveals how different production systems might be
incorporated and social relationships interwoven.

Combining different materials to form a single artifact demonstrates even closer
cooperative relationships among crafters. For example, bronze plaques inlaid with
turquoise (including those in a tiger shape) have been discovered on the Chengdu
Plain. They were first found at Sanxingdui and then at Jinsha. Such inlay was
influenced by the Central Plains, particularly the Erlitou site, and perhaps also
indirectly by the Qijia or earlier cultures (Fitzgerald-Huber 1995; Huang 2015; Qin
2016). The partnership between bronze and turquoise crafters in these cases must have
been close. Another example is a piece of painted wood furniture found at Meiyuan
that was inlaid with pieces of jade or other stones and combined at least three types of
material (Chengdu and Chengdu 2018:316–317, No. L58①:2). Wood, which is
perishable, often served as a base material to which metals and jades were attached.
Attaching these expensive materials required increasingly complex local crafts skills and
enhanced the value of the products. It is also possible that local products were
decorated with imported precious goods to enhance their value. Objects that brought
together domestic and imported materials therefore constituted a new mode of
production. As W. Brian Arthur (2009) observes, new technologies always come from
new ways of combining earlier technologies. A technology can be partitioned into
multiple parts or stages that are also technologies in themselves and are therefore
recursively self-contained (Arthur 2009:33). He further argues that if these smaller

<!-- Page 16 -->

parts or stages are heavily used, they become standardized modules, which are then
potentially subject to recombination with different components or other modules.
There are many possible, flexible ways to combine the different parts. The coexistence
of standardization with fluidity is an important source of technological innovation.

There seems to have been no constraints on the raw materials or the production
knowledge and skills used at Jinsha, at least in the cases of pottery and stoneware
production. Since such complex craftsworks might have exceeded the skill levels of
part-time crafters, who would have been required to work on more than one type of
production activity or undertake other subsistence endeavors to sustain their daily
lives, specialists who were fully engaged in a single form of craft production and made a
livelihood from their work might have existed. No evidence supports the inference
that such specialists were attached to elite classes at Jinsha, however. Furthermore,
theinterplay betweendomestic and long-distance materials was complicatedand flexible
and involved different levels of organization. Relying on the existence of a single
powerful entity to explain the management of resource exploitation and exchange
might be convenient, but would neglect details about the transmission of production
knowledge or the possibility that production depended upon the social networks of
common people. Moreover, the symbolic meanings of things and how they are replaced
with altered meanings may be beyond the control of top-down governance.

Because jade and stone objects make up most of the deposits at Meiyuan, we know
that stoneworking specialists in Jinsha had developed a variety of crafting methods,
including grinding, drilling, cutting, polishing and burnishing, carving, hollowing, and
coloring to work these materials, perhaps more skillfully than Sanxingdui crafters.
Archaeologists have found large quantities of raw materials and semi-finished products
in both Meiyuan and other residential areas. It is very likely that some, if not all, were
locally made and their circulation was not constrained. The materials discovered also
suggest that Jinsha crafters exploited wider resources for stoneworking. Jinsha people
seemed especially fascinated with jade or other stone cong 琮 cuboids. <sup>6</sup> Aside from the
Liangzhu 良渚 Culture (ca. 3300–2000 B.C.) sites, the largest number of such artifacts
have been found at Jinsha: a total of 27, not including many semi-finished products
(Chengdu Shi 2006d). The cong made at Jinsha contrast sharply with those made at
Liangzhu located more than 1000 km away. Jinsha cong were often made from low
quality jade or stone and were mostly undecorated. Jinsha crafters probably knew
about the more delicate Liangzhu import, however. A ten-tiered jade cong cuboid
found in Jinsha is believed to have come from Liangzhu even though it dates to about
1000 years earlier than Jinsha. It is not clear when or how it was transmitted to the
people of Jinsha, though it was possibly transported by several intermediaries (von
Falkenhausen 2003). Locally manufactured cong were probably imitations of the
Liangzhu artifact, but were executed in much simpler designs. Since it was not until
Jinsha that this kind of artifact became interesting on the Chengdu Plain, Jinsha people
apparently had their own interests and needs that differed from the people of Liangzhu
in producing such objects. One should also not assume that the people of either
Liangzhu or Jinsha held the same cosmology or ideology as the Zhou people who used
similar objects (as recorded in the undated Zhouli 周禮 [1981]).

Not only were objects imitated in the same category of material, they were
sometimes made of different materials to meet different needs. The use of jade and
stone increased both in volume and variety over time in Jinsha. Some replaced the
frequently seen bronze items in Sanxingdui. For example, bronze shapes might

have been imitated in jade to borrow the symbolic meaning of the original or
overcome resource shortages. Resource limitations also led to imitations being smaller
than the original objects from which they were copied. Thus, some bronze items from
Jinsha are reminiscent of those from Sanxingdui, but are often smaller. This is exemplified by a small figurine with a whirling crown (Chengdu and Chengdu 2018:78–79,
No. C:2); the Jinsha version is only 20 cm high, while a similar bronze figure is lifesized at Sanxingdui (Sichuan 1999:544, No. K2②:149–150). Finally, bronze vessels
such as those found in Sanxingdui pits are so far absent from Jinsha. The reduced usage
of bronze corresponds with the growing use of jade and might signal either a lack of
metal resources or a transformation in taste. The decline and depopulation of Sanxingdui perhaps resulted in a loss of the exchange networks necessary for acquiring metal
resources or importing objects such that its successor, Jinsha, could not fully replicate
all the products and life styles of Sanxingdui.

Meanwhile, Jinsha people added new materials such as turtle shells to their ritual
paraphernalia. These were used in ceremonies that had not been previously seen in
the Chengdu Plain. The custom of using turtle shells for divination was probably
transmitted from the people around the Middle Yangzi River who had in turn been
influenced by the Central Plains (Flad 2008, 2013). The peoples of the Yangzi River
used both turtle carapaces and plastrons and sometimes even fish gills for divination,
possibly because of the rich fishery resources especially in the Middle Yangzi River
(Flad 2005; Flad and Chen 2013:226–227; Yuan et al. 2008). Diviners from this region
drilled holes in shells to produce cracks in a similar way to the Shang people of the
Central Plains, but left no inscriptions, so we can only speculate about their purposes or
contexts of use. Once again, divination practices might have emerged from differing
cosmologies in different regions. Differences in the exploitation of natural resources
suggest once again that, although the production traditions of the Jinsha site can mostly
be traced back to Sanxingdui, they probably represented different economic modes
with distinct environmental exploitation strategies. Further, their long-distance connections might have shifted over time. Although the extent of social control and
the external connections to other societies remain unknown, we might eventually be
able to deduce them by examining the resources available to this society and the
distribution of some important items.


THE RE/CONSTRUCTION OF IDENTITY THROUGH PRODUCTION AND EXCHANGE


Without written records, it is nearly impossible to know how many social groups
constituted the Jinsha site cluster or their ethnic identities. Nonetheless, material
remains might provide some clues about their shared traditions and customs and their
differences. As many scholars have argued, a community is defined by the social
identity shared by a group of people with common interests and affiliations (Goldstein
2000). At Jinsha, it is likely that a number of nearby residential structures, production
areas, and a cemetery would have jointly constituted a lineage group and formed a
single group identity. Because we have seen combinations of these components at a
number of loci, each combination might point to a different lineage-based social
group. It is possible that the society was composed of segmentary organizations in
which lineage-based social divisions could have been integrated into a larger unit
(Tang and Jing 2009). The inner structure of each component is replicated amongst the
other associated components. For example, a graveyard may articulate a lineage by

<!-- Page 18 -->
LIN   - CRAFT PRODUCTION AND SETTLEMENT PATTERN OF JINSHA 383


replicating the structure and membership of the kinship structure in its layout. Temples
may have filled this function at a later period (Lewis 2006:119). Kin structures would
have influenced the division of labor in craft production and other social activities. As
social structures, lineages and kinship networks may also be fundamental to supporting
city or state societies (von Falkenhausen 2006, 2008).

Communal activities (including rituals) and the exchange and consumption of
commodities provided contexts for interaction that helped to maintain or enhance
consolidation at Jinsha. As these interactions accumulated, the widespread distribution
of several ceramic types, especially pointed-bottomed vessels, provided material
signatures which reinforced the people’s identity. Community identity was thus structured and expressed through commonly shared craft products, particularly ceramics for
daily as well as ritual use. Community-wide styles, therefore, simultaneously promoted
or helped maintain social integration while also creating distinctions amongst groups
(Barlett and McAnany 2000). By using pottery production and its products as a
medium for making social distinctions as well as sustaining common consensus, people
are able to (re)create, express or even deny their social identities (Barlett and McAnany
2000; Brysbaert 2014). This is because cultural identities exist in multiple layers and
can intersect through people’s participation in different activities. However, what
binds different social parts together is largely subjective and might only be revealed
from an emic view.

Since people move in and out of groups over several generations, Jinsha’s social
groups may not have been composed solely of relatives. Over time, they may have
acted more like corporate landholding groups than descent groups. It was probably at
this turning point that Jinsha, originally a society based on lineage, developed into a
society with mixed ethnic groups. New alignments and readjustments of power shaped
the forms of social interactions. Scholars have examined archaeological remains in an
attempt to identify specific ethnic groups in the Chengdu Plain. For instance, based on
the hairstyles and dresses represented on bronze statues uncovered in sacrificial pits,
Sun Hua (2013; Sun and Su 2003:214–235) has proposed that different ethnic groups
could have lived together in Sanxingdui society (Flad 2012). <sup>7</sup> Some scholars have even
traced the formation of Ba and Shu ethnic groups to an earlier time and believe that the
shift from Sanxingdui to Shi’erqiao was related to the westward migration of the Ba
group, who brought their own artifact types (Jiang Z. 2015). After Sanxingdui people
dispersed and moved to Jinsha, Jinsha became the new focal point of interregional
exchange, a site at which both goods and raw materials of different origin were present.
Different social relationships and identities intersected then with each other. In such a
society, the development of social groups with their own interests continued to exist
(Cohen 1969). Variations in the size of different social divisions might have been
considerable, however.

In this scenario, beyond its internal demographic growth, Jinsha developed into a
somewhat “coalescent society” that attracted people of diverse origins, especially after
Sanxingdui’s decline. Stephen Kowalewski (2006) has elaborated on the concept and
meaning of a “coalescent society” to emphasize that it involves a rapid rather than
gradual process of settlement intensification driven by outside forces. Several other
authors have argued that aggregation in a bounded area presents more social costs
than does dispersed living in which villagers enjoy greater privacy and autonomy and
have more direct access to their environmental resources (Kuijt 2000). Forces such as
wars or natural disasters may explain why many people are suddenly compelled

<!-- Page 19 -->
384 ASIAN PERSPECTIVES - 2019 - 58(2)


to concentrate in one place (Kowalewski 2013). We have seen that Jinsha people
displayed a strong affiliation with Sanxingdui’s craft traditions, but employed a more
diverse array of raw materials and artifact styles. Perhaps having experienced social
collapse, some Sanxingdui remnants or refugees migrated to Jinsha, where they
founded a multiethnic community that still included some of the original inhabitants of
the area and perhaps migrants from the east. Regardless of the driving force, the social
units of Jinsha were not homogeneous and probably came from multiple places or
ethnic origins. Each group brought different knowledge and experience to support
broadening exploitation of resources in the area.

Though different social groups were physically spaced apart from one another, the
proximity of some site segments reveals that they may have had close relationships.
Jinsha went through several stages of development. Meiyuan was important from the
beginning because it was the ceremonial focal point of the site. Lanyuan and Sanhe
Huayuan became important because of their proximity to and possible association with
Meiyuan. Lanyuan not only contained a residential area and an extensive graveyard,
but also yielded pottery vessels of increasingly greater standardization, as we saw above.
Sanhe Huayuan, on the other hand, provided public spaces for interactions. The
foundations of several large individual rooms and entire buildings have been
uncovered at this locus; building F9 was about 1000 m <sup>2</sup> and contained five partitions.
Due to their proximity to the sacred Meiyuan locus, these were likely the foundations
of public buildings. The buildings were surrounded by 16 pottery kilns (Chengdu
Shi 2001).

In his study of Greek and Roman cities, Edwin Owens (1991:3) suggests that public
buildings and their location within the urban environment reflect the function of a city
in terms of its religious, political, or economic importance. Likewise, the proximity
between Sanhe Huayuan and Meiyuan might reveal the central role of ceremonies
held in Jinsha. The production loci, including pottery kilns found nearly everywhere
in the open spaces of Sanhe Huayuan, probably served the needs of public gatherings,
which were in turn associated with ritual ceremonies held in Meiyuan. Even though
no city walls or moats have been discovered at Jinsha, constructing such large public
buildings probably required intensive collective labor. Extending north along Modi
River, this compound likely formed the center of the settlement. Together, Sanhe
Huayuan and Meiyuan provided inhabitants with opportunities to gather diverse social
groups together and reveal the emphasis on religious life at Jinsha.


SACRED VERSUS MUNDANE LIFE AT JINSHA


The Modi River (along which Meiyuan was located) cut through the community and
connected the region with outside villages. A major water resource for the area, it may
have flooded from time to time (Sun 2000:393). Scholars have proposed that the rites
held at Meiyuan would have been devoted to river deities (Zhou 2010).

Meiyuan may have been used periodically over the long-term. Since at least the
Sanxingdui period, potters had traditionally produced large serving vessels for
ceremonies and perhaps feasts. While such feasts might have been connected to ritual
occasions, the appearance of large vessels at Meiyuan further suggests the place was
a specialized sacred space. Production at Meiyuan fits the category of prestige technology
rather than practical technology, as proposed by Brian Hayden (1998), since products
were created there not to fulfill basic survival needs but to display social wealth or

<!-- Page 20 -->
LIN   - CRAFT PRODUCTION AND SETTLEMENT PATTERN OF JINSHA 385


power and accomplish other social tasks. As Katherine Spielmann (2002) notes, both
the scarcity of raw materials acquired from far-flung regions and skilled crafting can
enhance the value of artifacts. She also stresses that both ceremonial feasts and valued
goods are critical in ritual performances. Developing prestige technologies to serve
social needs cannot therefore be rationalized within the logic of economic profit.

Periodic rituals may also have regulated phases in craft production and other
subsistence economic activities intended to produce valued goods and surplus for
ceremonial feasts. Spielmann (1998, 2002) suggests that ritual needs may drive both
economic intensification and community specialization and define the nature and scale
of production. She is also right in proposing that, to fulfill the needs of ritual ceremonials, crafters could be intrinsically attached to ritual contexts instead to particular elite
classes (Spielmann 2002:202). Following this logic, craft production practiced around
Meiyuan may have been more standardized and specialized than at other loci not
because of commands from an elite authority, but because of the needs presented by
ritualistic occasions. Jinsha people practiced prestige technologies to meet ritual needs
and used valued goods, ritual performance, and feasts to create room to negotiate social
relations and provide opportunities for the accumulation of social wealth and power.
Because there are no signs of social stratification at Jinsha, some scholars have argued
that it was a religious rather than political center (Jiang Z. 2015). This is probably true.
However, social accumulation might nonetheless have been achieved as a side effect of
the need for prestige products in its religious activities.

Given the discovery of so many valued goods at Meiyuan, it seems to have been
the focal point of Jinsha. However, signs of social stratification, which would have
conveniently explained the formation of Meiyuan, the acquisition of long-distance
resources, and the use of public spaces in Sanhe Huayuan, remain obscure. Archaeologists have not discovered or identified differential treatments of burials or residential
structures nor found defensive structures such as city walls or moats at Jinsha. Moreover, scholars have failed to find a corresponding secular center to complete the social
structure at Jinsha. Questions thus remain regarding whether any permanent political
power existed there or whether a ruling class lived elsewhere beyond the Jinsha site
cluster. However, scholars drawing on political economy theory note that statusseekers sometimes attempt to earn social prestige by using ritual ceremonials or valued
goods (Brumfiel and Earle 1987; Hirth 1996). In such a situation, high-status
individuals may have acted as if they controlled everything and worked toward their
own goals or plans.

While this is one way to interpret the Jinsha social structure, the existence of
powerful elites would hardly explain the fluidity, scale, and various forms of production organization at Jinsha. The organization of social activities can develop in diverse
ways, including self-organization through the continuing accumulation of social
interactions that have no obvious goal. In addition, collective activities like rituals,
which create opportunities for interactions, often perform multiple social functions.
Thus, just as we see in today’s societies, centers with various functions (i.e., political,
commercial, transport, or religious) and differing levels of importance could have been
located in multiple locations. For instance, preserved wooden structures of longhouses
have been found at the type site at Shi’erqiao, five kilometers away from the Jinsha
site cluster (Sichuan and Chengdu 2009). These buildings could be candidates for
civic architecture, where collective decision-making would have taken place. Oracle
bones uncovered from the site also suggest the existence of divinatory activities

<!-- Page 21 -->
386 ASIAN PERSPECTIVES - 2019 - 58(2)


associated with decision-making processes. Divination would have required
specialized knowledge, in which maybe only a few people were proficient. However,
no further evidence suggests how this knowledge was related to the political or
economic life of the people of Shi’erqiao at either site. Indeed, we know little about
how social differentiation would have been interlaced with ritual ceremonials or which
religious or mundane political powers dominated their social life.

It is unreasonable to assume that religious life at Jinsha can be clearly separated from
mundane life or expect to draw a line between the ritual/ideological and economic
realms. It is not necessary to consider the functions of centers in distinctly economic or
non-economic terms. Since Shi’erqiao society was likely divided socially according to
lineage groups, it is possible that those living at the Shi’erqiao type site represented a
specific lineage but maintained social relationships with Jinsha residents. Ritual
occasions provided context for the aggregation of these different social groups. Such
ritual ceremonials and architecture including big houses might be viewed either as
indicative of the accumulation of power and social wealth or simply as evidence of
large public gatherings. In either case, there could have been tension and competition
between different social groups, since competition is a main driving force for both
surplus production and the specialization of ritual goods (Spielmann 2002). Ceremonials also provide a locus for the renegotiation of social relations. Observing that
religious behaviors can be an important factor in economies, Renfrew (2001) has
argued that scholars should not ignore the secular aspects of sacred sites. Meiyuan
provides one example of a locus where the ritual met the mundane. It suggests that
Jinsha society demonstrated collective decision-making and labor activities and
consolidation through its ritual ceremonials. This does not automatically mean that a
strict hierarchical stratigraphy existed or that elites formed a central power to organize
these ritual and economic activities, however.


BETWEEN THE RURAL AND THE CENTER: IF THIS WAS THE CENTER,

WHERE WAS THE RURAL?


The above discussion suggests that the Jinsha site cluster served as one, if not the lone,
settlement center of Shi’erqiao Culture. However, we still know little about its city
plan, if there was one. It was an urban center without any clear physical boundaries like
the city walls or moats found at the Baodun type site and Sanxingdui. Thus, we do not
know the scope of the Jinsha site cluster or its external connections to other regions.
Such lack of urban demarcation can result in controversy or confusion over the
identification of center-rural relationships. Some theorists believe that residents of
urban settlements always differ significantly in terms of roles, practices, and identities
from those who reside in rural settlements (Cowgill 2004). Others suggest there may
be a lifestyle continuum, especially when there are no clear boundaries between a city
and its hinterlands (Yusuf 1974). The difficulty of separating the urban from the rural is
clearly demonstrated in the settlement pattern studies of Mesoamerica, where several
settlement levels have been discovered in both the archaeological record and ethnohistorical documentation (Chase and Chase 2017; Chase et al. 2014; Hare 2000).
Although some scholars still maintain that city centers are places without agricultural
production and that they were mainly occupied by ceremonial and administrative elites
who obtained food fromthe hinterlands(Cowgill 2004; Liu 2006),suchemphasesonthe
roles of cities have specific historical contexts. Just as is the case today, rural spaces in the

<!-- Page 22 -->

past did not merely provide food surpluses to managerial elites in cities, they also
mutually exchanged nonmaterial things (cf. Childe 1950; Redman 1978:216).
Furthermore, the city not only provides services, but also creates new needs.

Implicit in this inquiry are the social divisions of labor, including how and where
different types of craft specialization came into being. We must also recognize that the
flows of materials and information tie together different social segments, including
those between any given center and its distant peripheries. Through recurrent
exchange, such relationships do not remain unchanged over time (Flad 2018). As Jason
Yaeger and Marcello A. Canuto (2000) have observed of Mesoamerica, peripheral
villages can be affiliated with different centers at different times and their material
cultures (particularly craft goods) can be transformed accordingly. Given this inseparability and fluidity, many researchers have stressed that we should study regions in their
entirety rather than dismissing small sites (Blanton 1976; Falconer and Savage 1995). It
is therefore better, perhaps even unavoidable, to conceptualize the social complexity of
the Chengdu Plain in holistic terms. The rank-size rule may perhaps be applied to
suggest a degree of integration in the region (Johnson 1980; Stein 1994). Following
this rule, if a population nucleates at a single site, while many other sites remain small,
the region can be considered more integrated.

From this point of view, Sanxingdui probably represented a highly integrated urban
society. We find only an isolated center where wealth was extremely concentrated and
few supporting villages in rural areas. By comparison, Baodun and particularly Jinsha
possessed many satellite villages with varying connections to their centers. Many of
Jinsha’s smaller sites are located along the Modi River, such as those found in modern
Pixian County, Xinfan 新繁 County, and the Gaoxin West District 高新西區 at the
northwestern fringes of Chengdu City (Chengdu Shi 2005d, 2005e, 2005f, 2007a,
2007b, 2008a, 2008b, 2008c, 2010a, 2010b; Deng 1959; Jiang Z. 2015) (Fig. 2). Most of
these sites are composed of farmland, ash pits or middens, and occasionally pottery kilns
similar to the kilns found at the center of the Jinsha site cluster. These production
loci contain xiaopingdi guan and jiandizhan among other ceramic types. Some of the
farmlands have been exploited since the Baodun period. Other villages featuring
Shi’erqiao ceramics are located much farther away in the mountainous areas west of
Chengdu City, including Ya’an 雅安 and Hanyuan 漢源 (Daduhe 2003; Sichuan,
Sichuan, and Ya’an 1990; Sichuan, Ya’an et al. 2008; Zhongguo et al. 2006). These
distant sites might not have been intimately connected to the settlement center in
Chengdu City, but were situated en route to the pathways that later constituted the
southern overland silk route.

During the Shi’erqiao period, archaeological remains showed that people not only
grew crops (mostly rice) in the many smaller agricultural villages in the northwest region
mentioned above, but also practiced this subsistence economy inside the Jinsha urban
center. <sup>8</sup> According to the remains of fauna and flora found in Jinsha and other
contemporaneous sites, hunting (e.g., deer, muntjac), foraging, and perhaps fishing also
played important roles in the local economy. It is still unclear if the residents living in the
Jinsha urban center were self-sufficient, but whether they were or not, subsistence
practices do not seem to have distinguished the settlement center from the surrounding
countryside. What is clear, however, is that people in the center led more elaborate and
materialized religious lives, which would have intensified other economic activities.

Religious activities at Meiyuan might have facilitated the development of strong
social bonds among Shi’erqiao populations that did not necessarily live within a single

bounded space. The social space of such affiliations could have been very extensive
yet remained fluid. Though not as standardized as those found in the Jinsha center
(Lin 2013), the vessel types shared among people in the hinterlands indicate that they
may also have shared a social identity with the settlement center. We also don’t know
the extent to which any cultural idiosyncrasies were preserved in pottery forms. Could
the sites in the mountainous areas that featured Shi’erqiao vessel types have been
outposts of the settlement center? And if so, were these outposts responsible for
exploiting mountain resources and delivering them to the settlement center? Or did
the rural sites occasionally obtain Shi’erqiao ceramics and then imitate them at their
own production loci? If the latter is true, would these rural sites be considered an
integrated part of the Shi’erqiao Culture or not?

Gailey and Patterson (1987) point out that emerging states only possessed uneven
control over surrounding regions, even those inside their own domains. We therefore
need to consider the dynamics of peripheral societies. Perhaps the settlements in the far
mountainous areas of the Chengdu Plain should be considered separately from those
on the fringes of the urban center. Operating independently of the Jinsha center, I
suspect that they were self-sufficient and enjoyed a high level of autonomy. This does
not mean that they departed from the cultural sphere of Shi’erqiao, however. On the
contrary, while adapted to mountainous environments, these sites imitated the
ceramics from the settlement centers on the Chengdu Plain. They produced some
forms more coarsely, however, and did not adopt the entire assemblage of Shi’erqiao as
a package. They selectively received a material culture that was more prevalent and
dominant in the center, but met their production needs with available raw materials
and techniques.


CONCLUSIONS


Archaeological sites around modern Chengdu City and its suburbs have become
known to scholars after decades of regional surveys and city construction projects. It is
now clear that many of the sites feature material remains identified with Shi’erqiao
Culture, suggesting that its influence reached far beyond the Chengdu Plain. This
identification has been derived from the assemblage of Shi’erqiao ceramics, in
particular the existence of the pointed-bottomed vessels known as jiandizhan, which
first appeared in the Sanxingdui period along with xiaopingdi guan, but only became
popular during the Shi’erqiao period. Both vessel types were used as ritual paraphernalia in late Sanxingdui but had additional diverse functions including as daily
household items and grave goods. Practical and prestige technologies converged in the
loci of these two vessel types.

Space is never neutral. Space shapes and is in turn shaped by human activities and
sensory experiences bearing memories, emotions, and personal cognitions. Space thus
plays an active role in the development of a community and spatial segregations suggest
social distinctions and specialized activities. This is seen both in the sacrificial zone at
Meiyuan and the aggregation of production in particular loci. Communal households
co-residing in a locus based on lineage may have operated a few inter-household
pottery kilns. Different social groups produced vessels at varying levels of standardization at these supra-household workshops.

While potters mostly adopted raw materials in situ and produced similar
ceramic petrographic structures, subtle differences in vessel dimensions could not be

<!-- Page 24 -->

recognized without systematic metric measurements. By measuring the vessel types
uncovered from the Jinsha site cluster and related sites, I found that some dimensions
were more patterned and consistent than other dimensions. The maintenance of these
dimensions became especially clear when I compared vessels from different contexts of
use. Both xiaopingdi guan and jiandizhan from cemeteries tend to be smaller than
the same vessel types found in other contexts (though only the latter are statistically significant). It is through such measurements that the distinctiveness of jiandizhan
used as grave goods could be detected.

The spatial distribution of production loci and the standardization of pottery
products suggest that most pottery production relied on daily practices based in a
few households or neighborhoods. Social segments of the Jinsha population were
loosely organized in a dynamic and complex network into which different production
types and subsistence activities were incorporated. In contrast with the precious objects
used at ritual occasions, what made the more utilitarian goods culturally salient is not
immediately clear. However, their patterns and features become evident if we investigate the scattered individual workshops in a holistic manner, along with their chaînes
opératoires and where they interplayed with other categories of production. In this way,
features unrelated to the functions of the vessels were discerned. These features reflect
crafters’ tacit agreements concerning the shape of the vessels. The remarkable achievement in craft production at Jinsha probably signals that the settlement was a regional
center that regularly received diverse raw materials from rural areas and then
disseminated its ceramic types and lifestyles to those areas. Both ceremonies and public
events such as feasts promoted social consolidation among segmentary residential units
and ultimately the formation of a cultural identity.

To argue that the Jinsha settlement was an urban center requires assessing not only
its material remains, but tracing the uses, social meanings, and social relationships
involved in the production of the objects. The ritual paraphernalia discovered in the
Meiyuan sacrificial zone and Meiyuan’s proximity to the large houses and cemetery of
Sanhe Huayuan and Lanyuan further reveal that these loci formed the core of Jinsha.
This settlement core ultimately grew into a regional center after the Sanxingdui period.
The multiple types of craft products found at Meiyuan also suggest that the space was a
locus for the intermingling of domestic production and long-distance exchange. It
provided occasions for heterogeneous inhabitants of Jinsha to negotiate or reconstruct
their social relations and thus became a convergent point of sacred and secular life. The
potters’ participation in these ceremonial occasions also allowed them to develop a
common consensus regarding vessel types.

Some remaining questions regarding urban-rural relationships and the political
forms of Shi’erqiao Culture demand further examination. The large number of burials
at Jinsha also raise questions about population size, nutrition, community sanitation,
and possible social divisions around age and gender. Although human bones in the area
are normally poorly preserved, dental tests might be useful for learning more about
ancient diets and biogenetic distance. This could then assist us in identifying multiple
ethnic groups at the Jinsha site cluster and beyond, including at related sites in
mountainous areas. Our efforts have so far been directed at the settlement center, so we
await future application of a rural approach to examining the center and its hinterlands
in a more balanced manner (Schwartz and Falconer 1994). This would allow us to
understand the complexity of the hinterlands and the active role they played in shaping
the cultures of the Chengdu Plain.
