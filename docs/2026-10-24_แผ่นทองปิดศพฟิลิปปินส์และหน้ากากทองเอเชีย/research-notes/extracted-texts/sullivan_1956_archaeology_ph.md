During the 19th century, a number of travellers, planters and others turned up
ancient pottery and stone implements in various places in the archipelago, but no archaeological exploration **was** carried out until Alfred Marche, a French traveller and explorer,
arrived on Marinduque in the Visayan Islands in April, 1881. In the following three
months he opened a large number of jar- and coffin-burials, the contents of which he took
back with him to France where they were deposited in the TrocadCro Museum in Paris.
In the years 1922--24 Dr Carl Guthe carried out an extensive exploration in the Visayan
Islands. His work was no more systematic than that of Marche, but resulted in the
collection of a huge quantity of material, and in the identification of a large number of
promising sites, chiefly in Cebu, Leyte and Bohol. The greater part of the Guthe
Collection is in the Museum of the University of Michigan at Ann Arbor. In addition
to these two major explorations, a considerable quantity of material has been found
through the years by other collectors-most of them amateurs-among whom the most
prominent were Hester, Wilson, Roth and Worcester. In 1940, Olov Janse carried out
careful excavations of burial sites in Batangas Province, while since 1950 Solheim and
Manuel have investigated jar-burials in the Bondoc Peninsula. For the rest-excepting
the work of Dr Beyer himself-that **so** great quantity has been found and brought to the
Museum in Manila is almost entirely due to chance finds and the treasure-hunting
which inevitably followed in the wake of discovery. It is consequently of more value
for the light it throws on the distribution of pottery and artifacts, and for the promise it
holds out for future excavation, than in establishing a precise chronology for any given
area.

to take up a Research Fellowship at Harvard, but decided to investigate the site before
leaving. He discovered that the excavations had revealed a stratum containing a great

By **1941,** the archaeological collection in the Museum of the University of the
Philippines and the Bureau of Science stood at around a million specimens, of which
nearly half came from the approximately one hundred and twenty sites excavated or
explored by Dr Beyer and others in the Novaliches area alone. The collection suffered
heavily during the Battle of Manila in **1945.** About seventy-five per cent of the larger
ceramic pieces were smashed, the same proportion of the Rizal-Bulacan collection lost,
and a considerable quantity of finds from other areas destroyed. Nevertheless, the
collection now housed in the University Museum is still of formidable size. The writer,
after four weeks of fairly intensive study, had seen rather less than half of it.

In spite of the obscurity that still hangs over many details of the archaeological
picture of the Philippines, the main outlines are now becoming reasonably clear. It is
generally accepted that during the Pleistocene period certain islands of the Philippines
archipelago, including Luzon, were part of a land shelf that extended down in a great arc
through Borneo, Java and Sumatra to what is now the mainland of south-east Asia, and that
consequently there was free overland movement of men and animals throughout that
area. No human remains of the Mid-Pleistocene Period, approximately **_250,000_** to
**_300,000_** years ago, have been found in the Philippines, but in Rizal, Batangas, Bulacan
and Davao Provinces, palaeoliths have been found which bear a close resemblance to
tools of **_Pithecanthropus erectus_** (Trinil Man) found in Java. These stone implements,
together with the fossil remains of the stegodon (an extinct elephant), rhinoceros and other
mammals, were often found in association with tektites, a form of glass meteorite known
to have fallen in showers in the Pleistocene Period. ‘ The presence of natural tektites
in a deposit ’, Dr Beyer remarks ‘ is . . . a chronological guide post, and has frequently
been of great assistance in enabling us to locate both Pleistocene artifacts and fossil
mammalian remains ’. A probable Late Pleistocene stage characterized by stone cleavers,
hand axes and other large palaeoliths, has been discovered in Batangas Province, but
the site has yet to be thoroughly investigated. Other palaeoliths have been picked up
just outside Davao City on the Island of Mindanao, while in sites in Batangas a number

of Neolithic tools have been found which look like reworked palaeoliths, suggesting that
a Neolithic site had been occupied by man in the Pleistocene Period.

As Dr Beyer’s survey of Rizal Province progressed, it became evident that four
successive later Stone Age cultures had succeeded one another there : a Mesolithic,
characterized by flaked obsidian microliths (or semi-microliths) ; an early Neolithic,
comprising both flaked microliths and stone adzes with ground surfaces ; a Middle
Neolithic, characterized by shouldered and ridged axe-adzes **_(FIG._** **3,** no. **3)** ; and a late
Neolithic. The Late Neolithic culture, which Beyer again divides into four phases,
seems to present a picture of ever-increasing richness, evolving from an ‘ Early Nephrite
Culture ’ through a transitional stage to the period of the early ‘ stepped ’ adze **(FIG.** 3,

Regarding the Philippine Neolithic culture in general, it would seem that there must
have been close intercourse with mainland China, for the use of jade, the early forms of
the stepped adze and the shouldered axe are all found in South China and Indo-China
sites. The Philippines, however, present one significant difference from the Neolithic
culture of these areas. For whereas in South China, Indo-China, Formosa and Japan,
the shouldered axe is associated with cord-marked pottery, Dr Beyer takes a position of
extreme caution in maintaining that so far no definite evidence has been found for the
existence of pottery in any Neolithic sites in the Philippines-a fact which, if substantiated, suggests that the archipelago, for all its close association with continental Asia,
**was** in this respect at least more closely connected with the islands of the Pacific, in whose
Neolithic cultures likewise no pottery has yet been discovered.

Another respect in which the archaeological history of the Philippines differs from
that of the adjacent mainland is in regard to the Bronze Age. Not only are bronze
objects very rare, but those that have been found were all associated either with Neolithic
or with Iron Age artifacts. There was, therefore, strictly speaking, no Bronze Age in
the Philippines at all. There are but two groups of bronze artifacts, of which one consists
of a small number of bronze Celts, which show a clear relationship to those of the Dongson
culture of Indo-China (FIG. 3, no. **_5)._** These were all found in Batangas Provincethe exact circumstances have not yet been published-in association with Late Neolithic

plain-backed ’ and ‘ transitional ’ adzes. In the opinion of Dr Beyer, this supports the

view that ‘ either the bronze specimens themselves, or the metal of which they were made,
came over to Luzon from northern Indo-China or the South China coast ’. He dates
these bronze Celts between **1000** and **500** **B.C.** The second group of bronze objects,
unearthed in Site **13,** Lake District, north of Laguna de Bay, central Luzon, consists
chiefly of a series of bronze ear-pendants and dagger-handles, the latter in the form of
modelled heads with elaborate headdresses. In the latter, Dr Beyer sees a kinship to
certain Bronze Age specimens from Indo-China and Formosa (FIG. 3, no. 6). The earpendants on the other hand **(FIG.** 3, no. 7), which in his opinion seem not to be related to

The oldest pottery found in the Philippines belongs to this first phase of the Iron
Age. It is a coarse red ware, unslipped, but generally decorated with incised geometrical
designs, the vessel often standing on a high base pierced with a regular series of triangular
holes **(FIG.** 3, nos. **8** and 9). Of this type of pottery decoration, Beyer states that it is **also**
found in ‘ Celebes, Japan, Korea and southern Manchuria. **Some** archaeologists
believe that it originated in Asia Minor or southern India I. It does indeed bear some
resemblance to Japanese pottery of the Yayoi period (2nd century **B.C.** to 2nd century **A.D.)**
and the fact that it was found in considerable quantities in the Batanes and Babuyan
Islands to the north of Luzon would indicate northern affinities. On the bash of his
finds at Novaliches, Beyer distinguishes two stages in the Iron Age chronology-the
unslipped ware, coming down not later than the 3rd or possibly 4th century **A.D.** ; and the
slipped ware, from the 4th to the 9th century. The reason why the Iron Age appears
to have ended in the 9th century is simply a question of terminology, and will be dealt
with in a moment. Meantime it must be emphasized that this chronological division
into successive stages of unslipped and slipped wares is based solely on Beyer’s excavations of stratified sites at Novaliches, the reports of which have not yet been published.
This stratification is not to be taken as absolute, however : when the writer visited Site A
at Novaliches with Dr Beyer in June 1955, we picked out a number of shards of both
types from a single exposed deposit about eighteen inches thick.

In addition to the great quantity of material unearthed at Novaliches between 1926
and 1930, Iron Age remains have been found in the Lake District (Rizal Province), in
Batangas (traces only), at San Narciso, Tayabas, and on the islands of Masbate, Samar,
Cebu, Mindanao (Cotobato Province) and Sulu. In many of these sites in addition to
pottery, gold, silver, bronze and copper ornaments and glass bracelets have been found.
**A** knowledge of the art of weaving has been inferred from the imprint of coarse cloth left
in the rust encasing certain iron implements found at Novaliches.

The later phase of the Iron Age in the Philippines has been called by Dr Beyer the
‘ Porcelain Age.’ This is a convenient term to indicate that a site, from the presence of
porcelain, belongs to the period following the first introduction **of** glazed wares from the
mainland of Asia in the late T’ang or early Sung period, that is, during the 9th and 10th
centuries. Before we can discuss this phase, however, we should consider an aspect of
Philippine archaeological history which seems to stand somewhat apart from the main
stream, namely what Beyer calls the ‘ Jar Burial Culture ’. If this term referred to all
occasions on which jars were used in Philippine burials it would be so general as to be
meaningless ; for burial in jars has been widespread in the Islands until recent times.

In most cases, however, these appear to be secondary burials, and the jars are Chinese
stoneware vessels of the Ming and early Ch’ing periods. Beyer’s Jar Burial Culture
(or, to distinguish it more clearly still, ‘ Special Iron Age Jar Burial Culture ’) refers

rather to a type of burial in which the body was placed in a very large coarse grey earthenware vessel, sometimes capped by a heavy circular slab of stone, and containing in addition
to the corpse smaller vessels of a thinner, but still coarse, grey ware, beads, shells, shell

and glass bracelets and sometimes gold ornaments and iron tools. No burials of this
type have been found in the heavily-populated Iron Age sites at Novaliches or in the
Batangas Neolithic sites, but they have been found, widely scattered, and generally in
caves or rock shelters, in the following places: the Batanes and Babuyan Islands,
southern Luzon (Tayabas), and on the islands of Marinduque (Marche’s discoveries of

**1881),** Mindoro, Masbate, Samar and Mindanao. This scattered distribution may simply

The term ' Porcelain Age ' is likely to be misleading if it conjures up a picture of a
cultural advance in Philippine society-as though the use of porcelain in itself might
indicate a primitive Iron Age phase being replaced by, or advancing suddenly into, a
higher civilization marked by the use of porcelain, that most sure indication of social
refinement. In fact, no such advance took place. Ethnically and culturally, the
Porcelain Age folk were no less savage than their immediate ancestors, **as** the accounts
of Chinese writers make abundantly clear. From the number of fragments and whole
pieces found, it is clear that Chinese pottery and porcelain were being imported into the
islands in the 9th and 10th centuries. Ma Tuan-lin, writing in 1317-19, was evidently
quoting from an earlier source when he recorded that traders were coming to Canton from
Luzon in the year 982. The first Chinese account of this trade appears in the **_Chu-fan-_**
**_chih_** of Chao Ju-kua (1225), who reports that in exchange for such native products **as**
wax, cotton, pearls and tortoise-shells, Chinese traders in the Philippines brought a
variety of consumer goods, of which the most sought-after was porcelain. Chao
Ju-ka tells us that the natives were extremely savage. At first the traders did their
business from the safety of their ships, and if a traveller did venture ashore he was likely
to be waylaid by the natives on some jungle path. But, he says, ' if anyone should throw
them a porcelain bowl, they would bend down joyfully to pick it up and hop away shouting.'. Wang Ta-yuan, writing in 1349, records that in exchange for native products the

There were two routes through the Islands. One came down from China, Manchuria
and Japan through the Batanes and Babuyan Islands to Luzon and thence down through
the Visayan Islands to Mindanao, Sulu and Borneo ; this was clearly the route along
which Chinese traders brought their porcelain for barter. The other route followed
the reverse direction northwards from Java, Borneo and Celebes, bringing the Siamese

wares of Sukothai (late 14th century) and Sawankolok (15th century) and possibly also
those of Annam. One whole piece of the dark grey unglazed Cambodian pottery has
been found in Bohol, and fragments in other islands, but there are no indications of a
flourishing trade in Cambodian wares. During the whole of this period, the coarse
native pottery was still made, though there appears to have been a deterioration in
quality ; no doubt it was relegated to the kitchen, and the use of families who could not
afford to buy the imported wares.

Owing to the conditions under which much of this pottery was discovered, coupled
with the great losses suffered during the war, too much reliance should not be placed on
this rough table, which merely indicates distribution in a very general way. No
systematic work has been undertaken since Dr Beyer’s own unpublished study of **1932-**
which has in any case been rendered out of date by subsequent finds ; for a detailed
analysis of the distribution of pottery types, therefore, we must await the appearance in
Manila of a scholar with the monumental patience to sift through and examine the
contents of many hundreds of trays of specimens in the University Museum. In the
meantime, a few general remarks might not be out of place.

Chinese Yuan and Ming wares, particularly celadons and blue and white, are
abundant in almost every area. Later Chinese wares are more common in Luzon than
in the south, while the wares of Sukothai and Sawankolok, which have not been found in
the Batanes or Babuyan Islands or in northern Luzon, are relatively uncommon in central
Luzon, but become ever more abundant as one proceeds southward-which, considering that they probably arrived by the southern route through the archipelago, is only to
be expected.

Of T’ang wares, the most frequently found seem to be **_yiieh_** types, and white or cream
glazed porcellanous wares. Quantities of shards, and a few whole pieces of these types
have been found, chiefly at Novaliches, Tagig and on the beaches at Navotas, a village
on the coast north of Manila which is believed to have been one of the landing places for
Chinese merchantmen. Very little of the lightly-potted reddish Changsha ware has been
found ; perhaps it was not popular with the natives on account of its fragility and the
tendency of the glaze to flake off.

porcelain found in the Philippines, particularly of the earlier periods, appears to be the
product of local kilns in Kwangtung and Fukien, such as have been investigated by Lin
Hui-hsiang of Amoy University and the late Malcolm Farley. One particularly fine

As in blitzed London, the ruins of downtown Manila have presented a great opportunity to the archaeologist, which in one case at least has been grasped, and a stratified
site carefully examined. Recently excavation has been carried out for the foundations
of the Government Service Insurance Building, just east of the old Botanical Garden
outside the walls. This area is the site of the Parian, the quarter to which the Chinese
community was moved ten years after the founding of Manila by the Spanish in 1571.
It existed as a separate ' Chinatown ',-at first almost as a Chinese Ghetto-until the
British occupation of Manila of 1762-1764, when it was abolished, although a few
Chinese may have continued to live there after the date. Last year E. Arsenio Manuel
of the Department of Anthropology of the University of the Philippines carried out a
careful examination, at five different points, of the sides of the trenches cut for placing the
foundations of the Insurance Building **(PLATE** I, **B).** He reports that below three metres
the soil is sandy and contains only the remains of mangrove roots and branches (Level A).
From three metres up to about **_250_** cm. below the surface (Level B) he found a few stray
stoneware shards, suggesting that there were probably pre-Spanish inhabitants in the
vicinity who had a small quantity of Sung and Yuan pottery. Between **_250_** and **_220_** cm.
(Level C), a larger quantity of Chinese black- or brown-glazed stoneware was found,
together with some native red earthenware, and a small number of celadon and Sawankolok shards, indicating a more intensive occupation during the 15th century. It is not
unlikely that Chinese merchants were then already beginning to settle in the Manila area.
The next level, roughly from 220 cm. to 180-160 cm. (Level D), contained almost
entirely Ming wares (chiefly blue and white), which from the types found, and the fact
that Sawankolok wares have now disappeared, suggests a long period of occupation in
the 16th and 17th centuries. This is overlaid by a layer of dark grey clay (Level E), in
the lower part of which some Ch'ing blue and white and some Spanish tiles were found.
Above these a layer of sand containing no shards whatever (Level F) suggests that the
site may have been flooded at about the time of its abandonment in the mid-18th century.
From 120 cm. to about 80 cm. we find renewed evidence of occupation (Level G),
with a quantity of imported European wares, chiefly early 19th century English table
china and stoneware beer bottles. From 80 cm. up to the surface (Level H), the earth
is full of stone, brick and timber, the evidence of the building activities of the last hundred
years*.

*When I visited the site in June-July **1955** and collected shards from the trench-sides **and**
from the mounds of excavated earth, summer rains had already flooded the trenches up to the
two metres level (see **PLATE** I, **B).** This outline is based largely upon information generously
given mc by Mr Manuel. When **his** detailed report is published, it should throw valuable light
not only on the chronology **of** blue and white but **also** on that of many other later Chinese wares
found in the Philippines-particularly the common stonewares and porcellanous celadons which
were made during the Ming Dynasty in **kilns** in Fukien and Kwangtung.

ARCHAEOLOGY IN THE PHILIPPINES

Sites **as** accessible and as clearly stratified as this are rare in the Philippines. However, when we consider the Philippines as a whole, a great deal of ground has been
covered during the last thirty years, the number of sites investigated or visited runs into
several hundred, and an enormous quantity of material has been collected. Much of
this material has been catalogued and the circumstances of its finding recorded by Dr
Beyer himself in type reports which, fortunately, have survived the war ; but until these
reports are published, scholars cannot make the best use of it, nor can the true value and
extent of Beyer's own work be appreciated. Moreover, although so much has been
accomplished, many problems remain to be solved. The question of whether or not
pottery was made by Neolithic Man in the Philippines is of great importance, as is the
true nature of the bronze culture,-if indeed it deserves the name,-and its rel'itionship
with mainland Asia. The chronology of iron age pottery needs further investigation,
and the whole matter of the ' Jar-burial Culture ' and its connection with the more
widespread Iron Age culture is still obscure. It is very much to be hoped that before
long Philippine archaeologists will have the opportunity to take on the double task of
checking results already achieved and filling in some of the more important gaps in the
general archaeological picture.
