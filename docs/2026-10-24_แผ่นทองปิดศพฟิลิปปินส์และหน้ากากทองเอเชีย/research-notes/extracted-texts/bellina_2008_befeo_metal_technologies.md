# The development of metal technologies in the Upper Thai-Malay Peninsula: initial interpretation of the archaeometallurgical evidence from Khao Sam Kaeo *


Thomas Oliver Pryce <sup>1</sup>, Bérénice Bellina-Pryce <sup>2</sup> & Anna T. N. Bennett <sup>3</sup>


**Introduction**


It is usually accepted that metal technologies arrived in the peninsular and insular
Southeast Asian world at some point in the mid 1 <sup>st</sup> millennium BC (Bellwood 2007:
268, Higham 2004: 57). Not only is this appearance of metal use at least a thousand
years later than that proposed for northern parts of the Southeast Asian mainland
(Higham 2004: 52, White [forthcoming]), it also differs significantly in that multiple
metal technologies (copper-alloy, iron, gold) are evidenced simultaneously – there
is no Bronze Age intermission between Neolithic and Iron Age periods. The lack of
any discernable experimental or developmental copper-alloy and iron technologies
has been interpreted as representing the introduction of a complete metallurgical
package to the Thai-Malay Peninsula and Island Southeast Asia (Bellwood 2007:
272).


Although plausible, this foreign origin proposition must be subjected to testing
by archaeometallurgical analysis of material from both metal production and
consumption contexts. The accurate ‘technological reconstruction’ of ancient
metallurgical practices, with the aim of identifying past ‘technological choices’
(Lemonnier 1993, Sillar and Tite 2000) and defining characteristic ‘technological

**Iron**


In the Thai-Malay Peninsula and Island Southeast Asia, the apparently
simultaneous appearance around the 5 <sup>th</sup> century BC of iron technologies and the
earliest evidence of exchange between the Indian subcontinent and littoral Southeast
Asia ( _i_ . _e_ . glass and hard stone ornaments, see Bellina and Glover 2004) has led to
suggestions that iron technologies were transferred by some means from the west
or north (Bellwood 2007: 268, Bronson 1992: 106). Through an ongoing focus
on technological styles in iron metallurgy, it is our aim to begin resolving this
longstanding question.


Iron minerals are common in Southeast Asia, but iron minerals which qualify
as ‘ores’ are unevenly dispersed across the geological landscape (Sitthithaworn
1990). The minerals capable of being smelted in Antiquity are the iron oxides (e.g.
haematite and magnetite), hydroxides (goethite and limonite), and carbonates (e.g.
siderite), but generally not the sulphides (e.g. pyrite). The production of iron by the
solid-state ‘bloomery process’, universal in the Pre-Medieval world excepting China
(Craddock 1995), requires a very high quality of ore with an iron content of 60wt%
or more. The technical requirement for the production of an iron-rich siliceous slag
in addition to the iron metal itself, dictates the use of an ore providing sufficient
iron for both slag and metal. The widespread laterite deposits found in the region
have a variable chemical composition and structure and cannot be automatically
regarded as potential iron ore sources. Although ancient ore sources need not equate
to modern ‘economic’ iron deposits in terms of scale, the sparse distribution of
high-grade iron oxides, hydroxides, and carbonates in Southeast Asia must be
seen as having some impact on the location and nature of iron production in the
region (Bronson 1992: 96). Data from the Thai Department of Mineral Resources
(Sitthithaworn 1990) indicates that the closest economic iron deposit to Khao Sam
Kaeo is 150km southwest on the island of Kao Samui, and after that the nearest
is 200km south-southwest, between Surat Thani and Nakhon Si Thammarat. The

location of these sources suggests the requisite ore minerals for iron smelting are not
to be found in the immediate locality of Chumphon, although there may be small rich
iron sources fully depleted or yet to be recorded. We do not have data for Burmese
mineral resources to the west and northwest. We continue to suffer from a lack of
recorded early iron smelting sites in Thailand (Suchitta 1983), and as yet, Khao

products in the form of slag (Bachmann 1982, Pryce & Natapintu, forthcoming),
meaning that smelting sites within the vicinity should be reasonably identifiable.


Excavations at Khao Sam Kaeo provide clear evidence of iron smithing activity.
The earliest firm date for iron (273 ± 31 BC) comes from an AMS <sup>14</sup> C-dated context
on Hill 3, containing iron-smithing slags. Khao Sam Kaeo’s demand for iron metal,
whether as ore, bloom, billet, or final products, may have been fulfilled by her
known participation in exchange networks. Thus, the site provides firm evidence of
ironworking (though not smelting) at least as old as the 200 BC limit proposed by
Bellwood (2007: 268).


**_Production_**


_Slag and technical ceramic_


Of the eighty test pits (TPs) excavated in the first three seasons at Khao Sam Kaeo,
over 57% have provided us with slags (siliceous metal production waste), totalling
over 53kg. Most of these TPs contained only a few hundred grammes of slag, which
we view as a background scatter. TPs 3, 14, 40, 54, 55, and 62 produced more than
1kg, and TP77 significantly more at over 20kg, and we tentatively interpret the
TP77 locale as representing either a workshop or its associated waste dumping area.
The general distribution of slag-bearing test pits indicates a significant level of iron
smithing was taking place on the western plateau of Hill 3.


The Khao Sam Kaeo slag assemblage is dominated by distinctive smithing hearth
bottoms (SHBs), or fragments thereof, formed by the hammering of hot bloomery
iron. SHBs are very characteristic of iron smithing and are identified by their planoconvex shape, magnetic susceptibility, and coarse iron-rich porous texture (Pryce &
Natapintu, forthcoming). These slags can range widely in size and the larger ones
may easily be confused with smelting furnace slags (Figure 1), but in the absence of
further evidence the default identification must be iron smithing not smelting. This
interpretation is supported by the absence of any technical ceramics which might
be identified as iron smelting furnace wall or tuyère. Confirmation of a smithing
interpretation for iron production awaits laboratory analysis of Khao Sam Kaeo slag
samples at the UCL Institute of Archaeology in London.

**Copper-alloy**


It is commonly assumed that the simultaneous introduction of fully-developed
copper and iron metallurgies to the Peninsula is supported by the paucity of copperalloy tools; the production of utilitarian copper-alloy artefacts being made obsolete
by the presence of mature iron production techniques. The evidence for copperalloy metallurgy at Khao Sam Kaeo supports this thesis as copper-alloy artefacts
recovered from the site consist solely of decorated vessels and ornaments.


**_Production_**


_Slag and technical ceramic_


There are no known economic copper sources in the vicinity of Khao Sam Kaeo
(Sitthithaworn 1990), although there may be small occurrences that were exploitable
in antiquity considering the mineral richness of the area (DMR 2002). To date, we
find no evidence for the smelting of copper at Khao Sam Kaeo, with an absence of
the expected metallurgical slag (MS) and technical ceramic (TC) produced by such
processes (Bachmann 1982). It is possible that a copper smelting locale remains to
be discovered in the area, but for now the actual source of Khao Sam Kaeo’s copper
alloys remains unknown; for the time being we assume that copper consumed
at Khao Sam Kaeo was imported – either as ingots or as finished products. The
intensive contemporary copper production at Non Pa Wai and Nil Kham Haeng in

**_Consumption_**


_Metallic artefacts_


The copper-alloy artefacts from excavation and survey contexts have furnished
us with some exciting evidence for regional and international exchange, both with
the Indian subcontinent and littoral Southeast Asia. Two copper-alloy artefacts from
Khao Sam Kaeo, vessels MA 72 and 73 (Figures 7 and 8) have had their chemical
composition qualitatively studied by Pryce using Polarising Energy Dispersive XRay Fluorescence ([P]ED-XRF) analysis in the Wolfson Laboratories at the UCL
Institute of Archaeology. These interim analyses were on unpolished and corroded
samples, with probable surface enrichment of tin oxide and thus the results are
tentative, but we can state that the composition of both vessels falls comfortably
within the range of what are known as ‘high-tin bronzes’ in the Southeast Asian
archaeological literature. Copper-alloys with 20-30wt% tin form alloys which,
like brass, can have the appearance of the noble metals (silver, gold etc.). Thus,
it is conceivable that ancient metalworkers were intending their products to either
skeuomorph precious metal equivalents or invest their own work with greater value
(assuming silver/gold were ‘precious’ in their value system).

  - Comparisons with the Indian subcontinent and the West


**Knobbed bowl**
A knobbed or central-cone high-tin bronze vessel (MA72, Figure 7) was found
during survey in 2007 at the foot of Hill 2, next to Wall 2. The morphology and
decoration of this bowl is comparable to examples in metal and ceramic known
further north in mainland Southeast Asia (e.g. Ban Don Tha Phet and Dong Son) and
from the Indian subcontinent (e.g. Coimbatore, Adichanallur, and Taxila) (Bellina
1999, Bellina and Glover 2004, Bennett and Glover 1992, Rajpitak and Seeley
1979, Srinivasan and Glover 1995) <sup>6</sup> . As at Ban Don Ta Phet and other sites, the
bowl is decorated with a series of concentric circles surrounding a central cone in
the base of the vessel (Glover 1987). This patterning was probably inscribed using
a lathe for concentric circles, and a drill with a centre point to produce the dot and
circle decoration (Bennett and Glover 1992).


Knobbed ware, which has been argued to derive from Hellenistic ceramics
decorated with an _omphalos_, appeared on the Indian subcontinent during the thirdsecond centuries BC. Therefore, Southeast Asian examples of knobbed wares in
copper-alloys or other materials can be argued to evidence the transmission of a
typological form from west to east during the protohistoric period. The pace with
which knobbed wares were adopted in Southeast Asia suggests Trans-Asiatic
interactions relationships must have been firmly established during the last few
centuries BC (Bellina 1999, Bellina and Glover 2004).


**Bird motif**
Two copper-alloy bird figurines from the Abbot’s private collection at Khao

at Sirkap, Pakistan, and dated to the Saka-Parthe period, _i_ . _e_ . the 1 <sup>st</sup> century AD

Three Heger I type Dong Son bronze drums, two large and one miniature, are
attributed to Khao Sam Kaeo; two of which are now preserved in the Chumphon
National Museum (Figure 8). Those Dong Son drums probably originated in the
Dong Son culture area of northern Vietnam and could have been made during

Jirawattana 2003: 173-180).

6. Southeast Asian ‘high tin bronze’ bowls usually have wrought and quenched microstructures, which

only reduce the brittleness of the alloy but also afford its lustre, reasonance, toughness and corrosion
resistance. Prior to decoration, grinding and polishing were required to remove the hammer marks and
oxidation layers formed during hot working.

**Tin**


The Upper Thai-Malay Peninsula is situated in the middle of a vast tin bearing
system stretching from the islands of Bangka and Belitung (off Sumatra), up the
Peninsula, along the Thai-Burmese border, and into Laos and southern China. Prior
to the discovery of South American sources, the Thai-Malay deposits constituted
ca. 95% of the world’s proven tin reserves, and were of the greatest importance
to the region’s economy until the mid 20 <sup>th</sup> century (Bronson 1992: 83, Hoskins
1969). Tin is widely known as the alloying component required to convert copper
into bronze; but can also be used to ‘plate’ surfaces or act as a colourant/opacifier
in glass technologies. The extremely uneven dispersal of tin around the Eurasian
World, dictates that any bronze-consuming society need maintain contact, directly
or indirectly, with tin producing areas. At present, we are uncertain of when the
Peninsula’s role in inter-regional exchange began to be influenced by tin production,
but it is reasonable to assume that exploitation of local tin reserves played some part
in the pre/protohistory of the region, as in west-central Thailand <sup>8</sup> .


**_Production_**


Within the Upper Peninsula, the greatest density of economic tin deposits is
located near Ranong (Figure 13), less than 100km southwest from Khao Sam Kaeo,
and at the other end of a geological fault thought to be a possible transpeninsular
exchange route connecting the Andaman Sea and the Gulf of Thailand. There is,
as yet, no evidence for the mining or smelting of tin in the immediate vicinity of
Khao Sam Kaeo, but smaller deposits may be fully depleted or remain to be found.

**_Production_**

Rehren and Kraus 1999). Khao Sam Kaeo is some distance from the nearest lead
deposits near Phetchaburi to the North, and Phattalung to the South (Sitthithaworn
1990), so primary production of the metal is again unlikely, but foundry processing
or involvement with copper-alloy technologies remain a possibility.

**Gold**


Gold is widely, though irregularly, distributed throughout Southeast Asia in
hard-rock (igneous and metamorphic) and placer (sedimentary) deposits (Gardner
1972: 333-343). Placers are usually created by the hydraulic erosion of primary
auriferous rocks, and the subsequent redeposition of gold nuggets and flakes in
river gravels. These gold concentrations can be exploited by ‘panning’; using a flow
of water to separate lighter fractions from the dense gold particles (Allchin 1962).
Panning is the most likely method of gold extraction in prehistoric and early historic
periods, requiring little investment in equipment and no specialist technology, but
unfortunately leaving no discernable archaeological signature.


The words ‘gold’ and ‘golden’ are often associated with regional place names
such as ‘Golden Khersonese’ of Ptolemy’s Geography (Wheatley 1961: 144-147).
Indian sources mention _Suvarnabhumi_ (Land of Gold) (Wheatley 1961: 177-184),
possibly referring to lower Burma and the Thai-Malay Peninsula, and _Suvarnadvipa_
(Islands of Gold) which may correspond to Borneo and/or Sumatra. Whether these
terms refer to the metal or to those lands endowed with enriching resources is
unclear. In the Upper Thai-Malay Peninsula, gold lodes are known around Phuket
and Prachuap Khiri Khan (Sitthithaworn 1990), 200-300km from Khao Sam Kaeo,
but small local sources may have been exploited in the past.


Ancient gold is regularly found by farmers and people working in the fields. This
gold is being increasingly actively sought, and much of the metal retrieved from
ancient sites has not been adequately recorded but has, because of its value, been
melted down or in some cases sold into the art market.


**_Production_**


The present evidence at Khao Sam Kaeo is for gold consumption only, although as
with extraction, the production of gold artefacts will probably leave little in the way
of evidence. If gold was being cast then we might expect crucibles and moulds, but
the cold working of native metals would most likely be archaeologically invisible.


**_Consumption_**


Gold ornaments are a useful resource when investigating typological evidence
for cross-cultural interaction due to their decorative nature. A number of interesting
comparators to Khao Sam Kaeo finds have been identified from sites ranging
geographically from the Indian subcontinent to the Philippines and chronologically
from late prehistoric first millennium BC to early historical centuries AD. Khao

have been recovered as chance finds, and were only available for brief inspection.

According to villagers interviewed and private collections examined and
photographed during each field season, most of the gold finds are from the Northern
and Southern slopes of Hill 2. Both these areas have been heavily disturbed but the
debris suggests that the ceramic assemblage is different from that found elsewhere,
and tenuous indications lead us suppose that they might correspond to disturbed
inhumations (see Bellina & Silapanth, this volume, pp. 257-293).


The presence of gold ornaments, perhaps in burial contexts, has strengthened the
evidence for this early river port’s participation in the early Trans-Asiatic networks
of a complex society that was to some degree already in place by the late first
millennium BC, long before the development of the early Indic states by the mid
first millennium AD.


Although speculative at present, it is conceivable that conquest of the Upper
Thai-Malay Peninsula by Funan mentioned in Chinese texts (Pelliot 1903: 265)
may have been in part stimulated by a desire to control the area’s mineral resources.
Funan’s demand for gold and tin, both of which are geologically absent in Southern
Vietnam, may have been one of the stimuli for recorded martial activity in the early
centuries AD.


**Conclusion**


In this paper we have shown that Khao Sam Kaeo has a number of metallurgyrelated technologies and behaviours attested archaeologically: the consumption of
gold ornaments, perhaps in burial contexts, the smithing of iron artefacts, probable
copper-alloy founding, and possible involvement in tin exchange. Iron-smithing
is the only technology indisputably evidenced on site, but the 2007 excavation of
copper-bearing slag and a number of ceramic crucibles increases our confidence
that copper-alloy casting was practiced on site. The efforts of the Thai-French
mission have born much fruit for our understanding of the technical practice and
social significance of metal technologies on site. We have used archaeometallurgical
artefacts to provide further evidence for Khao Sam Kaeo’s participation in TransAsiatic networks, with possible links to material culture from late prehistoric sites in
Vietnam, and the Philippines, and early historical sites in South Vietnam, the Indian
subcontinent, and for the first time to Han China.


The ongoing survey and excavation schedule for the Khao Sam Kaeo team bodes
well for an increased and diversified archaeometallurgical assemblage, and we hope
that future analysis and interpretation of this material will contribute significantly
to our knowledge of late prehistoric and protohistoric metallurgy in the Upper ThaiMalay Peninsula and beyond.
