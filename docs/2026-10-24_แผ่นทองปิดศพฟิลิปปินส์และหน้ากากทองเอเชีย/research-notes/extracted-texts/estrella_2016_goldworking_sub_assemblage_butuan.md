The goldworking sub-assemblage from
Butuan, northeast Mindanao, Philippines: The
archaeological record

Victor P. Estrella*

Abstract

This paper presents the artefacts attributed to goldworking activities found in sites in
Butuan, northeastern Mindanao, Philippines. Currently curated at the National Museum
Regional Branch in Butuan City, the assemblage consists of worked and unworked gold
items, as well as other artefacts deemed to be tools and other paraphernalia. Through the
use of simple visual examination, low-power microscopy and technical study of the items’
morphology, the study aims to recognize initial classification of the materials. In addition,
the Balanghai Archaeological Project excavations in Butuan City since 1976 and the
ASEAN 1986 excavation and conservation workshop, through the reports produced
thereof, were revisited in an attempt to provide contexts to this material culture. By
looking into both the physical objects and the textual references to them, this paper
explores what constitutes the archaeological record of the sites. Moreover, the research
suggests that the artefacts, attributed to the goldworking activities, are sub-assemblages
of a metal craft industry in Butuan that operated from the 10th to the 16th century.

Keywords: Protohistoric Goldworking, Butuan, Gold Sub-Assemblage, Assemblage
Formation

Introduction

In 1975, long wooden planks were accidentally unearthed in mud during an irrigation
project in Butuan City in Mindanao, southern Philippines (Figure 1). The discovery was
brought to the attention of authorities in Manila and, immediately, a team was sent to do
further examinations. Recovered nearby were human remains in dug-out wooden coffins,
animal bones and other cultural materials, such as earthenware and tradeware ceramics,
and some metal implements (Alegre 1976; Salcedo 1976b; Burton 1977). It was found out
that the lumbers were actually that of an early watercraft. Realizing the importance of the
discovery, the National Museum (NM) steered the Balanghai Archaeology Project. It was
named after the vessels recorded in the 16th century Spanish accounts which were used
in the locality (Peralta 1976b; Alegre 1977a; Galpo 1981; Dizon 1989). Remains of more
watercrafts were discovered in the following years (Figure 2) (Alegre 1977b; Galpo 1982).
To date, a total of nine boats have been archaeologically recovered. Three boats were

Butuan, historically, was also a center of commerce in this part of the region during
the 10th through the 14th century (Roxas-Lim 1992; Tarling 1999; Hontiveros 2004). This
flat deltaic land in the northeastern part of the island of Mindanao was a product of faults,

2002). Its geography made it a natural harbor that serviced traders not only from other
cultures within the modern-day Philippine archipelago, but also from the larger cultures
of China, India and the rest of the Southeast Asian Indianized states (Bolunia 2013, 2014).
In fact, tribute missions from P’u-tuan (Butuan) to China appear in _Sung Shih_ ( <mark>`宋史`</mark> <mark>)</mark> or the
History of Sung Dynasty. Accordingly, the tribute missions in 1001 CE, 1003 CE and 1004
CE were initially unsuccessful in establishing tributary status with Imperial China (Scott
1984). However, in 1011 CE, the polity was finally given the position upon the presentation
of a memorial engraved on a gold tablet, paired with other valuable products. Upon the
advent of Spanish colonization in the archipelago, Butuan’s abundance in gold was the
first to be observed as well. Antonio Pigafetta (1525) of the Magellan’s expedition, in his
visit to Mazzaua, described the king with strikingly black hair, clothed in cotton worked
with silk, ornamented with gold rings and gold-handled daggers and gold dental
ornaments. He also noted the population’s use of “good many utensils of gold, but very
little victuals” (Pigafetta 1525:107).

### **Gold in Philippine archaeological record**


The Metal Age came rather later in the Philippine archipelago. Metals only appeared in
Island Southeast Asia in 200 BCE (Bellwood 2007) but when these materials reached the
region, they all appeared at the same time, including gold, primarily because of the
growing interaction among cultures and societies already linked by networks of
exchanges (Hutterer 1977; Dizon 1983, 1990; Pryce et al. 2008).


The earliest appearance of gold in Philippine archaeological sites is as the material of
beads found in Early Metal Age Guri Cave (500 BCE to 300 BCE) and in the Developed
Metal Age Tadyaw Cave (300 CE to 100 CE) (Fox 1970). According to Bennett (2009), these
objects represent the earliest gold finds in maritime Southeast Asia. Reinecke (2015)
disagrees due to some dating concerns. Nevertheless, they could have been products of
trade. In fact, some have similar appearances with those gold items found in Khao Sam
Kaeo in the upper Thai-Malay peninsula, mainland Southeast Asia (Pryce et.al. 2008).

### **National Museum collection**

The National Museum of the Philippines’ own collection of gold artefacts will be the focus
of this study. It is, however, smaller in quantity as compared to what are available in other
public and private collections. One of the salient contributions of this paper is to present
the collection to the archaeological community and to document these gold items in detail.
This study also attempts to emphasize the nature of the collection and to contextualize the
artefacts in the archaeological record.


Both the materials and the site reports pertaining to the materials, produced during
the course of the Balanghai Archaeology Project excavations, were revisited to come up
with this documentation (Figure 4). These reports tell us that earlier metalworking
activities were present in the area as early as the 10th century CE (see Dizon 1989; Bautista
1991). Ronquillo (1987, 1989) in fact initially consolidated a list of cultural materials
pertaining to the presence of this type of activities in Butuan. The list includes: more than

### **The contexts of the materials**


The Archaeological Specimen Inventory (ASI) of the National Museum Butuan City
Branch as well as the site reports of the Balanghai Archaeology Project were the primary
sources of this search for the contexts of the artefacts attributed to the pre-colonial
goldworking activities in Butuan. The former can be found in the Butuan Branch of the
National Museum itself while the latter were accessed at the Records Section of the
Archaeology Division of the National Museum in Manila.

Only 43 gold and 11 non-gold items from Ronquillo’s (1987, 1989) list were found in
the Archaeological Specimen Inventory. The gold objects were assigned 14 accession
numbers since there are some accession numbers referring to two or more items. On the
other hand, the 11 non-gold items have their own assigned accession numbers. Initial
descriptions of the materials came from the inventory itself. There was no attempt to
identify them with certainty in the study since such undertaking is beyond the questions
of the present research but could be an important further study. What the Archaeological
Specimen Inventory failed to provide, however, are the archaeological contexts of the
materials. The museum branch also has no way of determining the exact provenience of

The report of the third Intra-ASEAN Archaeological Excavation and Conservation
conducted in 1986 consolidates all other reports in Butuan. It informs us further about the
places where many of the gold and the non-gold items, which are attributed to the
goldworking activities, were recovered. It identifies the excavations in Luna, Torralba,
Plaza, and Burdeos properties in Sitio Ambangan, Barangay Libertad, Butuan City to be
the sources of the materials (ASEAN 1986). These include the crucibles, the gold buckles,
gold ear ornaments, the gold ring, together with an array of other metal artefacts. ASEAN
(1986:64) suggests that the gold items were found in a rather “dispersed” manner. On the
other hand, other metals, like bronze and iron artefacts, were recovered in concentrated
areas, say in Luna Site, together with the crucibles and other metalworking paraphernalia
(ASEAN 1986).


The search for the provenience of the materials attributed to the goldworking activities
suggests that they belong to a multisite assemblage. The materials’ contexts at the time of
deposition, their exact coordinates within the stratigraphic layers, their proximity with
one another as well as their distribution within the sites excavated, are difficult to
ascertain. Nevertheless, their secondary contexts, in terms of to which cultural layers they
belong and in which locality they were found, were determined. The study cannot
emphasize enough the importance of this information since knowing it places the
materials in its temporal and geographical contexts.

refer to an array of artefacts made of different materials. It is however, important to note
that this set of materials is actually a part of a larger group of artefacts of a larger activity

Two archaeological data gathering phases were conducted to study the artefacts.
Because of the limited access provided by the National Museum during the first phase,
the archaeological materials were examined for one week only. The second phase was
actually a couple of days of discussions and interviews with the museum technician and
museum personnel. The examination of the artefacts was conducted inside the temporary
storage area and the administrative office of the National Museum Region 13 Butuan City
Branch. The researcher, together with an associate, brought their own low-power
microscope, weighing scale, digital caliper, recording forms and other pertinent
instruments.


**_Gold items_**


Of the total 43 gold items, 18 are ear rings, 19 are beads, two form a pair of a buckle, two
are finger rings and two are unworked wires (Figure 5). All of the items studied are
ornamental objects of different sizes and weight.


Two groups can be observed from the general character of the items: the worked and
the unworked. The terms worked and unworked were first used by the ASEAN (1986)
and Ronquillo (1987, 1989) and were found in the present study to be convenient initial
classifications. Worked items are objects from the collection which are finished products.
On the other hand, unworked items, which can also be referred to as pre-worked, are
component parts. The term unworked does not necessarily connote gold items with no
observable morphological change brought about by an application of a technique. Instead,
it is used to differentiate products from component parts that might have been prepared
earlier through simple techniques. It is further differentiated from raw gold, which is its
natural state.


Goldwork is a term used to refer to these objects composed of one or more components
assembled together to form another object (Harrisson 1968). A component part is a

product of a specific technique. The yield of a technique could be the item itself, in the case
of the nine pennanular ear rings, which are all in sheet forms and products of the process
of hammering or tubing. These pennanular ear rings’ diameters range from about 5 mm
to 14 mm, and they weigh from about 0.20 g to 1 g only. Alternatively, the yield could also
be a component of a multipart item, say in the case of another nine but more elaborate
earrings, composed of sheets, wires granules and many more. This type of earrings has
irregular sizes and weighs from about 0.40 g to 1.70 g. Apparently, the gold items that
have one component weigh lighter than those items with two or more components.


Nineteen of the total gold items are beads. One is a long tubular bead, 4 mm in length
and 6 mm in outer diameter. It has surface designs. Eight are smaller circular beads, with
outer diameters of about 6 mm. Based on close examination, both the circular and the
tubular beads are made of small gold granules fused together in a circular manner. The
circular beads appear to be interlocking since the granules were hammered to transform
a portion of the granules into zigzag edges. The tubular bead on the other hand, has
another component part: the tube made of gold sheet connecting two sets of granules
serving as ends. The small interlocking beads, if considered singularly, are the lightest
among the worked gold objects analyzed in the study. Together, they weigh only 6.35 g,
or about 0.35 g each on average.


A pair of buckles is also a part of the collection. The unbroken piece, with a size of
about 50 mm x 40 mm, although quite thin, is the heaviest of all the items, with a mass of
5.10 g. It is actually made out of a single gold sheet, tubed in one side, perhaps for
attachment, and elaborately ornamented through the combination of embossed and
incised designs. The National Museum also has rings. One ring, weighing about 3 g has a
hoop and a flat bezel, whereas another ring, weighing about 2 g, has twisted wires forming
the ring itself.


On the other hand, two objects from the collection are unworked items. Although one
is flat and the other is twisted, these items are both wires, or components with no definite
purpose. The unworked items are also quite small and in terms of weight, they are very
light, with an almost negligible 0.01 g of weight.


There was no attempt to test the quality of gold curated at the National Museum
Region 13 Butuan Branch. It is known, however, that gold items, especially the items found
in the National Museum collection, like in many other collections, are not of pure gold.
They are alloy, but hereto referred to as “gold” with no reference that they are indeed
pure. This is apparent with the observable discoloration in some pieces probably brought
about by corrosion of alloy like silver and/or copper. After all, although gold can be found
in its native state, working it into ornamental and other items could have often required
the addition of other metals to achieve the desired color and hardness (Read 1934; Selwyn
2004; Habashi 2009).


**_Non-gold items_**


Non-gold items include objects made out of fired clay, lithic materials, and metals other
than gold such as lead. Earlier literature suggests possible association between the nongold and the gold items (ASEAN 1986; Ronquillo 1987, 1989; Villegas 2004). The non-gold
objects are deemed to be tools, instruments by which gold objects were produced in the
past. The list of objects attributed to the goldworking activities in Butuan (Ronquillo 1987,
1989) further includes organic materials such as wooden instruments in the form of pincer
and sticks. However, the artefacts themselves were never found in the course of this
study’s search.

Fired clay artefacts are represented by bowl-type and disk-type containers. A couple
of bowl-types were excavated in Balanghai I Site in the 1970s (Peralta 1976b, 1980). More
of this type, together with the disk-type containers, were found in excavations in Luna,
Torralba, Plaza, and Burdeos sites in the 1980s (ASEAN 1986). The bowl-type containers
from the Balanghai I Site were found at the same level as the boat also found in the site.
However, Peralta (1976b, 1980) claims that the items were originally from the midden
layer, or even from the shallower levels above it, and their present position was brought
about by haphazard excavation at that time. The ASEAN Report (1986) and Ronquillo
(1987, 1989) suggest that the containers are crucibles used for metalworking in the locality.


The bowl-type crucibles are brownish grey in color and about 6 cm to 7 cm high with
rim diameters ranging from 10 cm to 11 cm. They are made of clay with very coarse
temper. Spouts are among the obvious features of these containers, probably used to lead
the metal out of the container when pouring. This type is very rough to the touch and has
hard glassy drippings on the outside surface. This external texture is actually caused by
vitrification. Vitrification happens when silica, the primary component of ceramic
materials, and the ashes found in metalworking environments are combined intentionally
or unintentionally through heating (Craddock 1995; Henderson 2000).


On the other hand, the second type of crucible, the disk-type, is represented in the
collection by a reddish brown rim part of an earthenware vessel. It is only about 6 cm long
and 2 cm wide and has a lozenge depression of about 0.40 cm. It was probably used to
melt smaller quantities of metals as its secondary function. Unlike the first type, it has no
visible glassy external surface.


In an analysis using x-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry done by the National
Museum Archaeology Division, the results reveal that the vitrification on the surface of
the bowl-type crucibles is composed of bronze (Alvarez and Bolunia 2013). The results
suggest that this type was used for bronze melting in large quantity whereas the disk was
probably used for melting smaller quantities of gold (Alvarez and Bolunia 2013; Bolunia
2013).


Worked stones and compacted sediments compose the lithic artefacts in the collection.
One item is a rectangular stone with rounded corners. It is grey to black in color with very
smooth surface and was probably cut at both its longer sides. The presence of visible
scratches parallel to its shorter sides suggests that the stone could have been used either
as a sharpener or a touchstone for identifying the quality of gold objects. A couple of
worked compacted sediments are tuffaceous materials. Although highly weathered, the
incised designs on the objects are still recognizable. Most of them exhibit circular
depressions and symmetrical curvilinear lines. These items are believed to be moulds used
for shaping liquid metal.


The last group of items attributed to goldworking are lead objects. Three of this type
were considered for this study. They are all dark grey to black in color. Two of them are
small objects and do have quite definite shapes: circular and somewhat ornamental in
purpose. On the other hand, the third specimen is bigger and heavier. In closer analysis,
it must have been part of a larger object since there are some observable breakages at its
bottom part.

### **Discussion: The archaeological record**


The current attempt to document the gold and non-gold archaeological materials
attributed to the pre-colonial goldworking activities in Butuan, now housed in the
National Museum, is also an endeavor to help understand the archaeological record of the


27

<!-- Page 12 -->
_The goldworking sub-assemblage from Butuan, northeast Mindanao_


locality. One must be guided that this record will never be complete, but, in attempting to
recognize one, the consolidation of several information is necessary (Lucas 2012).


Patrik (1985) lists five meanings of the archaeological record. According to her the
archaeological record may mean the objects of the past, the sediments and the deposits,
the group of these material remains, the archaeological sample, as well as the reports and
the publications produced from them (Patrik 1985:29-30). Lucas (2012) suggests that these
meanings actually appear as either the physical or the textual ontology of the
archaeological record. Whereas the physical refers to the tangible aspect of the material
remains and the processes it entails, the textual, on the other hand, refers to the
information and the documents produced about the objects and the past where they
belong (Lucas 2012). In addition, Jones (2015:336) emphasizes material evidence as the
heart of archaeological inquiry, and both the material remains and “material interaction”
make up such evidence. It goes without saying that both the immaterial and the material
aspects of the archaeological evidence –the artefacts – are important in understanding the
archaeological record.


According to Lucas (2012), when the approach of understanding the archaeological
record is focused on the aggregate of archaeological materials rather than individual
items, the assemblage formation is, therefore, based on find association and/or
combination. The materials are therefore seen as products of one complex human activity
in the past. The process of a material’s formation (Ascher 1968; Schiffer 1972) and the
temporal aspect of it (Binford 1981; Schiffer 1985; Lucas 2005) should be described and
linked in order to establish the assemblage and their relationships. More so, this approach
should be related further with the “deposit formation” approach, wherein the focus is on
the spatial relationship of things and the particular geographical unit (Lucas 2012:76, 90).


In an attempt to determine what constitutes the goldworking assemblage in Butuan,
the site reports were revisited in order to identify and describe the geographical and
temporal distributions of the materials. The materials’ vertical relationships were linked
with the horizontal positioning in order to describe their secondary context.


Conversely, according to Miksic (1999) there are generally two levels of category for
gold artefacts based on how they appear in the depositional environment. These categories
are based on how materials are grouped in relation to other materials in sites. An
assemblage is one category that refers to the totality of the archaeological materials
recovered in a particular site. More importantly, in an assemblage, all artefacts found in
one site should be attributed to one activity (Miksic 1999). A gold hoard is an example of
an assemblage, especially if it is the only find in a site. In this case, all the gold items and
other items made out of other materials are related to one human activity, say the burying
of such materials for safe keeping, or assembling the items for offering to the dead or to
the gods. A gold assemblage could also be viewed as a closed find, wherein the
“contemporaneity” of the materials is based on the depositional process (Graslund 1987;
Lucas 2012). On the other hand, a gold sub-assemblage is a category assigned to a group
of gold and non-gold materials that is deemed to be part of a single human activity in the
past among other past activities (Miksic 1999). According to Miksic (1999) the existence of
sub-assemblages is most likely among complex sites. Butuan, before the period of
colonization in the Philippines, was a thriving port area hosting several economic
activities. Metalworking was among the important economic activities in the area, and
goldworking might have been a specialization based on the recovered archaeological
materials. Hence, the National Museum gold and non-gold items believed to be part of
goldworking activities in 15th-to-16th-century Butuan are actually items in a subassemblage (Miksic 1999).

### **Concluding remarks**


This paper presents an initial documentation of the artefacts attributed to the goldworking
activities in Butuan during the 15th and 16th centuries. In doing so, it attempted to
establish the contexts of the materials through revisiting the written reports and
examining the materials themselves. The paper demonstrates that the materials, the
deposits from where they were found, as well as the reports and literature published on
them constitute the archaeological record from which the contexts of the materials can be
derived. It is the physical and the textual models, among others, that create the
archaeological record (Patrik 1985; Lucas 2012:7).


Due to limitations posited by the records, only the secondary contexts of the materials,
their temporal and geographical distribution, were determined. Absolute dating
conducted on the remains of the watercraft places it at around the 13th century CE. On the
other hand, the stratigraphic data provided by the site reports place the gold finds between
the 15th and 16th centuries CE, consistent with the dating of the balanghai boat, which
were found at deeper levels than the gold items. The ASEAN report (1986) suggests that
materials attributed to goldworking are few and dispersed, as compared to the artefacts
believed to be used for manufacturing items made of other metals, like bronze, which are
plenty and more clustered. Nevertheless, most of the archaeological materials deemed to
be related to goldworking activity were found together with artefacts of a larger
metalworking activity within the area of Butuan.


The archaeological materials themselves suggest that the gold and non-gold items in
the National Museum collection are actually a sub-assemblage of a larger assembly of
materials representing an important activity in pre-colonial Butuan. Compared to an
assemblage, which refers to all materials recovered in a site and attributed to one human
activity in the past, a sub-assemblage refers to a group of materials related to one among
many other human activities in an archaeological site (Miksic 1999). The gold and nongold items are attributed to pre-colonial goldworking activities, which are part of a larger
metalworking industry in the area. Artefacts deemed to be representing as evidence of this
goldworking activity are smaller in number compared to the artefacts believed to be used
in the manufacture of other metal implements.


**_Future Directions_**


Presenting the archaeological record of the goldworking sub-assemblage in Butuan
undeniably opens possible research directions. The true value of addressing the questions
of temporal and geographical contexts as well as the initial identification and classification
of the archaeological materials conducted here lies in an ability to ask further questions to
be investigated.


Since the present study was conducted with a technical perspective, further
elaboration of the techniques used and other processes involved in the production of the
goldworks is then imperative and a system of goldworking can be investigated.


More scientific studies can be done to maximize data that can be gathered from the
artefacts. Compositional/elemental analyses which use high-power microscopy and
technologically-advanced spectroscopy are highly recommended. Such studies could
address questions about elemental signatures and exact provenience. They could also help
in corrosion and authenticity studies, which in return can improve the curation and

conservation of these valuable cultural materials. However, the methods should be nondestructive to maintain their integrity.


A comparative study of gold items across the Philippine archipelago and the Southeast
Asian region should also be the next logical step. It should be done either through the
comparison of data gathered from the compositional and elemental analyses to further
identify interactions in sourcing and manufacture of items, or by way of stylistic and art
historical considerations of the designs to establish connections and exchanges among
cultures reflected in the materials’ iconography and style.
