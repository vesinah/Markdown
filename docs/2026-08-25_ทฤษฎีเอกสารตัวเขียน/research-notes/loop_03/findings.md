## Case 1 — Beowulf (BL Cotton MS Vitellius A.xv, "Nowell Codex")

**What survives.** The sole medieval copy of *Beowulf* is in London, British Library, Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, ff. 94r–209v ("Nowell Codex", second of two codices bound together for Sir Robert Cotton). BL interim catalogue record (live-fetched): copied in England in the late 10th century or first quarter of the 11th century (BL date range field: 990–1024); two contemporary scribes, hand 1 ff. 94r–175v(line 3), hand 2 to f. 209v. Contents: fragmentary homily on St Christopher (94r–98r); *Marvels of the East* with line-drawn miniatures (98v–106v); *Letter of Alexander to Aristotle* (107r–131v); **Beowulf (132r–201v)**; *Judith* imperfect (202r–209v). Provenance: name and date "Laurence Nouell 1563" on f. 94r; into Cotton's library under the bust of Vitellius, shelf A, position 15.
URLs (verified):
- https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/041-001102975 (full catalogue record incl. publications list)
- https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/ (Electronic Beowulf)
- https://www.humanities.mcmaster.ca/~beowulf/manuscript.html (McMaster Beowulf Project description)

**Fire damage and rebinding history.** Cotton library donated to the nation 1700; moved Essex House 1722, Ashburnham House 1730; fire of **23 October 1731** scorched edges of the volume (traditionally rescued by being thrown from a window). G.J. Thorkelin made two transcripts in 1787 (now essential witnesses of pre-decay readings); by the time of British Museum restoration much edge text had crumbled; **1845 rebinding**: leaves mounted in paper frames, which slowed deterioration but concealed letters at frame edges. Foliation attempts at least six times; official foliation 1884. Zupitza's collotype facsimile 1882 (EETS OS 245; 2nd edn 1959); Kemp Malone, *The Nowell Codex* facsimile edition 1963.

**Kevin Kiernan & Electronic Beowulf.** Kiernan, *Beowulf and the Beowulf Manuscript* (Rutgers UP 1981; rev. edn Univ. Michigan Press 1996) argued from codicological evidence (including the scribe B = compiler thesis) for an early-11th-century date close to 1010. Electronic Beowulf begun 1992, first published by the British Library on CD-ROM 1999; current **5th edition (2026)** online at https://ebeowulf.uky.edu/ (live-verified): fibre-optic backlighting + UV images reveal over 100 erased or faded readings and chemical reagent damage; digitized backlighting restores 1300+ letters hidden by conservation frames; includes all early transcripts and collations (Thorkelin A/B etc.). This is the model case of using digital imaging to recover text lost both to fire decay and to earlier conservation.

**Klaeber's Beowulf, 4th ed.** R.D. Fulk, Robert E. Bjork, John D. Niles (eds.), University of Toronto Press, April 2008, 704 pp. Publisher page (verified): https://utppublishing.com/doi/book/10.3138/9780802095671 — explicitly updates scholarship on "the construction of the unique manuscript" and textual cruces while preserving Klaeber's 1922/1936 framework. Standard critical text despite (indeed because of) the single-witness situation: emendation practice documented in Fulk, "Some contested readings in the Beowulf manuscript," *RES* n.s. 56 (2005), 192–223 (listed in BL record).

## Case 2 — La Chanson de Roland (Oxford, Bodleian Digby 23)

**What survives.** The Oxford version (siglum O) of the *Chanson de Roland*: Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Digby 23, part II (72 parchment folios), written in Anglo-Norman French in England, late 12th / early 13th century (M.B. Parkes' palaeographical dating study: *Medioevo romanzo* 10, 1985, 161–175). Composite manuscript: part I is Chalcidius' Latin translation of Plato's *Timaeus*. Medieval provenance opaque: Oseney Abbey ownership inscription; Thomas Allen (d. 1632); given to Bodleian by Kenelm Digby (1634).
URLs (verified):
- https://arlima.net/mss/united_kingdom/oxford/bodleian_library/digby/23.html (full bibliography, live-fetched; full extract saved in documents/)
- https://medieval.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/catalog/manuscript_4352 (official catalogue; content confirmed via search index; direct fetch blocked by bot-protection)
- https://digital.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/collections/digby (digitised images)

**Uniqueness argument.** Other medieval Roland versions exist (Venice MSS 4 and 7; Paris BnF fr. 860; Châteauroux/Versailles; Lyon 964(?); Roncesvalles fragment; Karlomagnus saga) but these are *rifaccimenti*/rewritings, not independent witnesses of the same poem — hence the tradition that O is the unique witness of the *Song of Roland* proper. Gautier's 19th-c. statement: "The most complete and ancient is that of Oxford… All others are Rifaccimenti" (Project Gutenberg #23819).

**Francisque Michel first edition 1837:** *La chanson de Roland ou de Roncevaux du XIIe siècle*, publiée pour la première fois d'après le manuscrit de la Bibliothèque bodléienne à Oxford (Paris, Silvestre, 1837) — the editio princeps after the ms's identification (ARLIMA notice verified).

**Joseph Bédier.** (a) "De l'autorité du manuscrit d'Oxford pour l'établissement du texte de la Chanson de Roland," *Romania* 41 (1912), 331–345, DOI 10.3406/roma.1912.4697 — demonstrated O's authority against eclectic editions. (b) His **édition diplomatique/directe of 1922** (Paris, Éditions d'art Les Bibliophiles / Piazza, with heliogravure reproduction of Digby 23) presents the manuscript text as-is without conjectural normalization — the programmatic answer to editing a unicum: print what the unique witness says, not what an editor wishes it said. Digital copy: https://archive.org/details/joseph-bedier-la-chanson-de-roland-fr-1922 (ebook re-edition; scan-level prefaces better consulted in print/Gallica).
Also: Samaran's ultraviolet readings (*Romania* 55, 1929, 401–410); Laborde/Samaran phototypic reproduction (SATF 1933); Duggan's Brepols corpus edition (2005); Andrew Taylor, *Textual Situations* (2002) on Digby 23 as a composite object read differently by successive communities.

## Case 3 — Catullus (single apograph tradition; lost Codex Veronensis)

**What survives.** No ancient manuscript. The entire transmitted corpus (poems 1–116) descends from one lost archetype, the **Codex Veronensis (V/A)**, which resurfaced in Verona ca. 1300 and was subsequently lost again. Direct evidence for its existence is essentially Benvenuto Campesani's epigram *de resurrectione Catulli poetae Veronensis* (celebrating the return of a copy of the poems to Verona); its identification with the archetype has been questioned but defended as majority view by **Dániel Kiss**, "The lost Codex Veronensis and its descendants: three problems in Catullus' manuscript tradition," in Kiss (ed.), *What Catullus Wrote* (Classical Press of Wales, 2015), 1–27:
- https://www.academia.edu/6816476/ (author's copy)
- https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2016/2016.05.24 (BMCR review of the volume)
Petrarch had access to the Verona codex by 1347 (Cambridge Companion chapter: S.P. Oakley, "The Manuscripts and Transmission of the Text," *The Cambridge Companion to Catullus*, CUP 2021, pp. 263–290, https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/...E8A9D5D2B2EC7D621FEB931ED85F1599).

**The apographs** (all details live-verified from **Catullus Online**, Dániel Kiss's digital edition, http://www.catullusonline.org/CatullusOnline/?dir=edited_pages&pageID=11 — full extract saved in documents/):
- **T** = Paris, BnF lat. 8071 (9th c., Carolingian anthology) preserves only poem 62 — the sole independent witness outside V's descendants.
- **O** = Oxford, Bodleian Canonicianus Class. Lat. 30 (14th c., probably Verona) — copied directly from lost archetype A.
- **G** = Paris, BnF lat. 14137 (Verona 1375; scribe Antonio del Gaio da Legnago identified by Billanovich); contains Campesani's verses; sibling of R, both copied from lost exemplar X < A.
- **R** = Vatican, Ottobonianus lat. 1829 (Florence ca. 1375–1392, made for Coluccio Salutati, who added his own conjectures).
- **m** = Venice, Marcianus lat. 12.80 (Florence ca. 1400) — mere apograph of R, adds nothing.
- Lost **S** (Codex Sannazarii, seen by Parrasio before 1522 near Lyon) was a collateral of T-class readings — a reminder that lost relatives of a unicum occasionally surface in marginalia.
~129 further humanist copies catalogued on the site are all derivative.

**Guarinus / "Doms" check (as requested).** Guarino da Verona (1374–1460), humanist active in Venice 1414–1419 and later public teacher at Verona, belongs to the early-15th-century Veronese humanist milieu through which V-descendant copies circulated (Verona exhibition page, University of Verona digital library: https://digitallibrary.univr.it/en/guarino — verified). No modern critical edition is "by Guarino"; the user-suggested editor name "Doms" could **not** be verified as a Catullus editor in any scholarly source found. Actual editorial chain relying on the apographs: Lambinus 1566 (systematic conjectures), Scaliger 1577, Schwabe, **R.A.B. Mynors OCT 1958** (text based principally on O), Eisenhut (Teubner), Goold (Loeb), and **Kiss's own critical edition** (De Gruyter, Text und Textgeschichte 55, 2008) plus Catullus Online. Because all complete-copy mss descend from one lost, corrupt apograph, Catullus is a textbook case where conjectural emendation is unavoidable (metrical impossibilities etc.).

## Case 4 — Asser, *Vita Ælfredi regis* (manuscript destroyed 1731)

**What survives.** Nothing of the medieval manuscript itself. The sole known copy was Cotton MS **Otho A xii**, written ca. 1000, destroyed in the Ashburnham House fire of 23 October 1731 (same fire as Beowulf's volume). The text is reconstructed from:
1. **Earlier transcripts** of the Cottonian manuscript (multiple hands, some copying Matthew Parker's marginal annotations as if part of the text — a classic editorial hazard); a facsimile of the ms's first page was made in 1722 by James Hill for Francis Wise;
2. **Extracts embedded by medieval authors**: Byrhtferth of Ramsey (*Historia Regum*), Florence of Worcester, the author of the *Encomium Emmae Reginae*.
Sources: CODECS entry https://codecs.vanhamel.nl/Vita_%C3%86lfredi_regis (verified); Oxford Maldon context page https://english.nsms.ox.ac.uk/oecoursepack/maldon/context/manu.htm (Otho A xii also carried *The Battle of Maldon*, now extant only via the Casley transcript, Bodleian Rawlinson B.203).

**Editions.**
- **John Smith, 1721** (first printed edition, based on the still-extant Cottonian manuscript shortly before the fire).
- **William Henry Stevenson, *Asser's Life of King Alfred, together with the Annals of Saint Neots erroneously ascribed to Asser*, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1904** — built by collating all surviving transcripts; still the standard Latin text (130-page introduction). Scans: https://archive.org/details/asserslifekinga00stevgoog ; Cook's English translation of Stevenson's text: Project Gutenberg #63384 (https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/63384, live-verified; preface quotes Plummer 1902 and Stevenson 1904 on authenticity).
- Modern annotated translation: Keynes & Lapidge, *Alfred the Great* (1983).

**Authenticity debate.** Impugned by Thomas Wright (1841), Sir Henry Howorth (1876–77), V.H. Galbraith (1964, "forgery by Leofric-era writer"), Alfred P. Smyth (2002, forgery by Byrhtferth thesis); defended by Plummer (1902: cannot be later than 974), Dorothy Whitelock (Stenton Lecture 1967, *"Genuine Asser"*), Stevenson (1904); today almost universally accepted as genuine (Cook/Gutenberg summary; CODECS). Methodological significance: when the physical witness is destroyed, authenticity arguments shift entirely onto transcript quality, internal criticism (style, vocabulary, anachronisms), and cross-references in independent medieval works — exactly how the debate was conducted.

## Case 5 — Archimedes Palimpsest (Walters Art Museum)

**What survives.** A 13th-century Byzantine prayerbook whose parchment was recycled from a 10th-century Constantinopolitan copy (associated with Leo the Geometer's school) of seven treatises by Archimedes — including the **only** surviving copies of *The Method of Mechanical Theorems* and *Stomachion*, and the Greek text of *On Floating Bodies*. Also palimpsested: speeches of Hyperides (otherwise nearly lost) and a commentary on Aristotle's *Categories*.
History (project site https://archimedespalimpsest.org/about/history/, live-fetched; full extract saved):
- Prayerbook finished **13 April 1229** (colophon deciphered by John Lowden 2002 under UV); probably made in Jerusalem (Jerusalem-rite prayers); belonged to Mar Saba (16th-c. inscription, now lost); in the Metochion of the Holy Sepulchre, Constantinople by ~1840.
- Catalogued by Papadopoulos-Kerameus 1899 (Ms 355); his transcription of a few undertext lines led **J.L. Heiberg** to visit in **1906**; Heiberg photographed and published the Archimedes texts in his new edition (1910–1915). One leaf detached via Tischendorf is now Cambridge UL Add. 1879.23 (identified by Nigel Wilson, 1968).
- Post-1908 losses: three pages with Archimedes text disappeared entirely; severe mold damage; four evangelist portraits painted over the text after 1929.
- **Sold at Christie's New York, 29 October 1998, $2,000,000** (after failed Greek injunction); deposited at the **Walters Art Museum, Baltimore, January 1999**; conservation + multispectral imaging (Roger Easton/RIT, Keith Knox, William Christens-Barry; see https://archimedespalimpsest.org/about/imaging/) and later X-ray fluorescence recovered undertext invisible to Heiberg; all raw data released open-access online; scholarly edition: **Netz, Noel, Tchernetska, Wilson (eds.), *The Archimedes Palimpsest*, 2 vols, CUP for the Walters Art Museum (© 2011/2012)** — JHS review https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/...0A925F281482EE4AE4E25A5057B42898
- Popular account: Reviel Netz & William Noel, *The Archimedes Codex* (2007). New reading of *Method* Prop. 14 from the palimpsest: Netz/Saito/Tchernetska, *Sciamvs* 2 (2001), 9–29.
Significance: demonstrates that a unicum can yield new text 90+ years after its standard edition purely through imaging technology + open-data policy.

## Case 6 — Pearl / Sir Gawain and the Green Knight (BL Cotton Nero A.x)

**What survives.** One modest quarto vellum manuscript, London BL Cotton Nero A.x (part 2), late 14th century, containing the **only** copies of four poems: *Pearl*, *Cleanness* (or *Purity*), *Patience*, and *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*; single scribe, with full-page illustrations (underdrawings possibly by the same hand — Maidie Hilmo, "Did the Scribe Draw the Miniatures in BL MS Cotton Nero A.x?", *Journal of the Early Book Society*; BL pigment/ink analysis by Paul Garside using multispectral imaging and XRF supported iron-gall underdrawing consistent with scribal ink: reported at https://www.medievalists.net/2018/10/gawain-scribe-also-artist/). The ms suffered water damage/staining (visible fading noted already in Tolkien & Gordon's introduction: "The manuscript is a small quarto on vellum… MS. Nero A. x" — wikisource scan of the 1925 edition, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Sir_Gawain_and_the_Green_Knight_-_Tolkien_and_Gordon_-_1925.djvu/15).

**Editorial practice.**
- Facsimile: Israel Gollancz (ed.), *Pearl, Cleanness, Patience and Sir Gawain … reproduced in facsimile from the unique ms. Cotton Nero A. X*, EETS OS 162 (1923; repr. 1931/1955/1971/2007) — note the title's explicit appeal to uniqueness.
- **J.R.R. Tolkien & E.V. Gordon (eds.), *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*, Oxford: Clarendon, 1925**; 2nd edition revised by Norman Davis, 1967 — standard conservative edition from the unicum.
- **Malcolm Andrew & Ronald Waldron (eds.), *The Poems of the Pearl Manuscript*** (York Medieval Texts, 1978; 5th fully revised edn with prose translation, Exeter UP 2007) — the standard classroom/scholarly edition of all four poems; cf. Finch/Andrew/Waldron/Peterson, *The Complete Works of the Pearl Poet* (California, 1993).
- Punctum Books open-access translation: *Cotton Nero A.x: The Works of the "Pearl" Poet* (https://punctumbooks.com/titles/cotton-nero-a-x-the-works-of-the-pearl-poet/).
- **Digital project:** The Cotton Nero A.x Project (Murray McGillivray, University of Calgary) — https://gawain-ms.ca/ (live-verified) — high-resolution digital surrogate of the unique manuscript.

Because every reading rests on one damaged copy, editors of SGGK keep emendation minimal and flag it; textual notes concentrate on faded/illegible letters rather than variant selection (no variants exist).

## Case 7 — Poetic Edda: Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to

**What survives.** Reykjavík, Árni Magnússon Institute, **GKS 2365 4to** ("Codex Regius", Konungsbók): 45 vellum leaves, single Icelandic scribe, palaeographically dated ca. 1270–1280; sole or principal source for most of the Poetic Edda (29 poems: 10 mythological, 19 heroic). Hávamál survives here alone; Völuspá additionally in Hauksbók. History: obtained by Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson **1643**; presented to King Frederik III **1662** (hence "King's Book"); Royal Library Copenhagen until returned by ship to Iceland **21 April 1971**; rebound in calfskin 1992. Official catalogue/facsimile: https://handrit.is/manuscript/view/is/GKS04-2365 (server returns 403 to automated fetchers but is the authoritative handrit.is record); phototypic facsimile Wimmer & Jónsson 1891.
Key descriptive sources (verified):
- Terry Gunnell, "Eddic Poetry," in McTurk (ed.), *A Companion to Old Norse-Icelandic Literature and Culture* (2005): "scholars refer to eddic poetry… usually mean the contents of a single… manuscript."
- Einar G. Petursson, *Medieval Scandinavia* (1988), quoted with Vigfússon 1883 at http://www.germanicmythology.com/works/CODEXREGIUS.html (live-fetched).
- Open Book Publishers dual-language edition: https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0308/ch1.xhtml (Völuspá from Codex Regius).

**The lacuna problem.** The fifth gathering (~8 leaves / 16 pages) is missing between the end of *Sigrdrífumál* and *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu* (the "Great Lacuna"); Brynjólfur himself marked the break "λόγος ἄκεφαλος" (headless tale). Petursson's notable claim: since complete *Sigrdrífumál* exists in **17th-century paper copies** connected with the copying of *Völsunga saga* (whose vellum Brynjólfur received 1641), the lacuna physically occurred **between 1641 and 1643** — i.e., leaves were lost/removed just before acquisition. Reconstruction of lost Sigurd material relies on:
1. those 17th-c. paper transcripts (for the end of Sigrdrífumál);
2. the prose paraphrase in *Völsunga saga*, which used this vellum or its exemplar while the leaves were intact;
3. stanzas quoted/paraphrases in Snorri Sturluson's *Snorra Edda* (e.g., Hávamál stanza variants, Grímnismál quotations) and the parallel version of Völuspá in Hauksbók.
Editors (Bugge onward, Neckel/Kuhn, Pettit's OBP edition) mark such supplied matter distinctly — reconstruction from indirect tradition, never silently.

## Case 8 — Winchester Manuscript of Malory, found 1934

**What survives.** Until 1934 Malory's *Morte Darthur* was known only from **William Caxton's printed edition of 1485** (two copies survive: John Rylands, Manchester; Pierpont Morgan, New York — i.e., even the "print witness" is nearly unique). In **June 1934 Walter F. Oakeshott**, librarian (later headmaster) at Winchester College, discovered during library cataloguing a large paper manuscript (~480 leaves) lacking beginning and end: the **Winchester Manuscript**, written by two professional scribes c. 1470–1483, now British Library Add. MS 59678.
Verified sources:
- Anna Caughey, "Print and Manuscript," Great Writers Inspire (Oxford): https://writersinspire.org/content/print-manuscript (includes Oakeshott's own account of opening the safe).
- **The Malory Project** (Takako Kato, University of Leicester; Nick Hayward; Keio funding): electronic edition + digital facsimiles of Winchester AND of the Rylands Caxton: http://www.maloryproject.com/ (live-verified).
- JSTOR: M.N. Salda, "Caxton's Print vs. the Winchester Manuscript: An Introduction," https://www.jstor.org/stable/27869113; R.M. Lumiansky, "Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte Darthur, 1947–1987," https://www.jstor.org/stable/2851785.

**Findings & comparison.** Printer's-ink offsets show the Winchester ms was in Caxton's workshop, yet linguistic evidence shows Caxton used a different (lost) copy. Caxton divided the work into 21 books/507 chapters vs the manuscript's 8 tales; he cut the Roman-war material and altered orthography toward Kentish usage; the famous corruption "sir Tristram slewe kynge Mark" (Caxton) is corrected in Winchester ("Sir Bellyngere revenged the dethe of hys fadir…"). Eugène Vinaver edited the manuscript in *The Works of Sir Thomas Malory* (1947; 3rd edn revised P.J.C. Field 1990), arguing the work was eight separate tales — spawning decades of debate (Lumiansky's *Malory's Originality* 1964; Matthews' counter-arguments about cross-references). Stephen Shepherd's Penguin (2004) also uses the Winchester text. Oakeshott, "The Finding of the Manuscript," in Bennett (ed.), *Essays on Malory* (1963); Kelliher, "The Early History of the Malory MS," in Pearsall/Takamiya, *Aspects of Malory* (1986).
Lesson: a newly found unicum can instantly reorganize the editorial history of a text previously mediated solely by print — and shows "unique manuscript vs unique print" as parallel single-witness problems.

## Case 9 — King Ramkhamhaeng Inscription (Sukhothai Inscription No. 1) — authenticity controversy

**What survives.** A four-faced siltstone stele (Bangkok National Museum reg. no. 158/2511), traditionally dated 1292 CE (Śaka 1211), discovered in 1833 at Sukhothai (Noen Prasat) by Prince Mongkut (later Rama IV) together with the stone throne. Traditionally regarded as the earliest Thai script and foundational for Thai historiography (Damrong's "Sukhothai golden age" paradigm; used for the successful 1991 World Heritage nomination of Sukhothai).
Verified sources:
- UNESCO Memory of the World register page (registered **2003**): https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/king-ram-khamhaeng-inscription + nomination form PDF https://media.unesco.org/sites/default/files/webform/mow001/thailand_king_ram_khamhaeng_inscription.pdf
- **James R. Chamberlain (ed.), *The Ram Khamhaeng Controversy: Collected Papers*, Bangkok: Siam Society, 1991** — entire volume online at SEAlang: http://sealang.net/sala/ram91/htm/index.htm (live-verified; contributors include Vickery, Piriya Krairiksh [3 articles], Terwiel, Diller, Woodward, Wyatt, Gosling, Penth, Gedney, Doré, O'Connor).
- **Michael Vickery**, "The Ram Khamhaeng Inscription: A Piltdown Skull of Southeast Asian History?" (paper at ANU conference July 1987; expanded in Chamberlain 1991, pp. 3–51): http://michaelvickery.org/vickery1991ram.pdf (file confirmed live; too large to fetch inline). Vickery attacks authenticity via script (complete modern tone-marking system otherwise attested only 17th–18th c.), vocabulary, and content. Sequel: "Piltdown 3," *JSS* 83 (1995).
- **Piriya Krairiksh**, "An Epilogue to the Ram Khamhaeng Inscription" (arguing the stele was carved under Mongkut): http://sealang.net/sala/archives/pdf8/pirya1991epilogue.pdf (file confirmed live).
- **Rebuttals / pro-authenticity:** A.B. Griswold & Prasert na Nagara, "Epigraphic and Historical Studies No. 9: The Inscription of Rāma Gaṃhṇ of Sukhodaya (1292 A.D.)," *Journal of the Siam Society* 59.2 (1971) — PDF on the Siam Society's own site: https://thesiamsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/1971/03/JSS_059_2k_GriswoldPrasert_InscriptionOfKingRamaGamhenOfSukhodaya1292.pdf (live fetch returned valid PDF stream); David K. Wyatt, "Contextual Arguments for the Authenticity…" (sealang archive); Hiram Woodward, "Sukhothai religious architecture and its relevance to the authenticity of Inscription One" (in Chamberlain 1991); Hans Penth, "Difficulties with Inscription No. 1," *JSS* 77 (1989) (Siam Society PDF); Anthony Diller's historical-phonology papers (sealang ram91 index).
- **Nidhi Aeusrivongse (นิธิ เอียดสระบุรี)** raised historical-context doubts in Thai mid-1980s (2529/1986), catalyzing the debate (overview in Mukhom Wongthes, *Intellectual Might and National Myth*, Matichon 2003; special Thai volume ed. Sujit Wongthes, *Silpa Watthanatham* 1988, ISBN 9748350843).
- Scientific test: **1990 scanning electron microscopy + energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry** comparing the RK stele with four other Sukhothai-period inscriptions concluded similar weathering age (~700–500 years) — widely reported (e.g., binntour.com summary; UNESCO nomination discusses conservation).
- Later assessments: B.J. Terwiel, "Using Ockham's Razor with Respect to the Ram Khamhaeng Controversy" (2006 conf./2011 publication; academia.edu 9903229); Terwiel, *The Ram Khamhaeng Inscription: The Fake That Did Not Come True* (2010).
**Verification caveats (honest flags):** (a) Peter Skilling's name could not be tied to a specific RK counter-article in the sources verified here — counter-arguments are instead attributable to Wyatt, Woodward, Gosling, Penth, Diller, Prasert na Nagara/Griswold; (b) siamese-heritage.org (former mirror of JSS PDFs) is now dead/domain-squatted — use thesiamsociety.org instead; (c) the 2531/1988 seminars were primarily domestic-Thai (Silpa Watthanatham volume + press), while the English-language record is Chamberlain 1991; debate remains formally unresolved though majority opinion accepts essential authenticity (UNESCO registration 2003 reflects institutional acceptance).

## Case 10 — Methodology for handling unique witnesses

**A. Editorial strategy.**
- With a true unicum, stemmatics is impossible; editors default to copy-text/diplomatic editing of the single witness (Bédier's 1922 Roland; Gollancz's "unique ms." EETS facsimile; Vinaver's Winchester-based Malory; Andrew & Waldron's Pearl-poem edition).
- Conjectural emendation remains legitimate but must be flagged: Oxford Handbook of Greek and Latin Textual Criticism, ch. "Emendation" (academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62564/chapter/560047904 — abstract verified): since no archetype is error-free, emendation is necessary, yet "lacks a clear methodological basis and consists largely of ad hoc judgements"; responsible practice lies between accepting everything construable and emending whenever imaginable. Catullus and Klaeber's Beowulf show the spectrum (Catullus: heavy conjecture because archetype corrupt; Beowulf: light-touch apparatus keyed to UV-recovered letters).
- When a second witness emerges (Winchester Malory vs Caxton; Hauksbók Völuspá vs Regius), the whole editorial history must be renegotiated — uniqueness claims are provisional.

**B. Physical evidence & dating.**
- Palaeography: script comparison gives relative dating with acknowledged subjectivity (Parkes 1985 on Digby 23; Dumville vs Kiernan on Vitellius A.xv dating; BL dates Nowell Codex 990s–early 1000s).
- Radiocarbon dating of writing support: Popović et al. 2025 (*PLoS One* 20(6):e0323185 — full article verified; extract saved): AMS 14C on parchment with lipid-cleaning pretreatment; Bayesian calibration (bimodality caution); AI style model trained on 24 dated samples predicts dates MAE ≈ 28–31 years for undated mss; key lesson — triangulate material (14C), geometric (shape analysis), expert palaeography, each with explicit uncertainties.
- Codicology: quire/binding archaeology when rebinding destroyed structure (Beowulf's six foliations; 1845 paper-frame remounting; Codex Regius's binding history — Vigfússon's complaint that the 18th-c. binder "did the vellum some harm by plowing its edges").

**C. Imaging & scientific recovery.**
- Multispectral/hyperspectral imaging separates inks spectrally (Archimedes project imaging pages; Electronic Beowulf's fiber-optic backlighting restoring 1300+ letters hidden under conservation frames; MSI of Nero A.x miniatures).
- UV light for erased/faded script: Samaran's 1929 UV readings of Digby 23; Lowden's 1229 colophon decipherment; eBeowulf UV readings (>100 recovered).
- X-ray fluorescence (XRF): elemental mapping of undertext/pigments (Archimedes project; BL pigment analysis of Gawain miniatures).
- Material authentication: SEM+EDX weathering comparison used in the Ram Khamhaeng controversy (1990) — science entering epigraphic authenticity disputes.

**D. Indirect tradition & reconstruction.**
- Lost-text recovery from extracts and paraphrase: Asser reconstructed via Byrhtferth/Florence of Worcester; Edda lacuna via Völsunga saga prose + Snorra Edda quotations + 17th-c. paper transcripts; Maldon via pre-fire transcript. Editors must label all such matter as reconstructed inference.

**E. Conservation ethics & access.**
- Minimal intervention and reversibility (Beowulf 1845 frames slowed decay but obscured letters; modern digital backlighting reverses the information loss without touching the object; Codex Regius 1992 conservation binding).
- Provenance transparency and lawful title (Christie's 1998 injunction litigation over the Archimedes Palimpsest; repatriation of Codex Regius 1971).
- Open digital surrogates reduce physical handling risk of unica and democratize access (Electronic Beowulf; Archimedes raw data release; Malory Project; handrit.is; Catullus Online; UNESCO MoW preservation/access mandate).
- Authenticity debates require separating scientific questions (material age) from philological ones (language, content) and keeping them publicly documented (Ram Khamhaeng seminar culture; Mukhom Wongthes' forensic sociology of the debate).
