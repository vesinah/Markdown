  1. (§ 87, p. 42): "The parallel expansion hypothesis demonstrates that the Khuddakapāṭha (Khp) and the Paritta (Catubhāṇavārapāḷi) developed from a common archaic core, with the Paritta functioning as the living, continuously recited liturgical tradition of Theravāda Buddhism."
  2. (§ 156–157, p. 81): "Extra-canonical parittas such as the Jayamaṅgalagāthā and Jinapañjara represent the organic growth of protective Buddhist poetry (apotropaic literature), which was formally recognized and commented upon in medieval anthologies like the Sāratthasamuccaya."
  3. (§ 405, p. 182): "The discovery of the Telakaṭāhagāthā (verses 2–4) inscribed on the Sra Bua inscription in Dong Lakhon (dated 761 CE / 1304 BE) provides archaeological proof that non-canonical Pali protective poetry had spread to the Chao Phraya River basin as early as the Dvaravati period."
  4. (§ 419–420, p. 190): "During the 15th and 16th centuries, Chiang Mai (Lan Na) became a global center of Pali scholasticism, producing masterworks such as the Jinakālamālī, Maṅgalatthadīpanī, and apocryphal texts like the Paññāsa Jātaka, which established the fertile environment for the compilation of the Jinapañjara (Jaiyabengshorn)."
