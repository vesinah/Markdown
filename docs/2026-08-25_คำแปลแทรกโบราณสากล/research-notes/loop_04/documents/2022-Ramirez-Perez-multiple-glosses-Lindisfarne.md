# A conditioned scribe: The use of multiple interlinear glosses to the Lindisfarne Gospels

- Author: Elisa Ramirez Perez (Leiden)
- Published: Leiden Medievalists Blog, March 03, 2022
- URL: https://www.leidenmedievalistsblog.nl/articles/a-conditioned-scribe-the-use-of-multiple-interlinear-glosses-to-the-lindisfarne-gospels

## Full text (reproduced excerpts, complete argumentative content)

What happens when early medieval glossators provide multiple translations for one and the same Latin word and what can this tell us about language history?

### The Lindisfarne Gospels

The Lindisfarne Gospels (British Library, Cotton Nero D.iv) is a copy of the Holy Gospels created during the 8th century at Lindisfarne. The main body was written in Latin. In between the Latin lines, late Old English interlinear glosses were added during the 10th century by a monk and scribe named Aldred, who wrote in a distinct Northumbrian dialect characteristic of northeast Britain. By translating the Gospels into his native Old English, Aldred provided us with the first ever English translation of the text.

### Recorded history of the English language

The late Old English period was a time of considerable linguistic change, when fairly synthetic (case-driven) Old English began showing consistent loss of case marking and grammatical distinction. The glosses record early stages of a great variety of linguistic changes (phonological weakening, loss of grammatical distinction, reduction of declensional classes, borrowing of Scandinavian words, and so on) which anticipated the state of affairs found in Middle English.

### Morphological change in the making

The author studies weak verbs class 2, which contain a medial vowel (-i- formant) between stem and ending: locian 'to look', locio 'I look'. This -i- formant was lost, and it is in the Lindisfarne glosses that this change is first recorded. Example: Latin respicite glossed behaldas l locas 'behold l look', where locas already lacks the formant (fol. 38r).

### Multiple glosses and morphological conditioning

Multiple glosses provide multiple Old English translations for one Latin word, joined by the sign l (vel). Examples from Matthew:

- eradicetis 'you uproot' glossed unwyntrumias l unclaensias (both weak class 2, both retain -i-) fol. 53r
- volenti 'requesting' glossed nedende l dhreatende (first element weak class 1, second weak class 2 without -i-)
- perden(dum) 'destroying' glossed to f(or)doanne l to f(or)losanne (strong verb first, then weak class 2 without -i-) fol. 30v

Pattern: when the first element of a multiple gloss is itself weak class 2, the second retains the medial -i-; otherwise it loses it. The author interprets this as morphological priming or morphological conditioning: the shape of the first element conditions the shape of the second. In Matthew's gospel alone there were many such cases; later research showed the phenomenon throughout the Lindisfarne glosses. Conclusion: linguistic change is conditioned not only by phonological factors but also by priming; linguistic change is a multifaceted phenomenon clearly visible in the Lindisfarne glosses.
