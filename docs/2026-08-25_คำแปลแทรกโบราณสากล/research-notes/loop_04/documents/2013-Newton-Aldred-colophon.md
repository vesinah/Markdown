# Domiciling the Evangelists in Anglo-Saxon England: a Fresh Reading of Aldred's Colophon in the Lindisfarne Gospels

- Venue: Anglo-Saxon England, Vol. 41, pp. 101-144 (2013)

- Academia.edu full text: https://www.academia.edu/7326684/

## Abstract (as reproduced)

The Codex 'Lindisfarnensis' (London, British Library, Cotton Nero D. iv, early eighth-century) was glossed in Old English by the tenth-century priest Aldred. Aldred's colophon purports to give information about the eighth-century makers of the manuscript at Lindisfarne. What is actually reliable about this highly literary colophon is Aldred's purpose in writing the gloss: to give the Evangelists a voice to address 'all the brothers' - particularly the Latinless. We propose new interpretations of three OE words (gihamadi, inlad, ora) misunderstood before. Aldred was learned; his sources extend from Ovid through the Fathers to contemporary texts.

## Introduction (excerpt)

"Some two centuries or more after its creation, that is, around 950, the priest Aldred, at Chester-le-Street (between Newcastle-upon-Tyne to the north and Durham to the south) glossed the entire text of the manuscript interlinearly in Old English - the earliest Old English version of all four gospels that survives. Aldred tells us that he did this in the colophon he added in his own hand in Old English and in Latin at the end (259rb)."

Aldred asserts the book was written by Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne (d. 721), bound by his successor AEthelwald, and decorated with gold and gems by Billfrith the anchorite. This information has been generally accepted by historians of art and palaeographers such as E. A. Lowe and Michelle Brown.

Lawrence Nees (Speculum, 2003) analyzed the colophon as a whole, calling attention to artistic symmetries such as the play upon the number four: invocation of the triune God (3+1), the four evangelists, and the four churchmen (Eadfrith, AEthelwald, Billfrith, Aldred). Nees called for an investigation of Aldred's sources; the present study traces backgrounds including Ovid, Jerome, Cassiodorus, Bede, Alcuin, Theodulf, and late Anglo-Saxon law codes and charters.

## Notes

- Places the glossing context at Chester-le-Street, ca. 950 (colophon-based dating; other sources give third quarter of the tenth century, probably before 970).
- Argues the colophon is a literary creation whose most reliable datum is Aldred's stated purpose: vernacular access for those without Latin.
