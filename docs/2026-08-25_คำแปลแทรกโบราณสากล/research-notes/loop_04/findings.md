## Key scholarly findings (with sources)

1. Date/context of Aldred's gloss: The gloss was written by Aldred, provost of the community of St Cuthbert at Chester-le-Street, in the third quarter of the 10th century, probably before 970 (CLASP entry 343: "OE gloss s. x3/4, prob. before 970"); Newton et al. state "around 950" based on the colophon context; the BL catalogue confirms the colophon on f. 259r names Aldred as the glossator ("hit ofgloesade on englisc") and records payments he made for portions (Matthew to God/St Cuthbert; Mark to the bishop; Luke to the community; John for himself).
2. Dialect: The gloss is the principal witness of late Old Northumbrian (De Gruyter book description; Leiden blog notes its Northumbrian character); it is the earliest surviving Old English version of all four gospels (Newton et al.).
3. Lindisfarne-Rushworth relationship: Rushworth/Bodley Auct. D.2.19 is an Irish gospel book of ca. 800 associated by colophon with Mac Regol of Birr; its OE gloss was added late 10th c. at Harawuda (Harewood) by Farman (Mercian; Matthew, Mark 1-2.15, Jn 18.1-3) and Owun (southern Northumbrian; remainder). Priority of Lindisfarne is likely; Ross argued Farman obtained access to Aldred's gloss when Ru1 was nearly finished (from Mt 26 onwards) and Owun followed it closely in Ru2; Kotake has challenged details (whether Owun really copied directly from Lindisfarne) in his 2016 chapter "Did Owun Really Copy from the Lindisfarne Gospels?" (TOC, De Gruyter) and Kotake 2012/2017 studies cited in idus.us.es article.
4. Historical-linguistic value: The glosses record changes in progress anticipating Middle English: extension of genitive -es and plural -as (Rodriguez Ledesma 2022, Cambridge ELL); loss of medial -i- in weak verbs class 2 (Leiden blog); Northern Subject Rule already operative (Cole 2014); third-person null subjects (Walkden 2016); Scandinavian borrowings (Leiden blog summary of research); morphological priming in multiple glosses (Ramirez Perez 2022).
5. Multiple glosses: Aldred frequently gives two or three synonyms for one Latin lemma joined by l (vel); functions studied by Pons-Sanz 2016 (open access via ORCA Cardiff) and Bolze 2016 (beon forms); incomplete double glosses predominate in his later Durham A.iv.19 gloss (Benjamins 2024).
6. Paris Psalter (BnF lat. 8824): mid-11th century bilingual psalter by one scribe, Wulfwinus cognomento Cada; Latin Romanum psalter left column, Old English right column; Pss 1-50 in prose (attributed credibly to King Alfred) with exegetical introductions adapted from Augustine's fourfold scheme; Pss 51-150 in a unique metrical version (Emms 2008; Biblissima; O'Neill 2001; ROEP). It departs from the older interlinear word-by-word psalter gloss tradition (Vespasian, Junius, Vitellius, Regius psalters) toward readable prose paraphrase (O'Neill dissertation abstract).
7. Authorship debate: whether Aldred alone wrote the whole gloss is still discussed; Cole 2014 review notes possible scribal change in Mark and John (following Brunner 1947/48, van Bergen 2008) and change of practice in Matthew (Kotake 2012).
