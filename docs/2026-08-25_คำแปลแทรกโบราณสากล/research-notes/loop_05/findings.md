## Key scholarly findings (with sources)

1. Abrogans: The St Gall manuscript Cod. Sang. 911 (8th century; end of 8th c. per BStK palaeography; around 790 per e-codices subproject; 775-790 per historyofinformation.com) is regarded as the oldest surviving book in the German language: a Latin-OHG synonym glossary named after its first entry abrogans = dheomodi "humble". It also preserves the earliest German Paternoster and Credo (p. 320-322). Notably, its German words are written beside rather than between the Latin lines (e-codices/Scherrer). At least three scribes are recognizable. Traditional attribution to the scribe "Kero" persists in the name "Vocabularius Keronis", but Scherrer already noted the hand of the Paternoster/Credo differs from any Kero attribution.
2. Dating tension for the Abrogans archetype: Koebler lists the "Hrabanisch-keronische Sippe" with a queried Freising origin between 740-757, while the surviving St Gall copy is end-8th-century; the classic Bischoff/Duft/Sonderegger facsimile volume (1977, listed by Handschriftencensus) is the key literature still to be consulted directly.
3. Tatian (Cod. Sang. 56): unique witness of an OHG gospel harmony laid out as Latin column left, German column right, in 181 chapters, copied in the first half of the 9th century; seven scribes distinguished by Sievers; language High Frankish (Fulda); described by e-codices as the most comprehensive OHG-era text and "foundation of Old High German grammar". Baesecke (via Persée review) stressed that PARALLEL-column versions differ functionally from INTERLINEAR versions (freer word order, fewer abbreviations).
4. Monsee-Wien fragments: remains (Vienna ONB Cod. 3093* plus two Hannover leaves) of the only surviving early-medieval Latin-German collective manuscript; written at Mondsee ca. 810 or first third of 9th c. in Bavarian dialect; contains OHG translations of Matthew, homilies, and Isidore's De fide catholica; arrangement places Latin on versos and corresponding OHG on rectos (Handschriftencensus). Hench edited them in 1890; the Vienna DFG project produced the first integrated manuscript-near edition.
5. OHG Isidore: the Paris manuscript plus Monsee fragments carry the OHG translation of Isidore's De fide catholica, belonging to the oldest layer of German textual transmission at the end of the 8th century (Universitaet Wien publication page).
6. Reichenau/Murbach glosses: Bodleian Junius 25 preserves 27 Latin hymns with a complete OHG interlinear version (Reichenauer/Murbacher Hymnen) written in the first quarter of the 9th century partly at Reichenau, partly at Murbach (Handschriftencensus 17901); recent edition review reports the conclusion that the German is not a direct translation of the transmitted Latin text; Oxford research models the glosses as a teaching aid operating on three levels (lemma-syntactic-visual).
7. Role of glosses in emergence of written German: per the Zurich Ad fontes tutorial, roughly two thirds of the known OHG vocabulary is transmitted only through glosses (>1,300 glossed manuscripts, ~230,000 attestations in the Bergmann/Stricker catalogue); interlinear versions constitute a word-for-word "raw translation" stage between isolated glosses and free translation; the practice was stimulated from the Anglo-Saxon mission area. This makes gloss corpora the single largest source for the earliest written German.
8. Psalter interlinear versions in OHG territory: verified examples include the Old Alemannic Psalter fragments (chapter in De Gruyter 2017 volume), Notker's Psalter (Cod. Sang. 21, freer rendering per Ad fontes), Windberg (12th c.) and Trier (13th c.) interlinear psalters (Graff 1839, archive.org/DTA). The famous label "Psalterium Foebironense" could not be securely tied to a specific shelfmark from fetched sources - yet to be confirmed.
