## Key scholarly findings (with sources)
1. Definition: gugyeol/kugyeol 口訣 is a family of annotation systems for rendering Classical Chinese into understandable Korean; attested first in Buddhist texts, flourishing in Joseon when readings of the classics were socially paramount (Cambridge Handbook summary; Language Log).
2. Typology: eumdok gugyeol 音讀口訣 (Sino-Korean readings inserted particles), sundok gugyeol 順讀口訣 (sequential reading aids), seokdok gugyeol 釋讀口訣 (interpretive: native-equivalent substitutions + word-order change), yeokdok gugyeol 逆讀口訣 (inverted order variant); complex sign inventories include inked and blind/stylus dots-and-strokes (Language Log, Park comment; Cambridge chapter keywords).
3. Chronology and canon: kugyeol appears to date from the Silla period (Cambridge summary); attributed traditionally to Sol Ch'ong (UPenn); major surviving witnesses are Buddhist sutras (e.g., Happu Kŭmgangmyŏnggyŏng vol. 3 sŏkdok kugyŏl studied by Chung Jaeyoung 1998; Inwang-gyŏng by Kim Tu-ch'an 1997 — reference list of Cambridge chapter), and a gugyeol-marked woodblock print of the Yogacarabhumi-sastra is displayed at the National Hangeul Museum (Language Log).
4. Diachronic shift: transition from interpretive (seokdok) glossing at end of Koryo to sequential (sundok) glossing in Choson, then after 1446 invention of hangul, two-dimensional practice adding vernacular glosses in the left margin (King 2013 abstract; Language Log); full vernacular translation (eonhae 諺解) took over the translational function while gugyeol simplified into reading aids.
5. Relation to Japanese kunten: Whitman treats kugyeol and kunten together as instances of universal "vernacular reading" glossing accompanying script borrowing (Ubiquity of the Gloss); Kobayashi Yoshinori 2002 directly compared Korean kakuhitsu (stylus) marks with Japanese old kunten (cited in Cambridge reference list); Tsutsumi et al. 2023 note Korean digital methods use an equivalent but incompatible coordinate grid (F1000 article).
6. Sociolinguistic value: kugyeol functioned not only as parsing aid but as instrument of eloquence, memory, recitation, and textual adornment (UPenn colloquium abstract); Mair stresses it is oralization rather than translation (Language Log).
7. Khitan contrast case: instead of interlinear reading marks, the Liao Khitan developed two full scripts — large script imitating/borrowing Chinese characters (created 920), small script phonetic block system with Uyghur-influenced origins (~925) — both coexisting until proscription in 1191-1192; no dictionaries/glossaries survive and only one manuscript codex is known (BabelStone/West; Zaytsev addendum).
8. Pre-hangul system context: idu, hyangchal, gugyeol constitute the three ancient Korean systems of writing Korean through borrowed Chinese characters (search results incl. Britannica hyangchal entry snippet, FutureLearn step, accesson.kr Korea Journal article v.50 n.2 — full text not retrievable this pass).
