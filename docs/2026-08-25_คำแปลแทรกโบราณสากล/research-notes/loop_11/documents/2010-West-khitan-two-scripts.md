Content notes:
- Khitan large script 契丹大字: logographic/syllabic script derived from and imitating Chinese characters; several hundred characters; up to 30% of known large characters borrowed directly from Chinese (e.g., 皇帝 "emperor", 囯 "country"); some later Jurchen characters derived from it. Per History of the Liao (遼史 juan 2, 75, 89), created by order of Emperor Taizu with Yelü Tulübu 耶律突呂不 and Yelü Lubugu 耶律魯不古, introduced at the start of year 920.
- Khitan small script 契丹小字: mixed writing system, mostly phonetic elements joined in rectangular phonographic blocks representing word pronunciation, plus a small number of logographs for frequent vocabulary (numbers, cardinal directions); phonetic elements appear derived from Chinese characters, some identical to large-script characters; reputedly devised four or five years later by Yelü Diela 耶律迭剌, influenced by the Uyghur script.
- Corpus: about fifty known monumental inscriptions (~17 large script, ~33 small script); no dated inscriptions near 920; earliest dated 986 (large) and 1053 (small); the two scripts coexisted for ~200 years and are never found together on the same monument/artefact; both continued into Jin dynasty until proscription of Khitan by the Jurchen court in 1191-1192.
- Hypotheses examined for why two scripts existed (chronological, geographic, functional, social, linguistic usage) — all found unsupported in the extant corpus.
- No contemporary dictionaries or glossaries survive for Khitan, unlike Tangut (Khara-Khoto finds), which is why Khitan remains poorly understood.
- Addendum A [2011-10-15]: Viacheslav Zaytsev (Institute of Oriental Manuscripts, St. Petersburg) identified a 100+ page cursive manuscript codex as Large Khitan script — announced 2010-11-29 — the first and only manuscript identified in either Khitan script.

Comparative relevance for interlinear-gloss project: Khitan shows a different strategy for adapting Sinographic writing to a non-Sinitic language (full indigenous scripts rather than interlinear reading marks), while its logographic borrowing from Chinese parallels Korean/Japanese adaptations; useful as contrast case against kunten/kugyeol annotation systems.
