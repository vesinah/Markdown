## Key academic findings

1. **Syriac 'Masora' as gloss infrastructure**: East Syriac schools transmitted companion booklets (kurrasa of shmahe) whose core curriculum consisted of excerpted Peshitta sample texts carrying marginal reminders on reading and interpretation; BL Add. 12138 (dated 899 CE) is the model exemplar, containing vowel marks, phonological marks, notes on schools, and exegetical glosses (Loopstra; URLs above).

2. **Interpretive glosses inside biblical manuscripts**: Peshitta-Daniel manuscripts embed paragraph-heading glosses that impose a specific historical exegesis (four empires = Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece; little horn = Antiochus Epiphanes), reflecting Porphyry-influenced early Syriac Christianity; chapter 11 glosses appear only from the 10th century onward, showing accretive gloss growth over time (Taylor; URL above).

3. **School-based exegesis pipeline**: Edessa (closed 489) -> Nisibis institutionalized the exegete (mpashqana) as head; Q&A scholia (Theodore bar Koni 792) and abbreviated commentaries (Isho'dad of Merv ca. 850, dependence on Theodore approx. 30%) represent the shift from oral school teaching to written annotation (Becker, Morrison, Leonhard, Scielo; URLs above).

4. **Persian interlinear Qur'an**: Tarjuma-yi Tafsir-i Tabari (under Samanid Mansur b. Nuh, r. 961-76 narrative; work itself late 10th-early 11th cent.) places a word-for-word Persian equivalent beneath each Arabic word in original order; terminology inverted: tarjuma = exegesis (92 occurrences) while the interlinear rendering is called tafsir; interlinear form driven by doctrine of i'jaz (inimitability) requiring preservation of Arabic nazm (word order) (Tahmasebian and Gould 2024, CC-BY full text saved).

5. **Scale of interlinear practice**: Al-Furqan survey documented 4,000+ manuscript Qur'an translation copies in 30 languages/37 countries; literal translations are characteristically interlinear, in different script style or red ink; multi-layer codices exist combining interlinear translation(s), short translations, and margin tafsirs; a claimed 308/939-40 interlinear Persian fragment exists but its Ibn Muqlah attribution is rejected (Ihsanoglu, full text saved).

6. **Judeo-Arabic tafsir layout**: Saadya Gaon's Pentateuch tafsir circulated in layouts where each Arabic verse follows the Hebrew verse (or Hebrew lead-word marked by colons/dots), and Genizah triglot fragments align Hebrew + Onkelos-Aramaic + Judaeo-Arabic column-by-column — a functional interlinear/tri-gloss format for scripture study (Zewi COMSt Bulletin; Penn CAJS RAR MS 85.126.39 OPenn record https://openn.library.upenn.edu/Data/0002/html/85_126_39.html).

7. **Garshuni as script-switching annotation medium**: Writing Arabic in Syriac script from at least the 14th century; scribes described it as transcription (naskh), sometimes even 'the Garshuni language'; motivations included readability for monks educated in Syriac script and communal identity; marginal notes freely mix Syriac, Arabic, and Garshuni within single manuscripts (McCollum/HMML blog posts, Bertozzi; URLs above).

8. **Glossing tied to recitation accuracy**: The Syriac Masora manuscripts explicitly aim to support accurate recitation and transmission of the Peshitta according to East Syriac school tradition — parallel to Jewish Masora function (Loopstra; URL above).
