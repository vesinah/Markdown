## Key content extracted (full-text notes)

1. Early period: Syriac translations of the Greek Gospels introduced (or preserved) interpretations no longer found in the Greek text; e.g., Tatian's Diatessaron (2nd cent.) reports John the Baptist consumed milk and honey (not locusts), linking his diet to Deut. 6:3; the Old Syriac Gospels report Barabbas was imprisoned for murder and heresy (Luke 23:25 Curetonian).

2. Aphrahat (337-45): composed 23 'Demonstrations' relying heavily on the Bible (cites NT approx. 700 times); reads the Bible as an integral book through 'exemplary sequences'; Fifth Demonstration ('On Wars') is an exegesis of Dan. 2, 7, 8. Quote: 'it is not sufficient that we learn to read the books of God [i.e., the Bible], rather, we must do them!' (Dem. 14, §32).

3. Ephrem: first running Syriac commentary is Ephrem's 'Commentary on the Diatessaron' (4th cent.). He distinguishes between the factual (historical) and spiritual interpretations. Events in the OT prefigure NT events; terms raza 'symbol/mystery' and tupsa 'type' are used; raze drawn from nature prompt contemplation of the divine.

4. Theodore of Mopsuestia (d. 428): works translated into Syriac by mid-5th cent.; opposed allegorical interpretation in favor of a literal sense respecting historicity; his 'Commentary on the Gospel of John' survives in Syriac.

5. School history: Emperor Zeno closed the School of Edessa in 489 for adherence to Theodore's theology; reestablished at Nisibis under Narsai (d. ca. 500). This led to distinct East- and West-Syrian exegetical traditions.

6. Narsai wrote over three hundred verse homilies (memre); Ya'qub of Serugh (d. 521) composed memre in couplets of 7+7 or 12+12 syllables; used intertextual typological approach (example: David's stone as Christ, Memra 34).

7. East-Syrian tradition: Theodoros bar Koni (8th cent.), teacher at Kashkar, wrote the 'Book of the Scholion' (792), comprising 11 memre, four on the NT, in question-and-answer format (a format well-known in E.-Syr. exegesis; Griffith 1982, 59). Explicitly typological hermeneutics; quotes John Chrysostom and Origen.

8. Isho'dad of Merv (mid-9th cent.): commentaries on most of the NT, integrating Ephrem, Basil of Caesarea, Cappadocians, John Chrysostom and unknown sources; adhered to Theodore of Mopsuestia's approach.

9. Gannat Bussame: anonymous lengthy commentary compiling E.-Syr. exegetical traditions according to the liturgical cycle; date disputed, possibly as early as 10th cent.; several E.-Syr. exegetes known only through quotations in this work.

10. West-Syrian tradition: Philoxenos of Mabbug (d. 523) initiated a new translation of the Greek NT into Syriac (Philoxenian version, fragments survive) because he viewed the Peshitta as unsatisfactory for theological argumentation; Mushe bar Kipho (d. 903) commented on the entire Bible; Dionysios bar Salibi (d. 1171) wrote commentaries on the entire NT, main source possibly Isho'dad of Merv; Bar 'Ebroyo completed 'Storehouse of Mysteries' in 1277.

11. Revision history shows gloss-like mirroring: Tumo of Harqel revised the Philoxenian version (AD 616), the Harqlean version, 'mirroring the Greek text in Syriac at the expense of good Syriac idiom'.

## Relevance to interlinear gloss research

- Demonstrates the layered annotation ecosystem around the Peshitta: sample-text booklets (Syriac Masora), scholia (Q&A format), marginal reading notes, and running commentaries form a continuum between gloss and commentary.
- The Harqlean 'mirror translation' practice parallels interlinear cribs in function: aligning a target rendering word-by-word with a source text.
