Content notes:
- Two explanations for origin of Tibetan writing: (a) evolution from an easy-to-write "Pungyig" (spungs yig) script of Persia appearing in Zhangzhung, whose letters gradually became the "headed" (dbu can) script; when dbu can was written quickly, the "headless" (dbu med) letter forms emerged; (b) the traditional Thonmi Sambhota story: sent by Songtsen Gampo to India in the 7th c., studied scripts with Lije and Lharigpai Senge, created Tibetan letter forms and grammar (sum cu pa, rtags 'jug).
- Letter-form history: during Trisong Detsen's reign the writing expert Khepa Khyungpo Yutri and translator Denma Tsemang established proportions of dbu can and dbu med; two systems known as Lilug (li lugs) and Denlug (ldan lugs), later merged.
- Three historical orthography revisions (dag cha): (1) beginning of Trisong Detsen's period - translators Kawa Paltseg, Chogro Lui Gyaltsen, Zhang Yeshe De revised proper nouns/technical terms of earlier translations; (2) mid-9th c. under Tri Ralpachen - revision of old prefixed/suffixed letters to accord with contemporary language; (3) late 10th c. under Lha Lama Yeshe O with Rinchen Zangpo - further revision; original decrees still extant per the essay.
- Script statistics given: 41 basic forms (4 vowels, 30 consonants, plus attachments); with superfixed/subfixed letters counted separately: 4 vowels, 30 consonants, 37 superfixed, 34 subfixed; adding header (mgo), dot (tsheg), vertical (shad): exactly 108 forms.
- Notes Tibetan script used to transcribe other languages historically: Chinese, Indian, Kashmiri, Sogdian, Uighur, Persian.
- Pillar inscriptions and Dunhuang writings cited as exemplars of the old script; pillar edict before the Potala (mid-8th c.) still legible.

Relevance: primary Tibetan-language historiography (translated) on dbu can vs dbu med development and the state-led orthographic revisions contemporaneous with the Mahavyutpatti-era terminology standardization.
