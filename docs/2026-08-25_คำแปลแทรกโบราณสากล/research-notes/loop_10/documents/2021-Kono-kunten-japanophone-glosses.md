Abstract (verbatim key passages):
- Premodern Japanese books include many written in Classical Chinese (kanbun) as well as wabun; Chinese and Buddhist classics occupied a prominent place.
- Learned people in ancient Japan developed a system for graphically assigning Chinese characters/vocabulary to specific native Japanese words, creating a script suitable for recording Japanese; around the 9th century kana was derived from Chinese characters to write Japanese phonetically; the modern mixed kanji-kana writing is a direct result of this process.
- "Known collectively as kanbun kundoku - the Japanophone gloss-reading of Sinographic text - this practice was born together with, and has persisted throughout, the history of Japanese language, learning, and culture."
- Kono examines documents of the Japanophone gloss-pointing method known as kunten, focusing on how media shifts (manuscript to print, now print to digital) changed page layouts conveying gloss-borne information, and how these changes influenced learning habits and knowledge.
- She argues that because of its adoption across successive media, kanbun kundoku with kunten glossing has relevance beyond East Asia and can open a new perspective for world philology.
