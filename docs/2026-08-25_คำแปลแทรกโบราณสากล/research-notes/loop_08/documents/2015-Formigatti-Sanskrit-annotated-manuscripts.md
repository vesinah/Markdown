## Extracted content (from fetched abstract pages and retrieved PDF)

### Project aims (verbatim key passages)

'A virtually unstudied aspect of variance in Sanskrit manuscripts is the presence or not, and form and content, if present, of marginal and/or interlinear annotation to the text copied. Such annotation can, however, in some cases provide us with as close as we can get to direct access to the thought processes of active participants in premodern Sanskritic cultures as they go about the business of producing, studying, and interpreting texts.'

From DFG record: 'This annotation can in some cases capture the moment of change from an oral to a written transmission, with a scribe who is simultaneously a student of a text setting down oral explanations of it by a teacher.' Also: study will clarify 'the process by which originally marginal material is added into the main text in subsequent copies'.

### Texts studied

(1) Abhinavagupta's Isvarapratyabhijnavimarsini; (2) Kalidasa's Raghuvamsha; (3) Dandin's Kavyadarsha; additionally Utpaladeva's Isvarapratyabhijnakarika, Abhinavagupta's Tantrasara. Materials: birch bark, palm leaf, paper; scripts: Sharada, Newari, Devanagari, Bengali.

### Key findings

1. Only the Kashmiri birch-bark and paper manuscripts are codex format with more space per page than palm-leaf manuscripts (e.g., the Kavyadarsha manuscript); their layout permits numerous interlinear annotations, unlike palm leaf.
2. Most annotations in the Isvarapratyabhijnavimarsini and one Raghuvamsha manuscript consist of quotations from independent running commentaries (e.g., from Abhinavagupta's Isvarapratyabhijnavrittivimarsini and Vallabhadeva's Raghupancika).
3. Annotations in the IP Vimarsini manuscripts were treated as an independent exegetical apparatus and copied together with the main text from manuscript to manuscript; most plausibly teachers' (or learned readers') own notes intended as memory aids for teaching; presence of typical copying errors indicates written rather than freshly-noted oral transmission.
4. Almost all Raghuvamsha manuscripts appear written for and used in teaching; one Nepalese Raghuvamsha manuscript carries annotations constituting an anonymous short running commentary written entirely in the margins.
5. Nearly all annotations can be assigned to the five purposes that Sanskrit tradition assigns to a running commentary: (1) word division (padaccheda); (2) syntactic analysis; (3) resolution of compounds (vigraha); (4) glossing word meanings; (5) explanation of content. IP Vimarsini annotations mostly categories 3, 4, 5; Raghuvamsha mostly categories 3 and 4; some Raghuvamsha manuscripts mark word division and syntax by supra- and sub-linear reading aids instead.
6. Clear regional differences in graphic design and content of annotations; differences also follow the literary genre of the root text.
7. Terminology adopted (citing E. Dickey 2007): 'scholium' reserved for longer exegetical explanations, 'gloss' for shorter ones explaining only a word or short expression; 'commentary' only for self-standing texts (hypomnemata), which may be transmitted above/below the basic text as tripatha 'threefold reading' commentaries or on all four margins as pancapatha 'fivefold reading' commentaries; 'annotation' used as generic term covering glosses and scholia.

## Relevance to interlinear gloss research

- Provides first systematic typology of marginal/interlinear annotation in Sanskrit manuscripts; demonstrates annotation-commentary continuum: marginal apparatus can be excerpted commentary transmitted alongside root text.
- Documents layout determinism: writing material/format governs whether interlinear glossing is possible (codex vs pothi).
- Captures classroom use: learner copies with glosses, supra/sublinear reading marks, sandhi markings.
