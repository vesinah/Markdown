## Key findings (with URLs)

1. **Huilin's Yiqiejing yinyi structure confirmed from primary source** (T2128 prefaces, saved extract): 100 juan (Gu Qizhi: 經音義一百卷，約六十萬言 "one hundred juan, about 600,000 words"; Jing Shen: 凡一百軸 "in all one hundred scrolls"); begun late Jianzhong era (c. 783), completed Yuanhe 2 = **807 CE**, final brush 817 at Ximingsi; scope "from the Great Prajñāpāramitā to the Hīnayāna records and biographies", i.e. 1,300 titles / 5,700+ juan of the canon; explicitly built by merging the two earlier sound-gloss traditions (Xuanying's 25-juan Yiqiejing yinyi and Huiyuan's 2-juan New-translation Huayan yinyi). Source: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cbeta-git/xml-p5a/master/T/T54/T54n2128.xml

2. **Phonological method**: Huilin took his fanqie standards from Yuan Tingjian's Yunying 韻英 (Qin/northwestern pronunciation) and Zhang Kaosheng's Qieyun, rejecting inherited fanqie practice where "ancient sound-reversals mostly used pang-niu as shuang-sheng" and Wu vs Qin tone values were confused; preface notes regional tone drift ("Qin people's departing tone resembles rising; Wu people's rising tone resembles departing"). Meaning glosses standardized on seven dictionaries (Yupian, Shuowen, Zilin, Zhitong, Gujin zhengzi, Wenzi dianshuo, Kaiyuan wenzi yinyi). Same source as above.

3. **fanqie visible inside actual entries** (Li 2022 examples; T2128 entry samples): entries give headword + 反切 formula (e.g. 卓犖 力角反; 儀 魚羈反) followed by meaning citations from Zuozhuan/Shuowen/Guangya and script-form discussions - the operational anatomy of a sound-gloss entry.

4. **Xuanying's Yiqiejingyinyi** (Li 2022/2023): oldest extant Buddhist dictionary in China; 25 juan, ~400,000 characters, >450 scriptures, >9,000 entries; produced inside Xuanzang's Chang'an translation bureau (yichang); Xuanying appears there as the team philologist also in Keyworth 2020 Hualin p. 278 ("the 'philologist' Xuanying 玄應 (d. 661: composed Da Tang zhongjing yinyi 大唐衆經音義)") - cross-verified across independent sources.

5. **Sound-gloss <-> interlinear-format link** (Li 2022, decisive for this project): manuscript copies of the Yinyi circulate in two layout types, one being the "small characters in two lines" (小字双行) double-column small-character gloss format - the same physical convention later regularized in printed zhushu/jiazhu editions (loop_09 luatex-cn spec). Layout variants themselves serve as stemmatic evidence, sorting the ten Japanese lineages into three groups. Additionally Ishizuka & Ikeda 1991 studied the Leningrad Dunhuang-provenance manuscript Ф230 of the Yinyi (cited in both Li papers).

6. **Print-vs-manuscript divergence** (Li 2023): Song woodblock canon copies (Sixi edition = Fuzhou/Tōzenji lineage) transmit a different textual family than Korean canons; manuscript lineages (Kongōji Kamakura copies, Hōryūji/Nanatsudera/Ishiyamadera Heian copies etc.) each have unique copying styles. The Yinyi thus documents gloss-transmission as an actively evolving manuscript tradition before print standardization.

7. **Keyworth 2020 chapter verified bibliographically only**: exact title "Glosses in Chinese and Japanese on Manuscript editions of Yijing's Translation of the Suvarṇabhāsottama-sūtra from Dunhuang and Japan", book-chapter, Dec. 2020, in *Production and Preservation of Buddhist Manuscripts in Central and East Asia* (author-asserted ORCID record https://pub.orcid.org/v3.0/0000-0001-7352-6423/works, put-code 110186258). No DOI, no indexed abstract, no accessible full text found (OpenAlex, Crossref, Bing exact-phrase, complete Hualin IJBS issue index 2018-2025 all checked and negative). Its content therefore remains unverified here; closest accessible proxy remains Keyworth's 2020 Hualin article (archived loop_09/documents/) which discusses glosses via Huilin's Yinyi and Dunhuang/Japanese manuscript canons, plus his 2021 SCR article on kengyō scripture-reading practice (https://doi.org/10.1080/23729988.2021.1996973).

8. **Transmission network**: Korea mediated Huilin's Yinyi back into China (Sin Wŏnch'ŏl 2024, metadata only: https://doi.org/10.1515/9783111322599-003); Japan copied Xuanying's Yinyi intensively from the Nara period onward and reused it for dictionaries (Maejima 2019: Shinzui's Jōdosanbukyō-ongi-shū; Fujita 2019: Qieyun material in Shinsen-jikyō, https://doi.org/10.20666/nihongonokenkyu.15.1_18).
