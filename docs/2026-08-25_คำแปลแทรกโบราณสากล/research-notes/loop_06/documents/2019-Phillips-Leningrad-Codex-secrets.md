# Learn the secrets of the Leningrad Codex

- Author: Kim Phillips (Research Fellow, Institute for Hebrew Bible Manuscript Research)
- Published: Tyndale House, Cambridge, September 6, 2019
- URL: https://tyndalehouse.com/2019/09/06/learn-the-secrets-of-the-leningrad-codex/

## Key content (full argumentative text preserved)

- Samuel ben Jacob completed the manuscript now known as the Leningrad Codex ("L") in AD 1008-1009 at Fustat (Cairo); identified via colophonic notes about who wrote it, when, where, for whom.
- L is the earliest COMPLETE manuscript of the Hebrew Bible (all 24 books); hundreds of earlier partial manuscripts exist; L lies behind translations such as NIV and ESV; one of the best examples of the Masoretic Text preserved by Masoretes ("traditionalists") active from about the 7th century AD onwards.
- Four layers on a typical page: (1) Masoretic notes surrounding the text - long notes above/below = Masora Magna; shorter notes between columns, often single-letter abbreviations = Masora Parva; (2) vowel signs designed to preserve the transmitted reading tradition; (3) consonantal base text; (4) accents guiding pronunciation, sentence/clause division and chanting.
- Originally each strand was transmitted separately; only consonants were written. The consonantal text was preserved virtually flawlessly for over a millennium. Reading consonant-only text requires an oral reading tradition developed centuries before Jesus and passed on orally until the Masoretes recorded it with vowel and accent signs.
- Guard-the-text mechanism by counting: first column of L, Gen 1:3 "And God said" has marginal note "29" = 29 places in the whole Hebrew OT where this form of "say" is followed by Elohim; danger of confusion with the >200 occurrences of "and the LORD said" (600+ with similar phrases); scribes compiled complete lists to guard against corruption.
- Citation system before chapters/verses: verses identified by short quotation snippets; example: finding the verse with "and God said" + "do you do well to..." = Jonah 4:9.
- Integrated system: Masora Parva attaches to passages liable to corruption and often points to Masora Magna, which gives writing instructions and links to every other occurrence, enabling rapid cross-checking of every occurrence of a textual detail.
- The Leningrad Codex contains no fewer than 60,000 Masoretic notes forming "a protective hedge around the text".
