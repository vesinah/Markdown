## Key content (full text preserved)

### Definition of Masorah

The term "Masorah", as used in modern-day scientific research, refers to any kind of metatextual element next to the consonantal text of the Hebrew Bible. It first appeared in comprehensive form in the great oriental Bible codices: Codex Cairensis (895 CE), Codex Babylonicus Petropolitanus (St Petersburg, RNL Evr. I B 3), the Aleppo Codex, and the Codex Leningradensis (Evr. I B 19a). The Masorah comprises mise-en-page and mise-en-texte, graphemes, grammatical, syntactic and statistical notes, as well as references and links, characteristic of Bible codices and also Torah scrolls used to this day in synagogue liturgy.

Distinctions: perpendicular Masorah on side margins (masora parva); horizontal Masorah on lower and upper margins (masora magna) or at the very end of a book (masora finalis).

### Transmission and recensions

Complete Masoretic Bibles and isolated Masoretic lists circulate since the ninth century (e.g., Okhla we-Okhla; Sefer ha-Hillufim). From the eleventh century they were continuously taken into account in Spanish-Provencal and Northern French-Ashkenazi Bible commentaries, Halakhic-liturgical works, and Hebrew-French glossaries. In medieval Ashkenaz emerged a distinct Masoretic subversion of the Tiberian Masorah deserving recognition as an independent textual type due to variant readings, idiosyncrasies, and reception history; its layout differs as masora figurata.

### Masora figurata

From the thirteenth century, Bibles appear in France and Germany where masora magna and list material are organized as decorative ornaments in micrographic writing given pictorial form (legendary creatures, chimeras, dragons, drolleries, zoomorphic figures, anthropomorphic beings), similar to Romanesque illumination programs; sometimes diagrammatic geometric representations. Contrary to previous claims, figurative Masorah did not necessarily lose philological quality; many lists relegated to last pages in oriental codices, often illegible/untraceable there, are presented in full on folio pages. Masora figurata occasionally contains quotations from commentary literature and Midrashim going beyond usual biblical commentary, e.g., MS Vat. ebr. 14.

### Project aims and methods

Reconstruction of integration of oriental knowledge traditions into Ashkenazi rabbinic scholarly culture; reappraisal of Western European Masoretic traditions between 12th and 14th centuries; digital edition of the Masorah of more than 15 Ashkenazi manuscripts in workspace BIMA; IIIF manuscript repository with Heidelberg University Library; SVG/XML encoding of figurative Masorah; Neo4J graph database; TEI-XML exports.
