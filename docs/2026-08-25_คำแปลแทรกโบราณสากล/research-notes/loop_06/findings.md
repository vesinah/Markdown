## Key scholarly findings (with sources)

1. Function of Masorah = guard-the-text system: Masoretic notes preserve minute details of the biblical text by counting occurrences; the Leningrad Codex alone carries about 60,000 notes forming a protective hedge around the text; Mp flags corruption-prone passages, Mm expands with lists locating every occurrence via verse snippets because chapter/verse numbering did not yet exist (Phillips/Tyndale House 2019; Mynatt/AJR 2020).
2. Circulus/circule referencing: a small circle placed over a word directs the reader to the matching marginal Masoretic note; Mp notes often point forward to numbered Mm lists (e.g., superscript leading to Massorah Gedolah list 784) (Mynatt/AJR 2020). Additionally, over 500 Mp doublet notes in L carry attached catchwords explicitly naming the parallel verse (Marcus 2007), and 38 Aramaic mnemonics substitute or supplement Hebrew simanim in Mm (Marcus 1999).
3. Terminology and typology: masora parva = perpendicular side margins; masora magna = horizontal upper/lower margins; masora finalis = end of book; Masorah first appears comprehensively in the great oriental codices (Codex Cairensis 895, Codex Babylonicus Petropolitanus, Aleppo Codex, Codex Leningradensis); from the 13th century in France/Germany it appears as masora figurata - micrographic ornament (animals, chimeras, drolleries) that contrary to older claims retains full philological value and sometimes adds commentary/Midrash quotations (Corpus Masoreticum project page).
4. Leningrad Codex identity: oldest complete Hebrew Bible manuscript, completed 1008-1009 by Samuel ben Jacob at Fustat (Cairo); colophon claims copying from exemplars corrected by Aaron ben Asher; basis of BHS/BHQ and of translations like NIV/ESV (Phillips 2019; Mynatt 2020).
5. Aaron ben Asher vs ben Naphtali: two rival Tiberian schools ca. 890-960; about 875 recorded differences, mostly accent placement; the Aleppo Codex colophon names Shlomo ben Buya'a (text) and Aharon ben Asher (vocalization/Masora); A follows Ben Asher against Ben Naftali in 94% of Kitab al-Khilaf cases and is identified with the codex Maimonides trusted in Egypt; whether Ben Asher was a Karaite remains debated (Klar/Dotan/Zer) (Khan 2020 OAPEN; Jewish Encyclopedia s.vv. Ben Asher, Ben Naphtali; Zer HUJI PDF).
6. Cairo Codex of the Prophets: colophon claims Moses ben Asher wrote/vocalized it in Tiberias in 894/5 CE with MP and MM (some geometrically arranged) and carpet pages; however radiocarbon dating and palaeographic analysis now favour an 11th-century date and reject the Moses ben Asher identification; its masoretic tradition sits closer to ben Naphtali than Aaron ben Asher (CSIC digital edition; Posen Library; Outhwaite 2021; HUBP note).
7. Ginsburg's Introduction (1897): freely available scans on archive.org; documents his Massoretico-critical edition grounded in Jacob ben Chayim's Bomberg recension (Venice 1524-25), with all variants relegated to the margin and restoration of ancient Sedarim divisions (archive.org preface/djvu text).
8. Targum Onkelos dating and liturgical use: rabbinic tradition (b. Megillah 3a) credits Onkelos the proselyte under R. Eliezer/R. Joshua (~130 CE), widely suspected of conflation with Aquila's Greek version; synagogue reading custom is first attested for the Usha period (mid-late 2nd c.) based on m. Megillah 4; performance rules required a translator distinct from the reader, upright posture, and oral delivery (written Targum banned in service as Oral Torah); "our targum" was authorized in Babylonia by the 6th c.; one recent study argues composition/redaction closer to ~400 CE from reliance on finalized tannaitic midrashim; the Liturgical Targum of mahzorim preserves a Palestinian recension progressively "Onqelosized" (Verrijssen/Brill; Seforim Blog 2015; Posen Library; Smelik/OBP).

## Relationship Masorah - gloss - commentary

The Masorah is defined by current scholarship precisely as a metatextual apparatus beside the base text (counting, grammar, statistics, cross-references), i.e., structurally analogous to glossing traditions studied in Loops 01-05; Corpus Masoreticum further traces its reception into 11th-century Spanish-Provencal and Ashkenazi Bible commentaries and Hebrew-French glossaries, showing continuity between masoretic lists, glossaries, and commentary.
