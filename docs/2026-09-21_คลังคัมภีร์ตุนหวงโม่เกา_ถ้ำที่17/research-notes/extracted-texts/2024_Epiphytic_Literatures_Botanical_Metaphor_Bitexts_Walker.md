Of Peninsulas and Archipelagos
Comprising 11 countries and hundreds of languages from one of the most cultur-
ally diverse regions in the world, the chapters in this collection explore a wide range 
of translation issues.
The subject of this volume is set in the contrasted landscapes of mainland 
peninsulas and maritime archipelagos in Southeast Asia, which, whilst remaining 
a largely minor area in Asian studies, harbors a wealth of textual heritage that 
opens to inquiries and new readings. From the post- Angkor Cambodia, the post- 
colonial Viantiane, to the ultra- modern Singapore metropolis, translation ﬁgures 
problematically in the modernization of indigenous literatures, criss- crossing 
chronologically and spatially through different literary landscapes. The peninsular 
geo- body gives rise to the politics of singularity as seen in the case of the predom-
inant monolingual culture in Thailand, whereas the archipelagic geography such 
as the thousand islands of Indonesia allows for peculiar types of communication. 
Translation can also be metaphorized poetically to conﬁgure the transference in 
different scenarios such as the cases of self- translation in Philippine protest poetry 
and untranslatability in Vietnamese diasporic writings. The collection also includes 
intra- regional comparative views on historical and religious terms.
This book will appeal to scholars and postgraduate students of translation 
studies, sociolinguistics, and Southeast Asian studies.
Phrae Chittiphalangsri has a PhD in Comparative Literature/ Translation Studies 
from University College London, UK. She is Chairperson for the MA program 
in Translation at the Chalermprakiat Center of Translation and Interpretation 
(CCTI), Faculty of Arts, Chulalongkorn University, Thailand, and has acted as co- 
Vice President of the International Association for Translation and Intercultural 
Studies (IATIS) since 2021.
Vicente L. Rafael has a PhD in History from Cornell University. He is the Giovanni 
and Amne Costigan Endowed Professor of History at the University of Washington, 
USA. Among his several notable books are Contracting Colonialism: Translation 
and Christian Conversion in Tagalog Society Under Early Spanish Rule (1988), The 
Promise of the Foreign: Nationalism and the Technics of Translation in the Spanish 
Philippines (2005), and Motherless Tongues: The Insurgency of Language amid 
Wars of Translation (2016).

A Botanical Metaphor for

Trent Walker
Abstract
Theravada cultures in mainland Southeast Asia have long cultivated distinct 
bilingual approaches to translation. Indic-vernacular bitexts, which typic-
ally combine portions from a Pali source together with their translation into 
a Southeast Asian language, dominated the region’s religious, technical, and 
aesthetic writing between the ﬁfteenth and nineteenth centuries. This chapter 
explores a botanical metaphor for such bitextual translations, namely plants 
that are structurally dependent on other plants, including the epiphytes that 
make Southeast Asian forests appear so lush. Examples explored include those 
that best demonstrate the epiphytic dimensions of bitexts across several genres 
in Vietnamese, Shan, Khmer, Lao, and Siamese contexts. These bitexts offer 
more than a mere translation of their Indic sources; like vines, orchids, or even 
strangler ﬁgs they transform their hosts, whether through processes of abbre-
viation, expansion, reinterpretation, exegesis, or versiﬁcation. These complex 
interactions between Pali hosts and vernacular epiphytes have the capacity to 
metonymically evoke the distinct historical landscape of how Indic-language 
scriptures came to be adopted in Southeast Asia.

a group of aggressive, structurally dependent plants, typically described as

to take over the mind. In the landscape of the Gangetic plain inhabited by 
the Buddha and successive generations of his disciples, massive strangler ﬁgs 
loomed large, both literally and ﬁguratively. Since the Pali Tipiṭaka describes 
Siddhattha Gotama’s awakening as occurring under a bodhi tree (assattha; 
Ficus religiosa), Buddhists have long held that particular variety of strangler 
ﬁg as sacred. Other trees with encircling tendencies and massive aerial root 
systems that play central roles in South Asian Buddhist literature include the 
banyan (nigrodha; Ficus benghalensis) and the cluster ﬁg (udumbara; Ficus 
racemosa) (Dhammika, 2015, pp. 45, 107).
When Buddhist texts and practices came to be adopted in mainland 
Southeast Asia in the early ﬁrst millennium, they entered a biome where stran-
gler ﬁgs were as prominent as in India. By the middle of the second millennium, 
most of the opportunistic plants mentioned in the “Tree sermon” would have 
been familiar to Southeast Asian Buddhists. Many strangler ﬁgs are described 
by botanists today as “hemiepiphitic”, in that they often begin their lives in 
another tree as epiphytes (plants that grow on other plants rather than from 
the soil) before growing down to the ground, often engulﬁng their host in the 
process (Zotz, 2013, p. 1015). Southeast Asian evergreen forests and, to a lesser 
extent, the region’s drier deciduous forests, are also home to true epiphytes, 
plants that spend their entire lives growing on other plants, such as certain 
varieties of orchids, ferns, and moss. Other structurally dependent plants that 
shape the Southeast Asian landscape include parasitic mistletoes and various 
vines. Our appreciation of the might and beauty of the region’s forests grows 
when we take the relationships between these structurally dependent plants 
and their hosts into account.
In this chapter, I explore the possibilities of an epiphytic metaphor for bilin-
gual Indic- vernacular translations in mainland Southeast Asia. I use the term 
“epiphytic” in its broadest possible sense, referring to the full range of struc-
turally dependent ﬂora, including both true epiphytes and hemiepiphytes. In 
short, I use the image of plants growing on other plants as a metaphor for the 
way texts may grow on other texts through a distinctly Southeast Asian pro-
cess of translation. Most of the translations in Theravada Buddhist cultures in 
mainland Southeast Asian from roughly 1450 to 1950 are bilingual, consisting 
of portions in an Indic language, usually Pali (but rarely also Sanskrit), along 
with portions in a local vernacular, such as Burmese, Khmer, (Tai) Khün, 
Lanna, Lao, (Tai) Lü, Mon, Shan, Siamese/ Thai, and Vietnamese. When 
structured in an interphrasal (phrase- by- phrase) or interlinear (line- by- line) 
format, I call these translations Indic- vernacular bitexts (Walker, 2022). Such 
bitexts feature what we can imagine as an epiphytic relationship between the 
Indic host and the vernacular’s verdant growth.
As Maria Tymoczko shows, translation is always a metonymic process, 
where selected elements of the source text and its cultural context are lifted 
up as representatives of a larger whole (Tymoczko, 1999, p. 55). The cul-
tural power of Theravada bitexts lies in their ability to metonymically recall 
the many acts of translation required in the transmission of Buddhism from

Epiphytic Literatures 57
India to Southeast Asia. Though the reality of each bitextual composition 
is often considerably messier, the genre of bitexts has a simple ideal: a Pali 
source text is interwoven with its translation into a Southeast Asian target lan-
guage. As André Lefevere argues, translation is necessarily a form of rewriting, 
“the hidden motor behind literary evolution and the creation of canons and 
paradigms” (Lefevere, 1992, p. 14). But Southeast Asian bitexts do more than 
rewrite their Indic sources. They also metonymically re- enact the primeval 
moment of translation between a South Asian Buddha and his Southeast 
Asian followers. The authors of bitexts are thus both translators of speciﬁc Pali 
sources and embodiments of a wider archetype. Whenever Theravada bitexts 
are read or performed, the author or the reciter metonymically transforms into 
a paṇḍita, in this case a learned person capable of mediating Indic scriptures 
for a Southeast Asian audience. The epiphytic structure of bitexts, in which the 
vernacular relies on the Pali host for support, makes this metonymic projection 
possible.
A bitext is usually based on an existing or invented Pali text, which serves 
as the “tree” (though in rare instances a bitext maybe based on vernacular 
text instead; see Lammerts, 2018, p. 93). The vernacular portions translate and 
transform this tree through various modes of analysis, gloss, expansion, or even 
elision. In some bitexts, the relationship is similar to that of true epiphytes, in 
that both the Indic and vernacular portions are retained in alternating phrases 
or lines, as orchids or ferns are supported by, but do not take over, their host’s 
trunk or branches. In other examples, the Indic portions are swallowed up 
by the burgeoning vernacular sections, save for some traces here and there, 
particularly at the beginning or end of the composition. Such bitexts recall 
the hemiepiphytic lifecycle of the bodhi, the banyan, and other strangler ﬁgs, 
which begin as epiphytes but eventually take over much of the original host 
tree. For other examples of bitexts, it can be hard to disambiguate which parts 
are native and which are adventitious, like a tangle of lianas or a knot of vines.
Indic- vernacular bitexts are too complex, too speciﬁc, too human to 
be seamlessly described by a single habit of botanical growth. Like most 
metaphors, the utility of the notion of epiphytic literatures lies in its capacity 
to evoke rather than explain. As Tymoczko reminds us, translation itself draws 
its power from selectively evoking cultural signs within and beyond the source 
text (1999, p. 50). Since bitexts are an underappreciated part of Southeast 
Asia’s literary landscape, my application of the term epiphytic highlights the 
aesthetic dimensions of these translations. Just as moss enhances the beauty 
of a wizened tree, so too do the vernacular portions of a bitext heighten our 
perception of the underlying Indic text.
Beyond the aesthetic realm, I see the term epiphytic as reﬁguring our con-
ception of the relationship between Indic hosts and new vernacular growths 
in Southeast Asian literature. More than in any other region, classical- 
vernacular bitexts in mainland Southeast Asia achieved a singular promin-
ence in literature, particularly in Buddhist contexts but also in grammar, the 
law, and belles- lettres. Much of the writing produced by Burmese, Khmer,

Khün, Lanna, Lao, Lü, Mon, and Siamese writers from the ﬁfteenth to 
nineteenth centuries were bitexts in which some or all of the vernacular 
portions were structurally dependent upon Indic passages. This relationship 
is reﬂected in a common local term for Indic- vernacular bitexts, nissaya, a 
Pali word that means “dependence” (from Sanskrit niśraya, ni+ √śri, “to rely 
on”). While the usual interpretation of nissaya in this context means a tool 
upon which a Southeast Asian student can rely in studying an Indic text 
(Okell, 1965, p. 187), we can also read the term as referring to the dependence 
of the translated portions upon a Pali source. Like the complex relationship 
between epiphytes and their hosts, Indic- vernacular bitexts adopt a variety 
of different strategies for managing this structural dependence in translation. 
At their core is a conception of vernacularization in which the classical lan-
guage does not simply disappear through the act of translation but is instead 
broken into its component parts, enriched with new additions, or otherwise 
kept in view.
The very terms “host” and “epiphyte” suggest a different ontology than is 
commonly assumed in the Western dichotomy between original and transla-
tion. The host is not necessarily better than the epiphyte. An epiphyte may 
grow larger than the host, or even render it invisible beneath a mass of leaves 
and vines. Yet the epiphyte usually remains structurally dependent on the host. 
Indeed, the host and the epiphyte cannot be neatly separated at all. The same 
goes for the Pali and vernacular portions of bitexts. Such a description is at 
odds with conventional notions that the original is the superior act of creation, 
that translations stand on their own, and that the original and the translation 
are ultimately separate entities. The bitextual format breaks down these con-
ventional expectations, drawing the host and the epiphyte into an intertwined, 
biological relationship.
The speciﬁc techniques used in the construction of Southeast Asian bitexts 
allow for the vernacular portions to blossom on the basis of Pali supports. 
Interphrasal and interlinear Indic- vernacular bitextual translations follow spe-
ciﬁc genre conventions. Their creation is not random but rather the product 
of rule- bound processes of selection, analysis, and presentation. Selection 
means citing an existing Indic text or inventing a new one. Analysis entails 
ﬁve separate techniques: 1) breaking an Indic phrase into its component parts 
(parsing), 2) adding new Indic words to the Indic passage to clarify its sense 
(ampliﬁcation), 3) reordering both the new and old Indic words to suit ver-
nacular syntax (rearrangement), 4) marking the grammatical features of the 
Indic text with specialized vernacular particles (annotation), and 5) pairing 
each Indic word or phrase with a vernacular equivalent (gloss) (Walker, 2022, 
pp. 274– 276). Presentation involves shaping a bitext for a particular purpose, 
be it philological, exegetical, homiletic, or poetic, by enlarging or curtailing 
particular portions of a text. A philological presentation, for instance, uses 
only enough vernacular to make the Indic portions legible to a skilled reader. 
An exegetical presentation tends to enlarge the Indic portions along with

<!-- Page 11 -->
Epiphytic Literatures 59
copious vernacular exposition. A homiletic presentation often abridges or 
excises most of the Indic portions to focus on creating a ﬂuent vernacular 
narrative. A poetic presentation emphasizes the metrical or lyrical qualities of 
the vernacular portions, separately or in tandem with the corresponding Indic 
passages (Walker, 2020b).
Since I examine these techniques and their history in more detail else-
where (Walker, 2020b, 2022), my focus here is on considering a wide range 
of Southeast Asian bitextual translations in light of the epiphytic metaphor. 
In this piece, I explore the aesthetic and structural entailments of bitexts as 
found in examples from each of the ﬁve countries that are located entirely 
within contemporary mainland Southeast Asia: Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, 
Thailand, and Vietnam. I select these examples both for their geographic 
breadth and for the diverse ways in which they structure the relationship 
between their Pali and vernacular portions. Working in reverse chronological 
order from the mid- twentieth to the late ﬁfteenth centuries, each of the ﬁve 
examples reveals a different possible course for navigating between Indic and 
vernacular worlds. These bitexts take an expansive view in terms of what 
counts as translation. In addition to the expected function of providing ver-
nacular glosses for an Indic source, Theravada bitexts may also 1) abbreviate, 
2) expand upon, 3) reinterpret, 4) expound on, or 5) versify a Pali root text. 
In this chapter I have selected examples from Vietnamese, Shan, Khmer, Lao, 
and Siamese contexts that best demonstrate how each of these ﬁve expansive 
forms of translation work. I argue that the botanical comparison heightens 
both our appreciation of the aesthetic qualities of these translations as 
well as our understanding of their complex and varied structures. For each 
example, I provide a Roman- script transliteration, an English translation, and 
a schematic map to aid in the comparative study of bitexts across the many 
languages and literatures of the region.
Abbreviation: Pali- Vietnamese Narratives of Hộ Tông
The story of Theravada Buddhism among the majority Kinh people of 
Vietnam is quite recent, beginning in 1939 with Hộ Tông’s establishment 
of a temple (Chùa Bửu Quang) in Saigon (McLeod, 2009, p. 70). Before his 
death his 1981, Hộ Tông published a wide range of books in Vietnamese on 
Theravada Buddhism, setting in place the liturgical and doctrinal foundations 
for the community he created. Many of his writings are not direct translations 
of Pali texts but translations or adaptations of existing Khmer and Pali- Khmer 
works by Cambodian Buddhist authors, some of whom Hộ Tông studied with 
throughout the 1930s in Phnom Penh (Walker 2020a). In this process of trans-
lation, he introduced not only Theravada Buddhism but also Pali- vernacular 
bitexts to a new audience of Vietnamese speakers.
The Pali- Vietnamese bitexts I discuss here are drawn from Hộ Tông’s Pháp 
thập độ [The ten perfections], ﬁrst published in 1961 (Hộ Tông, 1961; Thiện

Expansion: A Pali- Shan Discourse by U Paññābhoga
Pali- vernacular bitexts composed by Shan scholars in what is now Myanmar 
provide clear examples of how an underlying Pali source may be expanded 
and transformed through the process of composing a bitextual translation. 
Though Pali- Burmese and Pali- Mon bitexts have been produced for many cen-
turies in Burma (Pruitt, 1994, pp. 17– 23; Grifﬁths et al., 2017, pp. 104– 117), 
formal Pali- Shan bitexts of the type I discuss here are, like the Vietnamese case, 
relatively newcomers. While looser bilingual formats have appeared in Shan 
literature for at least several hundred years, strict Pali- Shan bitexts were ﬁrst 
developed in the mid- twentieth century on the basis of Pali- Burmese nissaya 
(Khur- Yearn, 2012, p. 86). Like their Pali- Burmese and Pali- Mon counterparts, 
these Pali- Shan bitexts incorporate various structural features not witnessed in 
the Vietnamese examples above, including extensive rearrangement and expan-
sion of the underlying Pali source text.

Reinterpretation: Scriptural Justiﬁcation in a Pali- Khmer Legal Text
Though the Vietnamese and Shan examples discussed above are the work of 
pioneering scholar- monks, Pali- vernacular bitextual translations are not limited 
to monastic contexts in mainland Southeast Asia. They are also found in law 
codes, among other ostensibly secular genres, including the traditional legal 
literature of Siam (Baker and Pasuk, 2016, pp. 33– 34) and Burma (Lammerts, 
2018, pp. 90– 102). The legal tradition of Cambodia, parts of which are closely 
connected to that of Siam, features a number of bitextual passages that dem-
onstrate their Khmer authors’ ingenious reinterpretation of Pali scriptures to 
serve the needs of sovereign power.

Versiﬁcation: Meter and Affect in the Oldest Pali- Siamese Bitext
In my ﬁnal example, I move away from legal arguments and scholastic exegesis 
to examine the application of bitextual techniques in the composition of aes-
thetically oriented literature, or belles- lettres. The oldest surviving extended 
Pali- Siamese composition is the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ, a bitextual translation 
of the Vessantara- jātaka (the same text explored in my ﬁrst Pali- Vietnamese 
example from Hộ Tông). Similar bitextual presentations are found in nearly 
all of the celebrated Indic- Siamese compositions that include the genre term 
gāṁ hlvaṅ (“royal edition” or “major composition”) in their titles, including 
the eighteenth- century Năndoṕanăndasūt́r gāṁ hlvaṅ (Assanee, 2012). None, 
however, are as old or inﬂuential as the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ, parts of which are 
thought to date from 1482 (Niyaḥtā, 2006, p. 721).
As mentioned above, the Vessantara- jātaka recounts the Buddha- to- be’s 
perfection of generosity in his antepenultimate life. The Pali- Siamese format 
of the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ is turned to poetic and affective ends, rather than

<!-- Page 23 -->
Epiphytic Literatures 71
exegetical ones. Some of the most aesthetically reﬁned and emotionally 
expressive passages in the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ appear in the eighth chapter, 
“Kumār” (The children). This section focuses on Vessantara’s giving away of 
his older son Jāli and younger daughter Kaṇhā to an old and cruel brahman, 
Jūjaka. Vessantara, believing he must give away his children in order to reach 
buddhahood in a future life, waits until the children’s mother Maddī has gone 
off to the forest in search of food. Jūjaka then comes to Vessantara to request 
the children. Jāli and Kaṇhā initially resist, hiding in a pond to avoid the 
brahman’s violent beatings. Eventually, however, the children resign themselves 
to their fate. When Jūjaka comes back to carry off the children, they take leave 
of their home and belongings. As Jāli and Kaṇhā are beaten and driven away 
by their captor, they cry out to their father, offering words of solace to both of 
their parents (Cone and Gombrich, 1977, pp. 61– 63).
The anonymous authors of the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ work carefully within 
the bounds of bitextual techniques to make this touching scene come to life. 
Unlike all of the examples discussed thus far, the vernacular portions of the 
Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ are in verse, not prose. Although there are some passages in 
more complex forms, most of the Siamese portions are in the simplest meter of 
Siamese verse, known as rāy₁, in which the last syllable of each phrase rhymes 
with any syllable (usually one of the ﬁrst three) of the following phrase. Each 
phrase may consist of any number of syllables but ﬁve is the standard (Hudak, 
1990, p. 10).

The brilliance of this bitext thus lies in the contrast it establishes between the 
bare Pali stanzas and their poetic reworking in Siamese translation. Even if 
semantically redundant, the Pali is not discarded, for it informs the reader 
or listener how to appreciate the aesthetic contributions of the Siamese. In 
botanical terms, we have a true epiphyte, for the local poetry thrives but never 
strangles its Indic tree. The aesthetic achievements of passages like this from 
the Mahājāt́i gāṁ hlvaṅ set an aesthetic standard in Siamese letters up to the 
development of modern prose literature, hinting at the expressive potential 
lying within a seemingly strict set of technical conventions.

both the geographic reach and diverse structures of bilingual Indic- vernacular

translations across mainland Southeast Asia. From the mid- ﬁfteenth through 
mid- twentieth centuries, Indic- vernacular bitexts occupied a central place in 
the intellectual, religious, and literary culture of the region. From the Mekong 
Delta to the upper reaches of the Irrawaddy, from the Malay Peninsula to the 
lower edges of Yunnan, monks, scholars, and poets studied, expounded, and 
expressed in Pali, Sanskrit, and their native vernaculars through the compos-
ition of Indic- vernacular bitexts.
Reading Southeast Asian bitextual translations as epiphytic literatures 
evokes key aspects of their structure, interpretative power, exegetical utility, 
and aesthetic potential. Not all Indic- vernacular bitexts are true epiphytes. 
Some can be likened to strangler ﬁgs, hemiepiphytes that eventually overwhelm 
much of their host, such that a largely vernacular text remains, with only traces 
of its Pali source. Other bitexts, including those that incorporate intricate series 
of rhetorical questions, recall how entangled vines and branches seem to blur 
the lines between host and epiphyte. Above all, however, the botanical meta-
phor encourages us to appreciate bitexts as consisting of separate organisms, 
namely their Pali and vernacular portions, as well as aesthetic wholes, like 
trunks lined with orchids or branches cloaked in moss. We cannot appre-
ciate the contributions of bitexts unless we can identity both their individual 
working parts and how those parts form a larger structure. The schematic 
diagrams employed in this chapter offer one method for conceptually mapping 
how bitextual translation works.
Theravada bitexts in Southeast Asia offer a unique take on the historical 
and ongoing transmission of Buddhist texts across cultures and languages. In 
the case of China and Tibet, the process of Buddhist translation was total, 
with the Indic source languages made almost fully invisible. The Chinese and 
Tibetan canons, products of some of the largest and longest translation efforts 
in human history during the ﬁrst and early second millennium CE, contain 
no bitexts but only monolingual Chinese- and Tibetan- language texts, respect-
ively. In Southeast Asia, by contrast, complete vernacular translations of the 
Pali Buddhist canon only took shape in the twentieth century. Prior to that, 
scriptural translation was always cultivated through the verdant ecosystem of 
bitexts. The complex way that Pali hosts and vernacular epiphytes intertwine 
ensures that the source and the target language remain locked in a tangle of 
structural dependence. Each time such bitexts are read or recited, they meto-
nymically evoke both other bitextual translations as well as the whole history 
of how South Asian scriptures came to be adopted in Southeast Asia.
In the broader landscape of translation in Southeast Asia, bitexts occupy 
a crucial if under acknowledged role, particularly in the mainland states of 
Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, and Vietnam. Translation, bitexts 
remind us, is not only about moving from the source language to the target 
language, but also about lingering in the space in between. Some varieties 
of Indic- vernacular bitexts, including the interlinear forms not studied in 
this chapter, ﬁnd ready parallels in the annotated manuscripts of medieval 
Europe (Blom, 2017) and East Asia (Kornicki, 2018, pp. 157– 186). But
