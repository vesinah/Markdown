Indonesia
Fenomena Tafsir Bugis dalam Islamisasi di Indonesia Timur

Abstract
This article examines the phenomenon of Bugis tafsir, both written and oral, produced by the As’adiyah 
ulama during the Islamization of Eastern Indonesia. As’adiyah, the oldest pesantren in the region, 
played a crucial role in the dissemination and teaching of Islam. Within this process of Islamization, 
Bugis tafsir emerges as a significant subject of study, as tafsir was foundational to the establishment 
of this pesantren. This is exemplified by the Bugis tafsir Tafsir Surah Amma Bil-Lughah Al-Buqisiyyah 
by AGH. Muhammad As’ad Al-Bugisy, the founder of As’adiyah. Furthermore, Bugis tafsir has been 
developed in various forms by As’adiyah scholars, initiated by the early students of the pesantren’s 
founder. This article argues that local tafsir is not only part of the intellectual heritage of pesantren 
but also plays a vital role in shaping pesantren traditions. This study employs a phenomenologicalhistorical approach, combining literature review and fieldwork. Its findings indicate that the production 
of Bugis tafsir is essential for the promotion and transmission of Islam in Eastern Indonesia through 
Qur’anic interpretation that engages with contemporary contexts. This role is particularly evident in 
three key aspects: (1) culturally rooted da’wah (religious outreach), (2) the preservation of intellectual 
and cultural heritage through interpretation, and (3) the safeguarding of the distinctiveness of 
pesantren and Bugis culture. These three primary functions highlight the importance of employing 
local languages in the process of Islamization through interpretation—especially within the cultural 
framework of the interpreter, particularly when the interpreter is a pesantren ulama.
Keywords:  Bugis Tafsir, Islamization, Ulama, As’adiyah Tradition, Eastern Indonesia.

Introduction
The use of local languages in Qur’anic interpretation by pesantren ulama 
has a long-standing tradition, as exemplified by the phenomenon of Bugis tafsir 
(Buginese Qur’anic Interpretation) produced by the ulama—commonly referred to as 
Anre Gurutta Haji (AGH) or Gurutta—of Pondok Pesantren As’adiyah Sengkang, 
the oldest and most prominent pesantren in Eastern Indonesia, particularly in the 
Sulawesi-Papua region.1 The founder of this pesantren, AGH Muhammad As’ad 
Al-Bugisy (d. 1952), pioneered the development of Bugis tafsir through his work 
Tafsir Suratul ‘ Amma bil Lugha Al-Buqisiyyah (1948). In addition to producing written 
interpretations (kitab), As’adiyah ulama have also delivered oral interpretations in the 
Bugis language, exemplified by AGH Abu Nawas Bintang (d. 2018), a senior figure 
at pesantren, during his sessions at the Great Mosque of Ummul Qura, Sengkang.2 
The use of the Bugis language in Qur’anic interpretation by As’adiyah ulama reflects 
an effort to communicate and contextualize Islamic knowledge within the Bugis 
cultural framework. Here, The term “Bugis community” refers to people of Bugis 
ethnicity, originally from South Sulawesi, who have continued to grow and spread to 
various regions, becoming one of the dominant communities in Eastern Indonesia, 
particularly in Sulawesi and Papua. 3 Their primary written language historically 
employs the Lontara script. Thus, Bugis tafsir refers to the interpretation of the 
Qur’an by Bugis scholars in their native language and within their sociocultural 
context.

significant as language functions not only as a means of communication but also as

study adopts a phenomenological-historical approach to explore the phenomenon of 
Bugis tafsir among pesantren in the islamization of Eastern Indonesia, particularly

Bugis and the Tradition of As’adiyah Pesantren
The discussion of Bugis tafsir among As’adiyah pesantren in Eastern Indonesia 
is inherently tied to the Bugis cultural context and the emergence of the As’adiyah 
pesantren within the region. “Bugis” refers to an ethnic group in Eastern Indonesia 
whose members, known as To Ogi (“the Bugis people”), have used Lontara-Bugis 
script as their primary language since pre-Islamic times. This language continues 
to serve as a medium of communication, including in the dissemination of Islamic 
teachings.20 The Bugis constitute the majority ethnic group and linguistic community 
in Eastern Indonesia. Notably, the world’s longest epic, La Galigo , was composed 
in the Lontara-Bugis script. Today, Bugis communities are primarily found in the 
regencies of Wajo, Bone, Pinrang, Sidrap, Sinjai, Pangkep, and Barru, as well as in 
other regions of Indonesia, including in diaspora communities in Saudi Arabia and 
Malaysia.
Before embracing Islam, the Bugis people adhered to a traditional belief 
system centered on Attoriolong, which involved the worship of deities and ancestral

his familiarity with the Bugis community, and as Alim Al-Allamah Sheikh Muhammad 
As’ad bin Muhammad Abdul Rashid Al-Bugisy, signifying his position as the supreme 
teacher of ulama in the Bugis region and his scholarly association with KH. Hasyim 
Asy’ari, founder of the ORMAS Nahdlatul Ulama.28 He was of Bugis descent and 
was born, raised, and educated in Islam in Mecca, Saudi Arabia, particularly at 
the Al-Falah Madrasa and through various halaqas held in mosques. This halaqa 
system was later introduced to the Bugis and renamed mangaji tudang. Furthermore, 
AGH As’ad taught Islam through mangaji tudang method, initially conducted at 
his residence before relocating to the Jami Sengkang-Wajo Mosque at the request 
of Arung Matoa. There, it eventually developed into a madrasa system known as 
Madrasah Arabiyah Islamiyah (MAI).
After AGH As’ad passed away, approximately twenty years later, in 1952, MAI 
was further developed into a pesantren and renamed Pondok Pesantren As’adiyah, 
under the leadership of AGH. Daud Ismail. The name ‘As’adiyah’ was adopted to 
honor its founder.29 As’adiyah went on to become the first pesantren in Eastern 
Indonesia. Since its inception, this pesantren has been dedicated to the dissemination 
and teaching of Islamic knowledge.30 The religious ideology accepted and practiced 
in As’adiyah is Ahlu Sunna wal Jama’ah, with the daily school tradition following 
the shafi’iyah school of thought, specifically aimed at developing santri (alumni) who 
are intelligent, faithful, pious, possess noble character, and love the motherland, the 
Republic of Indonesia.31

bestowed by the Bugis community on individuals who understand, practice, and

In daily life and study, As’adiyah santri and ulama are active (fluent) in and 
pay special attention to the use of Bugis language, both orally and in writing, even 
incorporating it as a learning resource in Regional Language studies. Pesantren 
literature is written and conveyed in the Bugis language, both by those who continue 
to use lontara script (lontara-Bugis) and by those who use Latin script (latin-Bugis). 
The term lontara-Bugis appears in tafsir books written by As’adiyah ulama, as well as 
in Arabic texts. Meanwhile, Latin-Bugis is used in da’wah materials, such as lectures, 
short sermons (seven-minute lectures or cults), and Friday sermons, which are studied 
and preached to non-Bugis students who are unfamiliar with lontara script.  All of 
this suggests that the Bugis language serves as the primary language (mother tongue) 
in As’adiyah, both formally (in learning) and informally (in everyday life).
As a result, the Bugis tribe and language became the foundational context 
for the emergence of As’adiyah pesantren, which was oriented toward da’watul 
Islamiyah. The Bugis tribe and language play a significant role in delivering and 
transforming knowledge from the pre-pesantren ( halaqah) to the establishment of 
As’adiyah pesantren. Simultaneously, in eastern Indonesia, Bugis serves as a medium 
for Islamic instruction by As’adiyah Pesantren, delivered through ustadz, Gurutta, 
and Anre Gurutta Haji, both formally (via educational institutions) and informally 
(through direct da’wah to the community via mosques). Using the Bugis language, 
this da’wah orientation evolved into the As’adiyah tradition, which is directly tied 
to the scientific tradition of this pesantren, encompassing both written and oral 
interpretation of the Qur’an. The next section will elaborate on tafsir, which is a 
major part of the pesantren academic tradition.

Bugis people have regarded the Qur’an as a guide to life in both belief and culture; 
hence, it must be studied and recited. In fact, the Bugis community believes that if 
Bugis Muslims do not read the Qur’an from beginning to end (khatam), their Islam is 
considered incomplete.33 The Bugis community also places a high value on Qur’anic 
interpretation (tafsir), particularly at the educational level. Pelras34 and Mattulada35 
in their respective studies, found that the study of Qur’anic interpretation became 
a learning resource at the highest level of education, a tradition preserved in the 
intellectual heritage of pesantren, including As’adiyah.
Tafsir plays a vital role in As’adiyah, as evidenced by the widespread creation 
of written and oral exegeses, which can be traced through publications, recitations, 
and specific educational programs (takhashus). In terms of written works, As’adiyah 
ulama were pioneers, producing numerous tafsir books in Lontara-Bugis, specifically 
the Lontara script in the Bugis language. These works include Tafisr Bahasa Boegisnja 
Soerah Amma (1948) by AGH. As’ad; Tafsir Al-Qur’an Al-Karim bi Al-Lughah AlBuqisiyyah juz 1 (1958) and 30 (1974) by AGH. Muhammad Yunus Martan (d.

by AGH. Muin Yusuf (d. 2004); and Tafsir Al-Qur’an Al-Karim bi Al-Lugah AlBugisiyyah (1997) by AGH. Abduh Pabbaja (d. 2009). These tafsir were originally 
handwritten and privately owned; however, over time, they were reproduced and 
became widely accessible.
In addition to written tafsir, As’adiyah ulama actively provides oral tafsir, 
particularly to reinforce tafsir Jalalain. This oral tafsir is recited in the Bugis language, as 
demonstrated by AGH. Abu Nawas Bintang (d. 2018), one of Ma’had Aly As’adiyah’s 
caretakers at the Great Mosque of Ummul Qura Sengkang. His sessions attract both 
Ma’had Aly students and the general public and are frequently broadcast through 
various media, such as Radio Suara As’adiyah (RSA), Facebook (@As’adiyah Pusat), 
and the YouTube channel (@As’adiyah Channel), thereby reaching audiences beyond 
the Sengkang-Wajo area. In addition to AGH. Abu Nawas, Gurutta Ahmad Agus

The previous section discussed tafsir in the scientific tradition of As’adiyah 
Pesantren. Tafsir, as one of the main scientific traditions, has an important and distinct 
role in developing the oral and written traditions of As’adiyah. This section discusses 
the unique position of As’adiyah tafsir to trace its development as a pesantren-based 
Bugis tafsir, in both oral and written forms. In this setting, the development of 
Bugis tafsirs at As’adiyah is closely tied to two interrelated dimensions: internal and 
external. The internal dimension includes language, methodology, references, and 
so on. The external context encompasses broader factors such as the social roles of 
interpreters, the pesantren environment, and Bugis cultural influences. These internal 
and exterior settings are linked together in Bugis tafsir among As’adiyah pesantren 
ulama.

As’adiyah ulama, such as AGH. Abu Nawas and Gurutta Agus, also produce

of contemporary scholars, both from the Middle East and Indonesia.42 With these

explained freely according to the listener’s context and sequentially from QS. AlFatihah to QS. Al-Baqarah, and so forth.
In the external setting, the development of Bugis tafsirs is directly tied to the 
social role of the interpreter, the situation and direction of the pesantren, and the 
demands of the local Bugis community, leading to adoption and adaptation. In this 
context, the employment of the Bugis language demonstrates that the interpretation 
is influenced by the Bugis external environment and is therefore tailored to its needs. 
The author’s use of Lontara-Bugis reflects the cultural distinctions among Bugis 
regions. Muhsin Mahfuz, for example, revealed that there are differences in the 
character (dialect) of the Bugis language in Bugis commentary books due to authors’ 
área of origin and/or the region where the books were written—for instance, AG. 
Daud uses the Bugis-Soppeng dialect, while AGH. Yunus and AGH. Hamzah uses 
the Bugis-Wajo dialect.43 When As’adiyah ulama conceal themselves in society, a 
dialectic emerges between their interpretations and the context in which they exist.

The Inevitability of Local Tafsir Based on Pesantren
Various earlier explanations clarify the critical role of Bugis tafsir creation, 
particularly as a primary science shaping pesantren tradition. Although Abdurrahman 
Wahid49 and Martin Van Bruinessen50 identify tafsir (in general) as one of the most 
important disciplines in pesantren, the use of the local language (Bugis) confirms 
(specifies) that pesantren traditions are also shaped by local tafsir. This section explores 
the importance of Bugis tafsir, particularly in revealing the positive aspects of both 
socio-religion and Bugis culture. Furthermore, this aspect reflects to the success of 
As’adiyah scholars, both individually and collectively, in understanding the Qur’an 
and communicating its meaning within the Bugis community’s religious and cultural 
context in Eastern Indonesia. 
Bugis society, with its own pre-Islamic religion and culture, became a central 
focus for As’adiyah scholars, culminating in the incorporation of Islam into Bugis 
culture. This phenomenon parallels other well-known examples of Islamic integration 
into local cultures include the da’wah of the Wali Songo preaching in Java. 51 This 
demonstrates that by producing local tafsir, pesantren circles can also—and even 
become important actors—in teaching Islam (particularly the content of the Qur’an) 
to the larger community by prioritizing culturally based benefits, particularly through 
the use of local languages. Within the As’adiyah tradition, the use of the Bugis 
language fulfills social, cultural, and religious needs, as it responds to the local 
community’s urgent need to comprehend its newly embraced faith. 
As previously stated, in teaching Islam—particularly understanding the 
content of the Qur’an content—As’adiyah circles use mangaji tudang (halaqah), 
a method derived from the scholarly tradition of Mecca and is believed to have

transitioning from direct (offline) to mangaji tudang to its current forms. This trend 
aligns with the findings of Nurtawab and Wahyudi 52 and M. Falikul Isbah,53 who 
discovered that pesantren-based education consistently adapts to changing time and 
context.
The production of Bugis tafsir by the As’adiyah ulama, which has become 
an intellectual asset for the pesantren tradition, demonstrates the adaptive nature of 
Islam and the Qur’an over time while also maintaining the Bugis script, language, and 
associated cultural identity. Bugis tafsir thus becomes part of the broader heritage of 
local tafsir in pesantren milieu. Historically, local tafsir began around the seventeenth 
century, as exemplified by Abdul Rauf Singkel’s Turjuman Al-Mustafid (PegonJawi). From the the nineteenth to the twentieth centuries, many local commentaries 
emerged by pesantren ulama, including Faid Al-Rahman (Pegon-Jawi) by KH. Shalih 
Darat, Raudat Al-Irfan fi Ma’rifat Al-Qur’an (Pegon-Sunda) by Ahmad Sanoesi, 
Al-Ibriz (Pegon-Jawi) by KH. Bisri Mustafa, Iklil (Pegon-Jawi) by KH. Misbah 
Mustafa, and others. Pink’s two written commentaries on Al-Ibriz and Iklil are 
strongly suggest that these written texts originated as adaptations of oral teachings 
delivered in pesantren circles.54 Beyond Indonesia, local tafsir from other countries 
can be found on websites such as Gloqur: the Global Qur’an,55 which provides tafsir 
and translations from many regions worldwide, including local languages. 
As highlighted earlier, many As’adiyah scholars produce Bugis tafsir to 
preserve pesantren identity and culture, demonstrating that the nationalist mindset 
is embedded in the As’adiyah legacy. In this context, the preservation of pesantren 
and culture is a manifestation of the As’adiyah ulama love for the country and 
their commitment to maintaining local culture, thereby enabling the generalization 
of the Qur’anic understanding to be readily accepted by the Bugis community.

 Conclusion
Based on the preceding discussion, it is clear that producing Bugis tafsir, 
both written and oral, in the As’adiyah tradition is essential for teaching the Qur’an 
in Eastern Indonesia. This is particularly significant given the history of As’adiyah 
historical development within the Bugis context, where the Bugis people hold the 
Qur’an in high regard as their new Holy Book. Bugis tafsir have evolved over time, 
both in terms of language and interpretive media, with three primary objectives: (1) 
cultural-based da’wah (communication), (2) intellectual and cultural treasures in 
the form of interpretation, and (3) the preservation of the uniqueness of pesantren 
and Bugis culture. These three objectives demonstrate the importance of using 
local languages in Islamization through interpretation within the context of the 
interpreter’s cultural area, particularly if the interpreter is an pesantren ulama. The 
creation of Bugis tafsir demonstrates that tafsir acticity is more than a solely activity 
for understanding the Qur’an; it is represent an efort to contextualize the Qur’an 
by prioritizing local socio-cultural relevance. Based on these findings, local tafsir at 
pesantren is predicted to maintain a prominent position as an intellectual legacy, a 
method of da’wah, and an identity, indicating the character of religious moderation 
in the production of local tafsir. To realize this objective, further research is needed 
on this topic, especially regarding the understanding of pesantren ulama in their 
local tafsir, so that pesantren can continue serving as a stronghold for promoting and 
consolidating religious moderation in Indonesia.
