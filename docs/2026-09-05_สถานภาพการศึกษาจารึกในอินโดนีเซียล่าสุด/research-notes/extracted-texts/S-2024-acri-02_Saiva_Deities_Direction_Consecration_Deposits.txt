The Śaiva Deities of the Directions  
in the Light of Two Recently Discovered 
Consecration Deposits ( Peripih )  
from Central Java
Mimi  Savitri1, Andrea  Acri2* and Sofwan  Noerwidi3
1Universitas Gadjah Mada (UGM), Y ogyakarta, Indonesia
2École Pratique des Hautes Études (EPHE, PSL University) and GREI, Paris, France
3Badan Riset dan Inovasi Nasional (BRIN), Jakarta, Indonesia
ABSTRACT
This article returns to the question of the Śaiva deities of the points of the compass in premodern Central 
Java in the light of archaeological and epigraphical evidence, namely the recently excavated, ca. 9th–10th 
century ritual deposits of Candi Kimpulan and Ringinlarik, which include most notably a golden lotus and 
inscribed gold foils laid within stone caskets. By comparing these new findings with relevant Old Javanese 
inscriptions and transmitted literature, as well as similar material and textual evidence from the wider Indic 
world (i.e., South and Southeast Asia), the authors will describe the system of twenty-four plus two protec -
tive deities surrounding the central deity (Śiva) that appears to have been prevalent in Central Java, and 
which largely agrees with the one hypothesized by Acri and Jordaan (2012) on the basis of the analysis of 
the reliefs of Candi Śiva in Loro Jonggrang, Prambanan. Further, the results of an analysis of the material 
component of the gold foils are discussed.

INTRODUCTION
Consecration deposits, known in Old Javanese and Indonesian as peripih ,1 have been found in 
‘Hindu’ (i.e., Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava) and Buddhist temples in several regions of South and Southeast 
Asia (O’Connor 1966; Soekmono 1995; Jordaan and Wessing 1996; Ślączka 2007, 2011, 2017). 
Our knowledge about these ritual deposits is mainly based on archaeological remains as well as 
Sanskrit ritual manuals from South Asia. Peripih s were not located in one specific part of the 
temple, but were placed in various parts such as the main temple area, the temple’s pavilion, the 
wall, the gateway, the chapel for the attendant deities, or wherever the first brick or stone was 
placed. Peripih were laid or planted in one or more locations in a temple not only to protect it 
from outside harmful influences, but also to consecrate and ‘activate’ it—in fact, a temple with -
out a peripih  may have no longer been considered active and functioning. Peripih s were also 
regarded as places where the main deity and his various manifestations incarnated (Soekmono 
1995: 14–15). The laying of the peripih  was part of at least two important consecration rituals 
performed at the time of the establishment of temples, i.e. the garbhanyāsa  and the ratnanyāsa . 
These facts demonstrate that peripih s and their caskets played a significant role in a temple and 
in the ritual and belief systems of the societies of premodern South and Southeast Asia. And yet, 
relatively little research on them has been carried out so far.

Judging from the  archaeological finds, it may be assumed that most of the temples built in 
Central Java from the 9th century onwards had at least one peripih laid within a casket under 
their main room. The peripih  containers that are often found in Central Java are made of ande -
site stone in the shape of a square casket with several holes. The peripih s were found to contain 
a variety of items such as seeds, ash, bones, precious stones, inscribed metal foils, and coins.  
A remarkable and extraordinarily well-preserved peripih  was found in a casket placed beneath 
the liṅga of the 9th to 10th century Śaiva temple now known as Candi Kimpulan in Yogyakarta, 
Central Java, which was discovered in December 2009 during excavations reaching 2.70 meters 
below the ground level.2 The casket contained semi-precious stones, inscribed gold and silver 
foils, tiny fragments of bronze, and coins, among other things (Fig. 1).3 
Another unique peripih  was accidentally discovered in 2016 in an open land area belonging 
to the village of Ringinlarik, Musuk sub-district, Boyolali district (Central Java), near the Merapi 
volcano. A square stone casket was found at 1.5 meters depth under a pile of stones and soil of 
what once must have been a Śaiva site of worship. Inside it there were twenty-two small gold 
foils, each of which contains a short inscription with the name of a deity in Sanskrit/Old Javanese 
language and Kawi script that is compatible with a 9th–10th century dating.4 
Both discoveries are important and unique insofar as inscribed gold foils have often been loot -
ed in the past, while in these two locations they have been found in their archaeological context. 
Furthermore, their inscriptions still await to be deciphered and interpreted in the light of similar 
vestiges as well as relevant textual documents.
Ritual deposits as archaeological remains offer useful insights for understanding the life of the 
Javanese in the past according to the perspective of archaeology, and to reconstruct aspects of the 
material culture and socio-religious life of the builders and users of temples. The main tasks of 
this article are three: first, to furnish a raw material description of the gold and stone artefacts 
belonging to the two aforementioned peripih s, including an analysis of the material composi -
tion of their gold foils; second, to produce a transcription and interpretation of the inscriptions 
engraved on the gold foils of Ringinlarik; and third, to explain the function of Śaiva temples as 
buildings reflecting central aspects of the premodern Javanese cosmology and ritual landscape, 
especially with respect to the spatial orientation of temples and the symbolic meaning of the 
directions of the compass.5 We will address the question as to whether the empty cavities of the 
casket of the peripih  found within the Kimpulan yoni  reflected a system of directional deities by 
comparing it with the system emerging from the gold foils of the peripih  of Ringinlarik. Having

THE PERIPIH  OF CANDI KIMPULAN 
More than eighteen undisturbed ritual deposits were recovered in the course of excavations un -
dertaken in 2009 at the site where building workers were digging the foundation for a new library 
on the campus of the Universitas Islam Indonesia in Sleman, on the southern slope of Mount 
Merapi, 14.5 km north of Yogyakarta (Indung Panca Putra et al.  2019a: 76). These peripih s be-
longed to a Śaiva temple consisting in two open buildings in andesite stone—the main temple 
and a subsidiary temple or candi  perwara —found close to a river. The temple, named Kimpulan 
thereafter, was clearly not an isolated one, but part of a network of sanctuaries that covered the 
area between Borobudur and Prambanan and that was associated with wet-rice cultivation ( ibid. : 
78). Two temples located closest to Candi Kimpulan are Candi Palgading and Candi Morangan. 
There are also several archaeological remains found spread around villages whose names contain 
the word candi  (‘temple’), such as Candi Karang, Candirejo, and Candi Winangun (see Map 1).
Kimpulan has been tentatively regarded as a village or hermitage shrine rather than a royal 
shrine ( ibid.), as suggested by its layout, its small size, and also by the fact that the main building 
does not have many ornaments, as the largest sanctuaries of Borobudur and Prambanan.7 Yet, 
surprisingly, the temple has yielded a significant quantity of foils made of gold and silver, as well 
as other objects made of precious materials, which were found beneath the floor of the main tem -
ple. Another interesting feature of the temple is the series of eleven convex round stones (internal 
row) placed on the temple terrace, roughly in correspondence with the points of the compass (in 
the east, southeast, south, southwest, northwest, north and northeast—the west being marked by 
the statue of Gaṇeśa) ( ibid.: 82).8 The arrangement of those stones can be seen in Fig. 2. Whereas 
the external row of twelve stones have been identified as pillar bases, the internal row of eleven 
stones may have functioned as markers of the directions ( ibid.). A similar arrangement is found 
at Candi Gunung Sari, a probably coeval Śaiva temple located in the district of Magelang, where 
the cylindrical stones bear short inscriptions indicating the eight directions (Degroot, Griffiths,

While all the aforementioned caskets contained deposit offerings,10 we will only discuss the 
first one, located under the liṅga of the main temple, because it was the only one to contain the 
round lotus-shaped gold plate in the cavity at the centre (see Fig. 6),11 surrounded by the sixteen 
cavities of the casket. As noted by previous scholars, the ritual deposits of Candi Kimpulan are 
possibly linked to the rituals of ratnany āsa and garbhany āsa; and while Sanskrit texts prescribe 
that caskets must be either round or square (preferably made from metal, unlike in Java) and 
subdivided into either nine or twenty-five compartments,12 the significance of the cavities, ap -
parently linked to the main and intermediary directions of the compass, as well as the gold lotus 
plate placed in the central cavity of the Kimpulan main casket, has not received much scholarly 
attention so far. Indeed, the question as to whether any deities were associated with the directions 
of the compass symbolized by the cavities of this and similar artifacts has rarely been formulated, 
let alone answered.

Javanese peripih s, but absent from most published Sanskrit textual sources, with the exception 
of the Guhyasūtra of the Niśvā satattvasaṁhit ā (2.110) and the Vaiṣṇava Hayaśīr ṣapañcar ātrapu -
rāṇa (12.18).13 These authors draw insights from those texts, and especially the last (along with 
the Agnipur āṇa), which associated the central deposit as an invocation of the earth goddess;

The above-quoted interpretation of the Kimpulan peripih is a fair attempt to make sense of the 
data in the absence of textual correspondences with Sanskrit texts from the Indian subcontinent 
and other archaeological vestiges. In particular, we agree that the invocation of the Earth (as a 
goddess) is especially meaningful in the light of the association with protection of the directions 
of the space—an association that is also found in at least one Old Javanese inscription,14 as well 
as the archaeological findings of Central Javanese peripih s (see below). At the same time, here we 
aim at revisiting the statement that a reference to specific deities in a mandala-like arrangement 
is absent,15 and contend that the very existence and arrangement of the peripih  casket with sev -
enteen cavities, the central one of which contains the eight-petalled golden lotus, already reflects 
a mandala of sorts inhabited by the deities of the directions and the sacred body of Śiva corre -
sponding to the cosmos. Since no inscriptions identifying the directions and, we assume, the 
deities that must have corresponded to each one of them, have survived in situ , we shall now turn 
to nearly coeval archaeological and textual data to reconstruct the cosmological system that must 
have justified the way in which this and other similar caskets containing ritual deposits found in 
premodern Javanese Śaiva temples were crafted. 
THE INSCRIBED GOLD FOILS OF RINGINLARIK AND THEIR ARCHAEOLOGICAL 
CONTEXT
In 2016, while workers were excavating a site to build a large (4.2 hectares) rainwater reservoir 
in Ringinlarik (Kecamatan Musuk, Kabupaten Boyolali) (Fig. 7),16 a stone casket, covered by a 
lid (Fig. 8), and reported to contain twenty-two inscribed gold foils was discovered among other 
ancient cut stones that must have belonged to a religious structure in the past. The stones are 
now inside the dried-up reservoir, and more stones are found on its borders, on the western side, 
covered by thick vegetation (Fig. 9).

The discovery of these remains is significant from several points of view. The region including 
the eastern slopes of Mount Merapi, in the Boyolali province, is not very rich in pre-Islamic ar -
chaeological remains, although there is a cluster of sites within a narrow area (Degroot 2009: 55; 
see Map 2 here). The reason of this relative paucity of sites is that the area where temple remains 
have been found has a high average altitude, between 300m and 700m above the sea level, which 
has the disadvantage of being scarce in water and very close to the active volcano of Merapi, as 
well as being not suitable for wet rice cultivation ( ibid.: 91, 103). On the other hand, the temples 
may have been located near a communication route linking the agricultural plains to the north -
ern coast of Java ( ibid.: 103–104, fig. 20). According to Degroot ( ibid.: 40, 49–50, 52), only ten 
temples are recorded in old inventories, of which three have disappeared, two are reduced to 
scattered stone remains, and only four are still extant.17 
As for the casket containing the gold foils, the fact that it has a lid suggests that it may have 
been the central one in the temple,18 thus located beneath a yoni , which however has not been 
recovered yet. The foils constitute a unique finding as they may be regarded as the remains of a 
peripih  that has reached us mostly intact, and that forms a nearly complete set of inscriptions con -
taining the names of Śaiva deities. As we will try to demonstrate, these documents are important 
to uncover the symbolism and cosmological system informing the caskets and offerings (among 
which, inscribed or illustrated metal foils and gems) found in Javanese peripih s, mainly because 
other similar vestiges are either incomplete or reflecting series that are different from what must 
have been the ‘standard’ series of sixteen guardians of the main and intermediate directions. 19

We will now turn to roughly coeval inscriptions and transmitted literature from Java and Bali 
to find an independent confirmation of the cosmological model reflected by the deities’ names 
found in the peripih  of Ringinlarik, and that can be also deduced from the seventeen-holed main

A genre of relevant transmitted sources in Old Javanese documenting various series of di -
rectional deities is the large collection of Sanskrit hymns from Balinese manuscripts edited by 
Goudriaan and Hooykaas (1971) under the title Stuti and Stava. While, generally speaking,  many 
relevant  hymns do not feature the ‘standard’ list of eight Lokapālas, in hymns 802, 824, 935, and a 
few others, we find mention of the quartet of Lokapālas of the cardinal directions, namely Indra 
(E), Yama (S), Varuṇa (W) and Kubera (N). An exception is hymn 40, Ākāśastava , which men -
tions the eight Lokapālas (with Sūrya instead of Nairr ̥ti) as aspects of Śiva. Much more numer -
ous are the hymns mentioning only the alternative series of eight Śaiva regents of the directions 
(Digbandhas), viz. Īśa, Maheśvara, Brahmā, Rudra, Mahādeva, Śaṅkara, Viṣṇu, and Śambhu;38 
indeed, according to Acri and Jordaan (2012), it is likely that the Digbandhas were regarded as 
hierarchically higher than the eight ‘Brahmanical’ Lokapālas and the eight minor Śaiva deities of 
the intermediate directions. 
The most interesting hymn for our discussion is 751, entitled Śivastava  or Śivasam ūha. It 
praises Śiva as the paramount Lord and characterizes his body as made up of various elements 
of the cosmos, as well as deities that constitute his manifestations. In verses 4–9, it equates Śiva 
with Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Mahādeva (three of the Digbandhas; see below), and then with the 
eight Lokapālas, namely Indra, Varuṇa, Yama, Kubera, Sūrya, Agni, Vāyu, and Aiśānya. It also 
lists Nirr ̥ti and Candramās (moon, next to Sūrya). In verses 9cd–10ab, the hymn refers to an -
other series of eight manifestations, namely Satya, Dharma, Kāla, Mr ̥tyu, Krodha, Viśva, Karma 
(Kāma? see below), and Hari (i.e. Viṣṇu); although this is not expressly mentioned in the text, 
each deity is apparently to be understood as being correlated with a direction of the compass. 
The series of eight deities of the main directions plus eight interstitial deities plus Brahmā, Viṣṇu, 
and Mahādeva, as well as the mention of the moon, corresponds to the deities engraved on the 
Ringinlarik gold foils, except Pr ̥thivī and Nāga. The account is also interesting insofar as it men -
tions Viṣṇu twice, as Viṣṇu and Hari (associated with, respectively, the zenith and the direction 
between north and northeast), which reflects the same double occurrence of Viṣṇu in the Ring -

THE RELIEFS OF DIRECTIONAL DEITIES ON CANDI ŚIVA AT LORO JONGGRANG, 
PRAMBANAN
On the basis of textual and iconographical data, Acri and Jordaan (2012) argued that the twen -
ty-four deities depicted on the reliefs surrounding the inner wall of Candi Śiva at Loro Jonggrang 
represent three series of āvaraṇ adevatā s, viz. the eight Lokapālas, the eight interstitial deities, 
and the eight Śaiva Digbandhas forming the ‘inner circle’ , plus the zenith (Viṣṇu, represented by 
Candi Viṣṇu to the north), the nadir (Brahmā, represented by Candi Brahmā to the south), and 
the centre, represented by the statue of Śiva in the temple’s main cella. We believe that the ar -
chaeological and textual data presented above confirm the reconstruction advanced by Acri and 
Jordaan, as the names of the eight + eight + three deities engraved on the gold foils of Ringinlarik 
and mentioned in Old Javanese epigraphic and transmitted sources closely correspond to those 
identified by Acri and Jordaan, plus the eight Digbandhas, which are mentioned in Old Javanese 
and Sanskrit transmitted sources in association with the aforementioned series of deities. This 
association is admittedly not easy to unpack, as indeed van Lohuizen-de Leeuw (1955: 376) was 
puzzled by the problem as to whether the one set preceded the other, or both were used simulta -
neously. She fleetingly considered the possibility of a conflation between the two groups of eight 
deities, especially in East Javanese Candi Panataran (ca. 14th century), but did not investigate the 
matter further. She regarded the Digbandhas—which are still popular in modern Bali, known 
under the collective denomination of Navasaṅa43—to have been introduced to Java in the middle 
of the 14th century at the earliest, but assumed that they derived from an ancient Indian proto -
type, possibly the eight Mūrtyaṣṭakas. As Acri and Jordaan (2012) argued, all three series were 
actually represented on Candi Śiva.
We argue that the system reflected by the iconography of Candi Śiva, comprising three series 
of deities delimiting the temple’s sacred space and representing Śiva’s emanation into circles of 
lower divinities, casts light on the function of seventeen-holed boxes housing consecration de -
posits recovered from Javanese temples, such as the one of Candi Kimpulan, which comprises 
an eight-petalled golden lotus at the centre. The lotus would represent the series of the Śaiva 
Digbandhas (forming the garbhāvaraṇ a or main retinue), with Śiva in the centre; the outer pet -

CONCLUSION
In this article we have shown that the combination of archaeology of landscape—for instance, 
the study of ancient artifacts as well as the archaeological context of the sites—with the study of 
ancient Javanese religious ideas and ritual practices in the light of Old Javanese texts, not only 
epigraphical but also in manuscript form, allows us to better understand the cosmological mean -
ing and symbolism of consecration deposits. The importance of the gold foils of Ringinlarik lies 
in the fact that, having been recovered in what seems to have been a nearly complete state, they 
name all the deities of what must have been a prevalent Śaiva cosmological system, except the 
eight Digbandhas. Our comparison with Old Javanese sources reveals that, besides a system of 
eight + eight + three deities, there existed another Śaiva directional system numbering eight + 
eight + eight + three deities. This system is reflected, we have argued, by the peripih  casket of 
Kimpulan with sixteen cavities surrounding a central cavity with a golden eight-petalled lotus 
placed inside it. The fact that the deposit was placed inside the liṅga—the most sacred place in 
the temple and the focus of ritual worship—indicates that it was meant to symbolically represent 
the body of Śiva himself. This arrangement seems compatible with the similar system of eight 
+ eight + eight + three deities found at Candi Śiva in Prambanan according to the hypothesis 
formulated by Acri and Jordaan (2012) on the basis of an analysis of the directional deities and 
manifestations of Śiva portrayed on the reliefs in comparison with Old Javanese sources. Thus, 
we conclude that the material, architectural, and textual data illuminate each other, and allow us 
to get a glimpse of the cosmology encoded by the ancient Javanese in their architectural, artistic, 
and textual productions.

es. However, in the case of the gold lotus in the peripih  of Candi Kimpulan, the higher amount 
of Au (c. 75% vs. between c. 65% and 68% of the other foils) may be significant. In view of the 
dearth of published data on the analysis of the composition of the gold items found in consecra -
