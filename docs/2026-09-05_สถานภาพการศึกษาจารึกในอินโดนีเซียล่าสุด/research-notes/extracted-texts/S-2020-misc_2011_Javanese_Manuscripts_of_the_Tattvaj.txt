The Tattvaj¤åna is an Old Javanese treatise, consisting of fifty short prose chapters, on ›aiva
philosophy, cosmology and yoga. The text, of uncertain date, belongs to the category of ›aiva
scriptures called Tutur s or Tattva s,3 which, like the vast majority of Old Javanese texts, have mostly
survived on palm-leaf manuscripts from the island of Bali. An annotated edition of the Tattvaj¤åna ,
based on five Balinese manuscripts and provided with a translation in Hindi, was published by
Sudarshana Devi (1962) under the auspices of the International Academy of Indian Culture.
This contribution is intended to add new elements to the work of Sudarshana Devi by making
known the existence of two previously unidentified manuscripts of the Tattvaj¤åna , the first from the
kabuyutan Ciburuy in West Java and the second from the Central Javanese Merapi-Merbabu
collection, now kept at the National Library of Indonesia in Jakarta.
This discovery is not as trivial as it may appear. Given the rarity of Old Javanese manuscripts
from Java, the very existence of a tradition of ›aiva treatises preserved in West and Central Javanese
manuscripts is in itself an interesting fact. As documented by the Dharma Påta¤jala , an important
Old Javanese ›aiva speculative treatise transmitted through a 15th-century manuscript of
Central Javanese origin,4 this tradition is clearly related to the Balinese one and shares the same
substantial number of parallels with the Sanskrit ›aiva literature from South Asia. Furthermore, to the

The discovery
In June 2008 I had the opportunity to inspect the collection of Ciburuy, a remote hillside-village in the
Garut Regency, West Java.5 The kabuyutan Ciburuy is regarded as the only ancient scriptorium to
have survived in Java. No less than twenty-six palm-leaf manuscripts, preserving texts in both
Old Sundanese and Old Javanese, are still to be found in a traditional Sundanese wooden hut, stored
inside three large wooden boxes. My attention focused on the four kropak s in the so-called
peti dua (box two), each one of which contained one nipah manuscript6 written with black ink in a
variety of Old (West) Javanese ëquadraticí script. As I went through the lines of the two ancient-
looking and worn-out leaves constituting what has survived of the manuscript stored in kropak
no. 23, I became aware that the Old Javanese text sounded familiar to me. A search in my database
confirmed that the text on both leaves was remarkably similar to the Tattvaj¤åna , therefore
suggesting that the complete manuscript must have contained a version of that text. This discovery
encouraged me to search further Indonesian manuscript collections, in the hope that other Javanese
manuscripts of the text could be identified.
My hopes were soon fulfilled when, during a visit to the National Library of Indonesia in
Jakarta, I went through the general catalogue (Behrend 1998) and noticed a manuscript bearing the
title Suluk Tattvaj¤åna (ms. 1L 214:342), apparently originating from the Central Javanese Merapi-

In the remainder of this paper I am going to present a transliteration of the section of text that,
following the numeration implemented in the edition, goes from line 14 of Chapter 10 throughout line
10 of Chapter 12, and from line 9 of Chapter 17 throughout line 4 of Chapter 18. Those sections
encompass the portion of text transmitted by the fragmentary manuscript from Ciburuy. The texts
transmitted by the three sources are presented in parallel, to make it easy for the reader to appreciate
the similarities and discrepancies among the versions from word to word.9
The left column presents the text constituted from the Ciburuy (C) manuscript ( peti 2 kropak 23),
consisting of two leaves (folios) whose sides have been arbitrarily numbered 1a/1b and 2a/2b.10
A portion of the left side of folio 1, approximately corresponding to between one-sixth and one-
seventh of the total length of the leaf, is missing because of deterioration. As a result, the damage
incurred has created a lacuna that amounts to one-third of the written left portion of the folio. Another
lacuna cuts in half the bottom-right line of text in folio 1a and the upper-right line of text in folio 1b for
approximately a half of the length of the right portion of written text. Leaf 2 is generally complete
apart from a small portion of leaf that has gone missing, which has caused the loss of the upper part of
a sequence of seven ak¶ara s in the upper-right line of folio 2b. Any lacunae in the text I have marked
with a series of dots ..., except when I felt that the missing portion of text could be reconstructed with
a high degree of probability through the comparison with the other versions of the text; the resulting
reconstructed portions I have enclosed within angle brackets < >. Round brackets ( ) enclose an
ak¶ara or a sequence of ak¶ara s that is not legible in its entirety as a result of either physical damage
to the manuscript or discoloration of the ink, but that I felt confident to reconstruct on the basis of
what is still legible.

Concluding remarks
A preliminary comparison of the texts reproduced above reveals a few interesting facts. The version
C 1 deviates considerably from MM and B in that it completely omits the portion of text elaborating
on self-identity ( aha∆kåra ) spanning across Chapters 10 and 11, and also presents a more general and
synthetic treatment of the topics. Towards the end of C 1b (= B Chapter 12) the text, before ending
abruptly, appears to run closer to the other versions. This is also the case in C 2, which leaves no
doubt as to its correspondence with chunks of Chapters 17 and 18 of B. MM and B run mostly in
parallel, presenting no significant omissions.
The three versions represent a telling example of contaminated transmission. The agreements
follow no discernible pattern, for instances of agreement between C and MM, C and B or MM and
B occur throughout the sample. In short, no version can be regarded to be completely unrelated to
another.
The aim of this short report is to indicate that the time is ripe for the publication of a new critical
edition of the Tattvaj¤åna , based on an extended number of manuscripts and provided with an
English translation. Given the characteristics proper to this kind of Old Javanese prose texts, to
prepare an edition on the basis of two different transmissions such as the Merapi-Merbabu and
Balinese appears to be neither desirable nor methodologically sound;11 yet, a systematic comparison
of those two versions may help to correct possible dubious readings and, what is more important,
enlighten us on the dynamics of textual production and transmission in ancient Java and Bali.
