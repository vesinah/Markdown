INTRODUCTION: APPROACHING MEDICINE IN JAVA AND BALI
IN THE LAST FEW DECADES, scholars have published extensively on the healing
traditions of pre-modern Asia. Even limiting the focus to “Monsoon Asia”—
whichwetakeinaninclusivesensetorefertoavastgeoenvironmentalzonecon-
necting South Asia to parts of Central, East, and Southeast Asia (Acri 2023)—,
there is a growing body of literature that explores the theoretical, practical and
social aspects of health practices. These efforts have primarily focussed on the
history of Āyurveda in South Asia (Wujastyk 2022; Angermeier 2023); the cir-
culation of Buddhism and associated medical practices (Salguero 2017;2022);
medicine and health among the Khmers, especially during the reign of Jayav-
arman VII (Jacques and Sharrock 2017); and finally on Chinese traditions of
healing, including Daoist ones, and their interaction with Buddhism (Li 2023;
Steavu2023). Within this complex puzzle, the traditions of pre-modern Mari-
time Southeast Asia, or Nusantara in the emic terminology, have hardly been
incorporated. We present here a preliminary survey of selected textual genres
from Java and Bali that we hope will encourage further discussions on both loc-
alised as well as translocal aspects of healing in the wider region of Monsoon
Asia.
While the present article focuses mostly on written sources, we acknowledge
that other disciplines and areas of research play a critical role. For example, an-
thropological work can provide insights into the diversity and complexity of
healing in Javanese and Balinese contexts. A recent documentary on the insti-
tutions providing mental health in Indonesian communities paints a picture of
diverging epistemologies promoted by trained health workers and psychiatrists,
religious healers such as Christian and Islamic priests, local shamans known as

What has been missing almost entirely in the study of healing in premod-

anecdotes that can be harvested across a large and diverse body of transmitted
literature. Old Javanese ornate literature ( kakavin) contains plenty of passages
describing sickness as well as therapeutic and preventive practices. One should
however bear in mind that this genre reflects a peculiar literary context akin to
Indian kāvya,which often contains artificial and idealized tropes. Thus, most
references to sickness actually refer to love sickness and the remedies proposed
are of a romantic rather than medical nature. Yet, kakavin still reflect aspects of
everyday life of premodern Java that are precious to reconstruct various aspects

perhaps our most abundant and detailed source of information on conceptions
of diseases and therapeutic practices and medicaments in premodern Java.

diagnostic vocabularies ( tatenger), plant-based remedies, applications, and

balians. This study does not attempt a survey of this vast literature, a substantial

examines how healing knowledge and medical ideas are embedded in various
genres of Old Javanese literature—with sporadic references to epigraphic
documents—produced from c.800 to 1500 CE. This undertaking, besides being
relevant in its own right, may also provide contextual evidence for the later

1 TERMINOLOGY IN CONTEXT: DISEASE, MEDICINE, AND
HEALERS
AS MENTIONED ABOVE, a fruitful entry point into the study of healing in
premodern Java and Bali comes from lexicography. Many references to
disease, plague, and epidemics are found in premodern Javanese literature,
both in the belles-lettres ( kakavin,kiduṅ) and in technical texts belonging to
religious literature ( tutur), traditional medical literature ( usada), and ritual
literature containing mantras and invocations to deities ( stuti). For instance,
the Old Javanese–English Dictionary (henceforth OJED: see Zoetmulder and
Robson 1982) attests such Sanskrit-derived terms as maraṇa/māraṇa (“[Skt]
maraṇa, the act of dying; māraṇa, killing, death; māraka, deadly disease, plague,
pestilence) death, plague, pest, crop disease”), roga“([Skt] illness, disease,
infirmity”), vikāra(“[Skt] change of form, deviation from natural state; change,
esp. for the worse, of bodily or mental condition; disease, hurt”), vyādhi(“[Skt]
disorder, disease, ailment, plague; disease, illness, disorder”), uṣṇa(“heat; hot”,
i.e., fever), et cetera, as well as the purely Old Javanese terms like griṅ/gəriṅ
(“illness, affliction”), lara/kalara(“pain [psychic or physical]; affliction, ache,
grief, heartache, heartbreak”), et cetera. A relevantpassageisfoundintheŚaiva
kakavin Śivarātrikalpa , which offers a vivid description of the hero’s illness and
contains various terms referring to illness and healing:

3 THE VEHICLES OF ADMINISTRATION OF MEDICINES:
WATER, ALCOHOL, OIL, AND OTHER OPTIONS
AHEALER WAS EXPECTED to undertake his or her own preparation of drugs.
Though some plant remedies, such as the kaṇḍuyuhan mentioned above,
would have been eaten in their raw form, active principles of drugs were
commonly extracted from herbal preparations in water, alcohol, and oil,
depending on their relative solubilities in the three media. Most alkaloids are
best extracted with alcohol; rice beer and distilled arrack (introduced in the
late thirteenth century CE) would have provided the strongest concentration
of alcohol available in pre-Islamic Java (Jákl 2021). The other vehicles seem to
have been (liquid) palm juice or sugar, honey, and betel quid. The routes of
administration of drugs mentioned in Old Javanese texts are oral and external
application, as well as fumigation. But we can be sure that other routes of
administration, suchasvaginalapplication(remediesandcontraceptives), were
known.

5 IMPORTED MEDICINES
RECENT ARCHAEOLOGICAL RESEARCH has opened a fascinating new window into
the premodern trade in medical substances and medicines, with a num-
ber of shipwrecks excavated in Southeast Asian waters yielding evidence for
Middle Eastern and Chinese products being traded to the Malay and Javanese
world. For example, small glass bottles recovered from land and shipwreck ar-
chaeological sites have often been interpreted as medicament vials (and hence
imported medicines). Though some bottles found in shipwrecks still contained
the residues of the substances filling them, the chemical studies of the residues
are yet to be conducted. A prominent cargo of glass vessels comes from the
Cirebon wreck, a ship of Indonesian construction that wrecked around 970 CE
some ninety nautical miles from Cirebon on the northern coast of Java. Needell
(2018:97–98) has suggested that smaller bottles and flasks, which show aﬀinit-
ies with Persian glass, “undoubtedly held scented liquids, oils, or powders used
as cosmetics or medicaments”, while the small and medium-sized bottles and
flasks “could have contained a substance such as rose water ( gulaborgolab), an
essential Persian product with cosmetic, culinary, and medicinal uses”.
Delicate white Qingbai ceramic boxes from Fujian, recovered from a num-
ber of Southeast Asian shipwrecks, have often been interpreted as containers
for medicines, cosmetics or perfumes (Mathers and Flecker 1997:131). Niziolek
and Respess ( 2017:796) have claimed that numerous ceramic and glass bottles
recovered from the Java Sea Shipwreck, like many found at archaeological sites
throughoutSoutheastAsia,“arepieceswithclearmedicinalapplicationsinclud-
ing wide-bodied kendis… likely used in the preparation of brewed applications,
ambrosia bottles possibly intended for use in medical rituals, and hundreds of
small boxes popular throughout the region for the storage of medicines, per-
fumes, mirrors, jewelry, and cosmetics”.
Two intact and three damaged ceramic bottles (ca.10cm in height) were
recovered from the Java Sea Wreck. The form is rare among existing pottery
in Southeast Asia, but it is well-known from the sculpture where bottles of a
similarshape, interpretedasholdingtheelixirofimmortality, serveasattributes
ofdeities. MathersandFlecker( 1997:129)callthecontainers“ambrosiabottles”
and hypothesize that they might have served as “container[s] for especially
treasured medicines.” Scholars have also suggested that some of the imported
Chineseceramicboxes,suchasa qingbaiboxwithwhatresemblesaphallus,may
have been put to use in the context of fertility medicine (Niziolek and Respess
2017:796). Though we must certainly avoid the assumption that a particular
ceramic type may ostensibly be made to fulfil a specific function (ceramics
are, in practice, highly adaptable and are often put to uses very different from
those for which they were originally made), there is no doubt that small glass
and ceramic vials traded over long distances did contain precious substances,
including medicaments.

healing in premodern Java and Bali. The fact that the passages were found
acrossavarietyofliterarysourcesandgenresisanindicationthatthisfieldofen-
quiry still has considerable potential. In this preliminary effort, we have covered
basic lexicography, pharmacology, literary depictions of healers as well as the

Whilethematerialspresentedheredonotallowustodevelopasophisticated
historiographical analysis, some key issues begin to emerge. Such issues go well
beyond the cliché of Nusantara as the “pharmacy of the world”, with its tradi-
tional focus on the trade in spices and herbal medicines. One central discussion

the one hand, often the terminology of passages analysed here point to a con-

ritual texts, a cosmopolitan component is easily detected in the form of Indic
models that are carefully selected and readapted to fit local needs. Comparison
with Āyurvedic texts as well as South Asian ritual manuals will certainly offer a
number of parallels. In this sense, the study of healing practices in premodern

tices, we also need to study the largely unexplored body of literature referred to
in modern Bali as usadha, consisting in a mix between recipes to produce natural
remedies as well as mantras and other supernatural means to protect, reinvigor-
ate, and heal human beings. Within this context, a comparison with medicinal
practicesin mainland SoutheastAsia, particularlythe Khmer context, will prove
helpful.
While we have not detected top-down royal initiatives of the kind sponsored
by Jayavarman VII, the literary richness of Javanese and Balinese sources is un-
