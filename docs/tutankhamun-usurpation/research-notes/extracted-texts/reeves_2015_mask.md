# Tutankhamun's Mask Reconsidered
## C. Nicholas Reeves
### Bulletin of the Egyptological Seminar (BES), Vol. 19 (2015), pp. 511–526

<!-- Page 511 -->
The gold funerary mask of Tutankhamun is arguably the most famous ancient artifact in existence. Discovered by Howard Carter in the burial chamber of KV62 in October 1925, it has come to symbolize the opulence of the boy-king's reign and the artistic pinnacle of late Eighteenth Dynasty craftsmanship. Yet despite nearly a century of study, several fundamental physical and epigraphical features of the mask remain poorly understood and require rigorous re-examination in light of contemporary archaeological methodology.

<!-- Page 512 -->
From the moment of its initial recovery, Carter noted minor peculiarities in the mask's finish, but subsequent discussions have tended to assume that the object was conceputalized and manufactured as an indivisible whole for the young king. A critical examination of the physical object in the Egyptian Museum, Cairo (JE 60672 / Carter no. 256a), combined with high-resolution digital imaging, reveals an entirely different reality: the mask is a composite assemblage bearing unmistakable evidence of technical modification and royal usurpation.

<!-- Page 513 -->
Close inspection of the mask reveals that it is not a unified piece of goldsmithing, but was constructed in several distinct sections that were subsequently joined together. The mask comprises at least two major metallurgical components: the front face-plate, and the rear headdress encompassing the nemes stripes, lappets, and broad collar. These elements were fabricated separately and united along an irregular join line.

<!-- Page 514 -->
The face is made of a noticeably lighter-colored gold than the nemes-headdress and collar. Scientific analysis indicates that the face consists of approximately 23.2-carat gold, whereas the surrounding headdress and collar are composed of an 18.4-carat gold alloy. This color differential and compositional divergence cannot be dismissed as accidental workshop variation; they represent two distinct manufacturing episodes utilizing different batches of royal bullion.

<!-- Page 515 -->
A distinct join or soldering seam is visible running completely around the edge of the face-plate, demonstrating that the face was inserted into the pre-existing headdress. The lapis lazuli inlays and glass pastes bordering the facial margin were evidently adjusted and set in mastic to conceal the physical junction between the newly inserted portrait of Tutankhamun and the prefabricated golden nemes.

<!-- Page 518 -->
The pierced earlobes of the mask have long puzzled commentators. In ancient Egypt, kings are not shown wearing earrings as adults; ear-piercing is characteristic of women and royal children. Although Tutankhamun was crowned as a child, adult pharaonic iconography strictly proscribed the display of open ear piercings on formal funerary masks. On Tutankhamun's mask, the ear holes are clumsily covered with small circular plaques of gold foil, a cosmetic repair intended to mask an iconographical feature that betrayed the female gender of the original owner.

<!-- Page 521 -->
Beneath the incised prenomen cartouche of Tutankhamun (Nebkheperure) on the back of the mask, faint traces of an earlier inscription are discernable. A crucial stroke is the sign ankh, together with kheperu, indicating the original presence of the prenomen Ankhkheperure. The signs had been chased out and smoothed over before the current royal titularies were hammered into the gold sheet.

<!-- Page 522 -->
The underlying epithet can be reconstructed as mery-Neferkheperure, 'beloved of Akhenaten', which is an epithet known exclusively from monuments belonging to the female pharaoh Neferneferuaten. This epigraphic trace provides conclusive textual corroboration of the metallurgical observations: the original owner of the headdress was Akhenaten's female co-ruler, whose names were erased when the object was expropriated to equip the burial of Tutankhamun.

<!-- Page 524 -->
The conclusion is inescapable: the mask was originally manufactured not for Tutankhamun, but for the female coregent and successor of Akhenaten, Ankhkheperure Neferneferuaten. Following Tutankhamun's premature death, his embalmers and funerary officials, faced with severe time constraints, removed Neferneferuaten's original face-plate, soldered in a new portrait representing the deceased young king, sealed the pierced ears, and reinscribed the cartouches.

<!-- Page 526 -->
This technical adaptation is not an isolated phenomenon in KV62. Rather, it aligns perfectly with the extensive usurpation previously demonstrated for the middle coffin (Carter 254) and the miniature canopic coffins (Carter 266g), establishing that the core ritual apparatus of Tutankhamun's burial was largely requisitioned from the cached funerary equipment of Queen-Pharaoh Neferneferuaten.
