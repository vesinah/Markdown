# The Complete Tutankhamun: The King, The Tomb, The Royal Treasure
## C. Nicholas Reeves
### London: Thames & Hudson, 1990

The middle coffin (Carter no. 254) is undoubtedly the most exquisite of the three, but its features present an extraordinary contrast with those of the innermost solid-gold coffin. While the innermost coffin (Carter no. 255) and the outermost wooden coffin (Carter no. 253) exhibit a consistent, youthful facial ideal that clearly portrays Tutankhamun, the middle coffin introduces a strikingly alien artistic sensibility into the burial nest.

<!-- Page 105 -->
The physiognomy of the middle coffin does not represent Tutankhamun; the facial contours, with their elongated eyes and drooping mouth, belong unmistakably to the classic Amarna artistic canon. The inlay technique, using vibrant polychrome glass pastes and imported stones set into gilded wood, replicates precisely the decorative techniques favored in the central palace workshops of Akhetaten during the final years of Akhenaten's reign.

<!-- Page 108 -->
Traces of erased cartouches are visible on the foot and lid of the middle coffin. The original inscription bore the prenomen Ankhkheperure and the nomen Neferneferuaten. The original hieroglyphs had been scraped away and replaced with the names of Tutankhamun, but the palimpsest remains clearly legible under oblique lighting, demonstrating beyond doubt that this masterwork was appropriated.

<!-- Page 112 -->
The quartzite sarcophagus (Carter no. 240) exhibits multiple anomalies, including the fact that its pink granite lid does not belong to the box and was cracked in antiquity, then repaired with plaster. The corner goddesses—Isis, Nephthys, Neith, and Selket—were originally carved without wings, their protective wings having been added in a secondary recarving phase that cut across earlier decorative borders.

<!-- Page 118 -->
The four miniature gold coffins (Carter no. 266g) that held the king's mummified viscera were similarly adapted. In 1973, J. R. Harris demonstrated that their interior inscriptions originally named Ankhkheperure Neferneferuaten. The feminine epithet Akhet-en-hyes ('effective for her husband') proves conclusively that the original owner was a female monarch ruling in the immediate aftermath of Akhenaten.

<!-- Page 122 -->
A conservative assessment suggests that between seventy and eighty per cent of Tutankhamun's ritual funerary equipment was second-hand, having been expropriated from earlier Amarna royals. The sudden death of the young king before his own funerary apparatus was finished forced the court officials, headed by the divine father Ay, to requisition pre-existing funerary equipment from royal storerooms.
