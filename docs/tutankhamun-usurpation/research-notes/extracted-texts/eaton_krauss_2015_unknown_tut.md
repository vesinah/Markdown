# The Unknown Tutankhamun
## Marianne Eaton-Krauss
### London: Bloomsbury Academic, 2015

<!-- Page 78 -->
The nest of three anthropoid coffins contained within the stone sarcophagus in KV 62 constitutes one of the greatest triumphs of ancient Egyptian woodworking and metallurgy, but it is simultaneously a document of severe political and domestic improvisation. Rather than a carefully pre-planned mortuary ensemble, the contents of the burial chamber reflect rapid assembly in the wake of an unanticipated royal death.

<!-- Page 81 -->
The second coffin (Carter no. 254) displays a style of carving and inlay entirely incompatible with that of the outer and inner coffins. The face is clearly not that of Tutankhamun, but resembles closely the portraiture associated with the late Amarna coregent. The delicate modeling of the lips and chin, together with the intense polychrome glass inlay, belongs firmly to the artistic tradition of Akhetaten.

<!-- Page 85 -->
Caution is warranted, however, when attempting to identify the original owner of these usurped monuments. While the cartouches point to Ankhkheperure, scholars remain sharply divided as to whether this ruler was Nefertiti, Meritaten, or a male prince such as Smenkhkare. The epigraphic and physical evidence demonstrates reuse, but attribution to a specific historic individual must be weighed with philological rigor.

<!-- Page 92 -->
The reuse of royal funerary equipment in the burial of Tutankhamun must not be viewed as an act of desecration or hostility; in ancient Egyptian mortuary practice, the repurposing of available royal furniture was an established response to unforeseen dynastic crises. The young king needed an appropriate royal burial within the canonical seventy-day period, and existing state reserves were drawn upon to satisfy this urgent religious mandate.
