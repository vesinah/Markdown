Theory and Practice of Mantra 
in the  
Esoteric Theravāda Mahānikāya Tradition *  
 
 
Pedro Manuel Castro Sánchez 
 
 
Introduction 
 
Between the years 1965 and 1975, the French scholar François Bizot discovered 
the existence of a Theravāda esoteric tradition established in some areas of the 
Indochinese peninsula and with a special relevance in Cambodia, called ‘ancient 
Mahānikāya’, that will be denominated here as ‘Theravāda Mahānikāya’ (hereafter 
TMN).1 Despite sharing a common goal, the TMN distinguishes itself significantly from 
Sri Lankan Mahāvihāra and Thai Dhammayutikanikāya traditions, both being based on 
the Theravāda’s Pāli Canon and its interpretation by Buddhaghosa. TMN’s distinctive 
characteristics are related to its assimilation of certain elements of Hindu (Brahmanical, 
Ṥaiva and Tantric), Buddhist (Vajrayāna) and Indochinese animistic origins (RBK I: 
31), what lead it to be named as ‘Tantric Theravāda’ (RBK V: 16). However, I consider 
such denomination as problematic. By one side, Bizot acknowledges that the TMN’s 
Tantra is not comparable to the Tantric traditions already known, that is, Hindu and 
Buddhist ones, but unfortunately, he does not goes to analyzing which would be those 
differences and close the issue considering it as an enigma ‘that defies any 
identification’ (RBK IV: 108). And by another, Harris negates the suitability to call the 
TMN as ‘Tantric’ or including any Ṥaiva, Vaiṣṇava and Mahāyāna Buddhist influences, 
because: 1) its terminology and doctrinal background are Theravāda, and 2) there is no 
mention of any characteristic deities belonging to Hindu and Buddhist Tantra in their 
textual sources. So, Harris concludes that TMN is ‘an indigenous Southeast Asian 
tradition that may have come under ill-defined external influences in the course of its 
development’ (Harris, 2005: 94). 
I would consider both views as unsatisfactory and rather slightly ‘extremist’ 
ones, because Bizot is asserting an unverifiable ‘Tantricism’ and Harris denies it 
because does not share any similarity with those established as Hindu and Buddhist 
ones. For this reason, I will suggest a third view dealing with the enigma of the TMN’s 
Tantric assimilation critically assessing its more apparent than real dissociation from

practices within a rigorous Theravāda soteriological framework: ‘to break the five

I.- Historical links between TMN and Hindu/Buddhist Tantra 
 
Originally, TMN lacked any denomination of its own, it was simply the form of 
Theravāda Buddhism implanted in Cambodia and bordering areas from 13th century CE 
to the first part of 19th century CE, that is, TMN was introduced into Cambodia a 
century before the falling of Angkor empire (14th century CE) (RBK I: 32). However, 
from 1854, the reformist and westernized Dhammayutikanikāya school, of Thai origin 
and founded in 1829, was introduced into Cambodia (RBK I: 4-8). Hence, to distinguish 
TMN itself from this new school, was created the designation of ‘Great Nikāya’ 
(Mahānikāya), or ‘the Nikāya of those who keep practicing as long ago’ 
(Āciṇṇakappikanikāya) (RBK IV: 103), also designated as ‘unreformed Mahānikāya’ 
(RBK III: 4).4 The description of ‘great’ alludes to its geographic area, coinciding with 
the Siam’s influence extension: Cambodia, Lanna (northern Thailand), Laos, Sipsong 
Panna (southern China) and Chiang Thung (Burma) (RBK IV: 103; Lagirarde, 1994: 63, 
n. 1). 
Such is TMN’s wide area the hypothesis has been raised of its Burmese origin, 
concretely Môn, that, through Thailand, it was introduced into Cambodia at the 
beginning of 12th century CE (RBK I: 32; RBK IV: 103, 119). However, the Theravāda 
presence in Môn land dates from 5th century CE and towards the half of 11th century 
CE, some Môn belonging to Mahāvihāra took the place to the Buddhist/tantric Āri 
stream from Burma, which was established in Pagan several centuries before (Harris, 
2005: 23). In return for this, there are strong indications of an Āri influence on the TMN 
origin. Āri tradition consisted basically in a Mahāyāna/Tantra synthesis emphasizing 
mantra recitation, snake-deities cult (Skt nāga), alchemy, magic, medicine and sexual 
rituals. Āri’s area covered Burma, northern Thailand and Cambodia, and it is quite 
plausible that the Āri tradition served as prototype for other heterodox movements 
within Indochina, especially the TMN, because the striking similarities between Āri and 
TMN traditions.5 From the 16th century CE onwards, Āri changed their designation, 
being described as ‘boxer-monks’ owing to their martial skills and they still remain 
nowadays, scattered through remote areas of northern parts of Burma and Thailand 
(RBK I: 36-42).6

While Āri influence on TMN seems more obvious than the Môn one, is there  
any indication linking the Indochinese TMN with Indian Buddhism? Here I will focus 
on three approaches: the influence of Sinhalese Abhayagirivāsins, Angkor’s religious 
syncretism and some Vajrayāna teachers from Nālandā Buddhist university 
(northeastern India) belonging to the Mūlasarvāstivāda Vinaya, who emigrated to 
Cambodia (ancient Kamboja) during 12th century CE (Chimpa/Chattopadhyaya, 1970: 
319, 330).  
It has been pointed out a textual Abhayagirivāsin influence on TMN through the 
Vimuttimagga, especially for a passage in which is described the weekly development 
of fetus and eighty groups of worms lying within the human body (RBK I: 25-26; VM: 
173-176), paralleling another passage appearing in a TMN text (MBK: 86-87; 108-110). 
However, nothing has been said on a more direct Abhayagirivāsin influence on TMN. 
On this point, there is archaeological evidence of the Abhayagirivāsin presence in Java 
(ancient Ṥrīvijaya) in 792 CE, where the Abhayagirivāsins introduced a Vajrayāna 
focused on the Sarvatathāgatatattvasaṃgraha (STTS) tantric Scripture (Sundberg, 
2004: 96; 110-116).7 
That the STTS was transmitted from Java to Angkor by Abhayagirivāsin monks 
seems likely, because there is epigraphic evidence of Kīrtipaṇḍita, who introduced the 
STTS in Angkor as Vajrayāna master for the royal family of Jayavarman V (c. 968-
1001) (Sanderson, 2003-2004: 427, n. 284).8 Moreover, during Sūryavarman I’s reign 
(c. 1002-1050 CE), an inscription from Sab Bāk mentions Vajrayāna deities as the five 
Buddhas (Skt pañcasugata) and Vajrasattva (Harris, 2005: 17). As will be seen below, 
such notion of five Buddhas will be assimilated by TMN from its own perspective, what 
shows a likely link between Abhayagirivāsins and TMN through the STTS’s Vajrayāna 
established in Angkor.9 
It seems that the Angkor’s Ṥaiva/Buddhist syncretism wielded a key influence 
on the TMN formation. Sūryavarman I’s Lopburi inscription mentions two kinds of 
Buddhist monks, Sthavira and Mahāyāna ones, sharing their residences together with 
Brahmanical ascetics focused on mantra recitation (Skt tāpasvi yogi) (RBK IV: 111-
112; Harris, 2005: 17). Likewise, in 1296 CE, the Chinese traveler Zhou Daguan 
noticed a close cohabitation between Brahmanical erudites (Skt paṇḍita), Ṥaiva 
worshipers of the liṇga and Theravāda monks (RBK I: 39; Harris, 2005: 24-25). Such 
interrelationship was so close, that Buddhist monks and tāpasvi officiated identical rites 
and eventually, the tāpasvi adopted the ten Buddhist precepts (Skt daśaśīla), becoming 
in this way a modality of Buddhist asceticism which blended tantric and Theravāda

Moreover, it is evident that there might have been a Mūlasarvāstivādin influence

Lanna and Laos. Those communities have preserved some features reflecting a 
Mūlasarvāstivāda/Vajrayāna’s heritage from Nālandā: the enthronement of senior

as ‘Dharma monarch’ through the sprinkling of ‘water from the five oceans’ 
(Strickmann, 1996: 50). Moreover, one of the distinctive emblems of Lanna/Laos 
bhikkhus is a stick with hanging metallic rings, being used during their alms rounds. 
This kind of stick was used exclusively by Indian Mūlasarvāstivāda monks and their 
Central Asian, Tibetan and Chinese heirs (Bizot, 2000: 521-522). On a textual level, 
there is evidence of a Mūlasarvāstivādin influence on the Saddavimala, a key TMN text 
focused exoterically to the Kaccāyana’s Pāli grammar, but esoterically dealing with the 
formation of a ‘Dhamma body’ made from the creative power of Pāli syllables 
(Bizot/Lagirarde, 1996: 49-50, 67-72). This relevant topic will be treated on part II. 
According to those data, it is possible to deduce a significant Brahmanical, 
Śaiva, and Hindu/Buddhist tantric influence on the TMN’s formative period, that 
approximately happened simultaneously to the last three centuries of Angkor’s empire 
(from 12th to 14th centuries CE), being the period of its highest splendour and later 
decline. The question is: what were the conditions for TMN of being able to preserve 
those influences within a Theravāda framework from 12th century CE to the 20th 
century CE? It has been argued that the main reason for the TMN survival in Cambodia 
lies in ‘its relative remoteness of centralized reforming bureaucracy from the lives of the 
majority of the population’ (Harris, 2005: 94). While such factor seems likely, however, 
it sounds to me a rather simplistic interpretation. For this reason, I would like to add two 
more hypothetical factors trying to explain the TMN survival in Cambodia until 1975. 
There is an external factor, where TMN origin merges together with the creation of the 
religious and national identity of Cambodia. As was the case with Tibet and Japan, 
where Vajrayāna was introduced during the formative period of their religious and 
national identities (Strickmann, 1996: 231), likewise, the Angkor’s Brahmanical, Śaiva, 
and Mahāyāna/Vajrayāna synthesis shaped the religious and national identity of 
Cambodia. Hence, if TMN wanted to remain there, it had to do it adapting creatively

II.- Theory and Practice of Mantra in TMN 
 
There are two key points that will help to understand the theoretical background 
lying behind the TMN mantra practice: the analogical continuity between the 
supramundane and mundane levels of reality and the identification of the supramundane 
level with the intra-physic and intra-uterine dimension of the individual. Mahāyāna 
Buddhism recognizes correspondence/analogy (Skt upamāna) as a valid medium of 
knowledge, and especially Vajrayāna developed correspondence systems based mainly 
on three and five factors, where are established connections between supramundane 
principles (Buddhas, mantras, etc.) and mundane ones (aggregates, defilements, etc.) 
(Wayman, 1990: 30-31). The meaning lying behind upamāna is that mundane 
categories contain naturally the potentiality of becoming supramundane ones, or 
expressed from an alternative view: ‘it is possible to use negative mental states to help 
traverse the path’ (Williams/Tribe, 2000: 201).  
 
Likewise, analogical knowledge is the fundamental structure lying behind TMN 
(Bizot, 1981b: 167), where ‘elements are connected between them not through a causal 
link, but because of a similarity’s relationship’ (Becchetti, 1991: 57). As will be seen 
below, TMN establishes correspondence sets mainly based on three, five and seven 
factors, emphasizing the similarity between Buddhas, mantras and Pāli syllables, with 
elements, bodily organs, etc. In the same line, TMN corresponds the supramundane 
dimension (P lokuttara), not with a metaphysical abstraction, but with the ‘intra-uterine 
world’ (P loka-udara): ‘the august supramundane dhammas are born into the motherly 
matrix’ (RBK I: 120). As will be seen below, this same place establishes the psycho-
physical basis of a yoga practice focused on a subtle physiology. 
 
According to this, the goal of TMN mantra practice is clear: accomplishing the 
Buddha’s body-mind which is already underlying as a potentiality within the initiated 
body-mind. In this sense, besides of conceiving a ‘Dhamma body’ (P Dhamma-kāya) 
according to the mainstream Theravāda approach of ‘the Buddha is truly “embodied” in 
the dhamma, rather than in his physical person’ (Harrison, 1992: 50), TMN is 
emphasizing a ‘Dhamma body’ conceived as ‘the whole of ideals (dhamma) located into 
the body (kāya)’ (RBK III: 45). That is, TMN is asserting that the initiated’s body 
unfolds a set of correspondences between his/her 32 bodily formations (P ākāra), the 32 
consonants of the Pāli syllabary giving origin to those bodily formations, the 32 
contemplative ‘basis to action’ (P mūlakammaṭṭhāna) and the 32 Buddha’s bodily 
marks (P lakkhaṇa). The key factor linking those correspondences is mūlakammaṭṭhāna, 
interpreted by TMN, not in the conventional Theravāda sense of ‘subjects of meditation 
and methods of practice’ (Vajirañāna, 1962: 29), but of kammaṭṭhāna as ‘body’, ‘the 
only work place available to the meditator for his/her own action’, and mūla as 
‘embryo’, ‘perceived as body’s origin and basis’. Moreover, mūla designates the root 
syllables originating and composing the Dhamma (Bizot, 1992: 29-30, 34-35 and 293-
300).12

To accomplish such Buddha/Dhamma-kāya, the Yogāvacara initiated needs 
passing through a complex process of memorizing his/her own embryonic formation 
together with its syllabic correspondences, building up a new body composed by the 
syllables’s phonetic energy, to internalize such verbal body within himself/herself and 
locating it into the lower abdomen to the navel’s level, and using this body as a 
immortal vehicle that would replace the ordinary body after death (Bizot/Lagirarde, 
1996: 38). Below I will show three key mantras which are playing a pivotal role in this 
process, but now, I will focus briefly on the Vedic/tantric background underlying TMN 
mantra theory and practice.  
 
Vedic tradition conceived ultimate reality (Skt Brahman) as word/sound (Skt 
śabda) having the power (Skt śakti) to create beings and worlds, especially through its 
indivisible phonetic unities: the syllables (Skt akṣara). Because their indivisibility, 
akṣaras are identical ‘to the imperishable, the indestructible, the eternal’. Akṣaras are 
endowed with a creative energy and their expressive power lies behind the enunciated 
objects: ‘the Word precedes the object, it brings it forth, it is the energy that upholds it, 
its innermost nature, that into which it will dissolve at the time of the cosmic 
reasorption’ (Padoux, 1990: 50-51). And the most powerful and effective modality of 
akṣaras are the mantras, considered as the ultimate reality in the form of sound, 
syllables and words (Bhattacharya, 1999: 416). According to this, mantras are the 
unchanging vibratory models form which are derived deities, beings and things. 
Mantras are composed by individual syllables or set of syllables of the same frequency 
as the being to which it appertains, hence, a wise use of mantra ‘is considered capable of 
desintegrating the object with which it is in vibratory accord, or of impelling deities to 
emit their divine influence’, that is, ‘mantra possesses the same kind of creative force 
which was present at the creation of the universe’ (Gonda, 1975: 264, 279). Later on, 
Hindu Tantra would develop a complex system of phonetic emanations in which the 
Sanskrit syllabary (Skt varṇapatha) is able to create beings and worlds from the ‘matrix 
energy’ (Skt mātṛkā) underlying every syllable. Likewise, mātṛkās play a key role 
within the tantric practice of the ‘ritual imposition of mantras’ (Skt nyāsa) (Padoux, 
1990: 51, 148). As will be seen below, both aspects were assimilated by TMN. 
 
While it is true that TMN personified the Dhamma, transforming it into a 
creative principle (MPBDL.33.2: 247), an idea that is ‘completely alien to canonical 
Theravāda Buddhism’ (Crosby, 2000: 195, n. 65), it is not less true that TMN likes to 
emphasize a human onto-genesis/embryo-genesis produced in any time, instead of an 
universal cosmogony happening ‘at the beginning of the time’. Thus, some TMN texts 
locate the embryo-genesis within an indeterminate time (MBK: 74), while in others, is 
located during the ‘auspicious cosmic cycle’ (P. bhaddakappa) within the conditioned 
existence (SV.3.1-2: 212). Likewise, TMN mantras show a different formal structure 
from those belonging to the Hindu/Buddhist tantric traditions. None of them begins with 
the syllable OṂ or finish with syllabes as SVĀHĀ, HŪṂ, or PHAṬ either, as is the 
case with the mantra SAṂ VI DHĀ PU KA YA PA.13 However, at least two pivotal

Lastly, mantra NA MO BU DDHĀ YA (cf. Chart 3, below) synthesizes the 
mundane and supramundane dimensions of the whole TMN. In first place, it is 
significant its quinary Buddhas’s classification, that, as far as I know, it is exclusively 
elaborated by Vajrayāna tradition and is utterly alien to Theravāda Buddhism, which 
emphasizes instead classifications of four Buddhas and especially, that of the seven past 
Buddhas (Hōbōgirin III, 1974: 195-197). While Vajrayāna elaborated a quinary system 
including supramundane Buddhas, that is, Vairocana, Akṣobhya, Ratnasambhava, 
Amitābha and Amoghasiddhi (Williams/Tribe, 2000: 211), TMN from its own side and 
being faithful to its Theravāda background, elaborated a quinary one with five historical 
Buddhas, including the future Buddha Metteyya to fit into the system (cf. Chart 3-b).

Another aspect worth noticing is the group of qualities (guṇa) (see Chart 3-f), 
especially those making reference to the 12 liquid organs and 21 solid ones transmitted 
by parents, that in fact, they are created by the vibratory power of the 33 consonants of 
the Pāli syllabary, that equally, they are integrating the five aggregates from a material 
perspective (SV.5.1-5: 215-216). Those correspondences syllables-organs and locating 
the five Buddhas into several bodily places (see Chart 3-c), strongly suggest a link to 
the tantric practice of ‘imposing mantras on the body (nyāsa), having as purpose 
impregnate and finally transform the ordinary body into a ‘body of the ultimate reality’. 
Thus, one of the main nyāsa designed for this goal is the ritual imposition of the whole 
mātṛkās of Sanskrit syllabary (Padoux, 1980: 63-64, 87-89). While TMN texts are 
referring to a syllable recitation, it is quite likely that mantra recitation was preceded by 
a nyāsa and its further visualization during mantra recitation.19 
Finally, the ’10 doors’ (see Chart 3-f) are summing up the beginning of the 
Yogāvacara path as an embryo and its last phase as a Buddha, because those ‘doors’ are 
respectively analogous to: mouth, two eyes, two ears, two nasal orifices, urinary meatus, 
anus and crown of head, that likewise, are related to the syllables SAṂ VI DHĀ PU KA 
YA PA MA A U. TMN yogic physiology identifies the breath’s descent-retention into 
the coccyx (anus) with the Arahanthood, and the breath’s ascent-retention into the 
crown of head with the opening to Nibbāna (RBK II: 248-249). For this reason, those 
ten ‘openings’ are also designated as ‘the ten doors of the Buddha’s body’ (MBK: 101, 
n. 2). 
 
Conclusions 
 
 
While Theravāda Buddhism, as it was systematized by Buddhaghosa, presents 
itself as an orthodox, unritualized, exoteric and demystified version of Dhamma, TMN 
by contrast, shows an alternative version where ritual, esotericism and myth are 
creatively blended, displaying a more complex and varied picture of the diversity 
underlying Southern Buddhism. Thanks to the painstaking efforts of F. Bizot, the reality 
of an alternative version of Theravāda developed in Cambodia was made evident to the 
world. Despite of being improperly described as ‘tantric’, being instead the 
denomination of ‘esoteric’ the most fitting one given its oral and textual self-definition, 
yet, TMN shows evidence of a significant Hindu/Buddhist tantric influence. However, 
such influence was reworked by the TMN representatives with a subtle skillfulness, 
taking from the tantric tradition only those features fitting well to their soteriological 
project that always remained, at least to the letter, faithful to the Pāli Canon. Two of the 
key tantric elements assimilated by TMN are the analogical knowledge and the 
internalization of the unconditioned within the human body along its embryological 
development. And besides these elements, TMN also assimilated the methods to

Buddha’s teachings, may be equally certain that the Tipiṭaka is issued from an 
enlightened way of breathing as well, although as it is the case with TMN, such
