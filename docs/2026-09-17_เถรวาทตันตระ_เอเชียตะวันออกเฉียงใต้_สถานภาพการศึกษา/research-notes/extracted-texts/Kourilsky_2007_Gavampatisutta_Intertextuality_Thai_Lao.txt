Introduction
The Khavampattisut (ຄະວັມປັດຕິສູດ; “the 
sutta of Gavampati”) is a Pali–Lao text 
recounting the teachings of the Buddha’s 
disciple Gavampati. It is preserved 
in two extant manuscripts from Laos 
(formerly the kingdom of Lan Xang) 
and exists in numerous related versions 
circulating in Thailand under various 
Siamese and Pali titles. Although it shares 
its name with a Pali canonical discourse 
in the Saṁyuttanikāya (S V 436–37), these 
texts differ significantly in content, 
structure, and narrative scope. This 
contrast reflects the broader divergence 
between Gavampati’s marginal role in 
early Pali tradition and his far greater 
prominence in later Thai–Lao Buddhism.
In the Pali scriptures, Gavampati 
appears among the early converts of 
the Buddha (Vin I 15–20; see also John

remains a secondary figure within the

Keywords: Filial Piety; Gavampatisutta; Manuscript Transmission; Pali Nissaya 
Literature; Thai–Lao Buddhism
Abstract―Reflecting his prominence in Thai–Lao Buddhism, the 
Buddha’s disciple Gavampati is the subject of a corpus of texts recounting 
the religious career of the “Fat Monk” and culminating in his final extinction 
(parinibbāna). Although these narratives have received some scholarly 
attention, closer study of their manuscript transmission and textual 
features invites a reassessment of their structure, intertextual relationships, 
and circulation. This article examines the Gavampatisutta within the 
broader context of Tai Buddhist literary production, highlighting its 
links with Pali canonical and post-canonical traditions, the role of nissaya 
literature in vernacular transmission, and the articulation of themes such 
as merit-making and filial piety across Thai–Lao communities.

since, as Lagirarde (2000) later demon-

a literary genre in their own right 
within the Thai–Lao Buddhist corpus, 
especially in Siam (central Thailand).7
	
This genre, attested in both monolingual 
Pali and vernacular compositions, may 
be regarded as a subcategory of sutra 
(sutta), as some titles clearly suggest 
(see below). The Khavampattisut is com-
posed in vernacular Lao, more precisely 
in the form of a nissaya: the main text, 
written in Lao, is interspersed with 
Pali terms or passages that are glossed 
or explained in the vernacular.8 Such 
bilingual Pali–vernacular compositions, 
or “bitexts”―a term coined by Trent 
Walker (2022a: 271)―constitute a major 
component of the Buddhist manuscript 
corpus not only in Laos, but also in 
Thailand, Myanmar, and Cambodia.
	
Lagirarde was the first to offer a 
study and French translation of the 
Khavampattisut (2001: 321–443), more 
than 80 years after its initial mention by 
Finot. He also compared this Lao nissaya 
with related versions of the Gavampati 
narrative from central Thailand, where 
the text exists both in monolingual Pali 
and in Siamese translation. No Pali– 
Siamese nissaya, however, was identified 
in his survey.

Gavampatisutta and Khavampattisut:  
A Comparison
The relationship between the Pali 
Gavampatisutta preserved in Siamese 
manuscript collections and the Lao 
Khavampattisut―a vernacular nissaya 
version of the same narrative―can be 
clarified through a close comparison of 
their structure, language, and textual 
features. 
	
Considering the various extant 
versions of the narrative of Gavampati 
in Siam and Laos, the question arises 
as to which served as the basis for the 
others. The most straightforward 
assumption is that the Pali version 
preceded the vernacular ones, which 
would then represent translations or 
adaptations. Some caution is nevertheless 
required, since in mainland Southeast 
Asia it is not uncommon for Pali texts 
to have been reconstructed from 
vernacular 
compositions.17 
Only 
a 
close comparison between the Pali and 
vernacular versions can shed light on 
this issue. Such a comparison is now 
possible thanks to the available critical 
edition of the monolingual Pali Gavam-

Despite their titles, the Gavampatisutta 
and the Khavampattisut do not conform 
to the structure of a classical sutra. 
They do not begin with the standard 
Pali formula evaṁ me sutaṁ ekaṁ 
samayaṁ bhagavā […] viharati (“Thus I 
have heard: at one time the Blessed One 
was residing […]”), which traditionally 
signals that the discourse was “heard” 
(suta) by an auditor―often identified as 
the disciple Ānanda―from the Buddha 
himself. Whereas canonical sutras 
typically depict the Dhamma as spoken 
by the Buddha, often in verse (gāthās), 
the Thai–Lao narratives place such 
teachings in the mouth of Gavampati 
(Lagirarde 2001: 328).

The suffering endured by the mother 
during gestation and childbirth is 
addressed in several canonical and post-
canonical works―e.g., M I 266, Ja 532, 
Vism 305. However, this theme is 
particularly developed in Buddhist 
literature composed in mainland 
Southeast Asia.56 It appears, for instance, 
in the Maṅg-d, which explicitly draws 
on the Soṇandajātaka (Ja 532). Vernacular 
meditation manuals―such as the Mul 
preah kammatthan (មូូលព្រះ1ះកម្មដ្ឋាាន) in Cam-
bodia and the Bualaphantha (ບົົວຣະພັັນທະ) 
in Laos and Lan Na―remind the practi-
tioner (yogāvacara) of the suffering 
their mother endure for their children 
(Lagirarde 1994: 68). Some exercises even 
require the yogāvacara to reproduce 
these sufferings through a series of 
ascetic practices: sleeping only in a 
sitting position represents the discomfort 
experienced during pregnancy; not 
sheltering from the sun evokes the pain 
endured when “being on fire” after 
childbirth, etc. (Bernon 2000: 475). The 
torments of the mother also feature in 
the Khmer “Prayer of Repentance” 
(sma lia, ស្មាារលា; see Bernon 2000: 748, 
757; also Walker 2022b: 71–79) and in 
certain Lao formulas for “recalling the 
vital spirits” (su khwan, ສູ່່�ຂວັັນ).57

common to the entire region, transmitted 
over the centuries either through the 
circulation of texts or through oral 
transmission from master to disciple.

Concluding Remarks
Beyond its philological implications, the 
present case study offers broader insights 
into the historical circulation of texts 
and the dynamics of Buddhist literary 
production in mainland Southeast Asia. 
It highlights the role of regional centers 
in shaping both the transmission and 
transformation of Pali and vernacular 
traditions.
	
Close philological and comparative 
examination of the Thai–Lao Gavampati 
narratives indeed confirms our initial 
hypothesis that the Pali version predates 
the nissaya and other vernacular adap-
tations. Given the terminus ante quem 
provided by the dated copy of the Lao 
Khavampattisut (1697/8 ce), the compo-
sition of the Pali Gavampatisutta must 
go back, at the latest, to the end of the 
17th century. The wide diffusion of this 
narrative―attested in both Pali and 
Siamese versions preserved in collections 
from central Thailand―and its relative 
absence in northern regions suggest that 
it was most likely composed in Ayutthaya, 
then the capital of Siam, where the 
composition of original Pali texts is well 
documented (Skilling & Santi 2002).
	
The story of Gavampati had thus 
already circulated well beyond the 
borders of the Ayutthaya kingdom prior 
to its destruction by the Burmese in 
1767. It was evidently present in Lan 
Xang before the fragmentation of the 
Lao kingdom into several principalities 
(ca. 1707 ce). This pattern likely extends 
to other texts belonging to the 
so-called nibbāna genre, which are 
abundant in Siam but comparatively 
rare in present-day Laos. For instance, 
only a small number of manuscripts 
of the Mahākassapanibbāna have been 
recorded in Lao collections, whereas 
numerous copies―both in monolingual 
Pali and in Pali–Siamese nissaya―are 
preserved in central Thai collections 
(Lagirarde 2006: 108–110).58 As in the 
case of the Lao Khavampattisut, the Lao 
Mahakatsapaniphan (ມະຫາກັດສະປະນິບພານ; 
“the nibbāna of Mahākassapa”) appears to 
be a nissaya derived from a monolingual 
Pali version (Mahākassapanibbānasutta) 
that most likely originated in Siam.59
