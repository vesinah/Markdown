NEW DISCOVERIES of ancient gold or silver treasures
have often made world headlines. However, prehistoric
gold masks are not everyday finds. Even in the case of the
metal-age cultures on the north coast of Peru, where the
most gold masks have come to light, more recent finds of
these distinguished art treasures remain still of special in-
terest. While in 2007, Vietnamese newspapers even re-
ported on two gold masks found in Bulgaria!

From the Old World one thinks immediately of the
mask of Agamemnon (about 1500 BC) which the German
Heinrich Schliemann, the father of Mycenaean archaeol-
ogy, discovered in Mykene in December 1876. Or of the
gold mask of the Pharaoh Tutankhamun, who died as a
young man at the age of about twenty at the beginning of
1323 BC. We also know of some 6th and 5th centuries BC
gold masks from Macedonia; the 3rd—2nd century BC
gold eye masks from Tajikistan, as also 5th century AD
face masks found in Kirgizia and 5th and 6th century Xin-
jiang from the Tian Shan mountain area, which document
connections between the West and China along the Silk
Road. As we go further these precious items tend to be
later, such as the gold funerary face masks of the Liao dy-
nasty (907-1125 AD) of northern China.

Thus, the discovery by Vietnamese archaeologists of
the first prehistoric gold masks in the whole country, two
in the spring of 2003 and a third in 2005 during two exca-
vations at Giong Lon burial site near Vung Tau in south
Vietnam in the midst of picturesque salt fields, close to
the seashore, is surely important and might have been ex-
pected to receive similar attention. However, all three
2000 years old gold masks were surprisingly shelved in the
storage of the provincial museum.

In 2004 Vietnam News Agency published a short an-
nouncement of this newly discovered archaeological site:
“Ba Ria~Vung Tau (VNA)—As many as 1638 artefacts,
believed to belong to the Oc Eo culture some 2000 years
ago, have been unearthed at the Giong Lon relic site in
Long Son commune of Vung Tau city in southern Ba
Ria—Vung Tau province. These artefacts, which were in-
troduced to the public in Vung Tau on July 28th by the
National Museum of Vietnamese History, Hanoi, and
the provincial museum, include pots, vases, bracelets, ear-
rings, swords and daggers made of various materials such
as stone, bronze, pottery, glass and gold.”

In 2007 and some months later in 2008, two reports on
both excavations in 2003 and 2005 of the Giong Lon site
were published in Vietnamese. The three gold masks of
both campaigns were illustrated (very small) with other
gold ornaments and were briefly described.! Because both
archaeological journals are only sold in Hanoi, these ex-
citing objects remained “undiscovered” for the general
audience. And thus, it is hardly surprising, that by the
summer of 2008 the three gold masks of Giong Lon were
even completely unknown to many Vietnamese archaeol-
ogists.

Consulting the director of the provincial museum of
Vung Tau, Mr Pham Chi Than in August 2008, we dis-
covered the reason for the restricted information on the
spectacular discoveries—the authorities are afraid of
tomb looters. Generously, the excavator of the Giong
Lon site, Mr Vu Quoc Hien, acting director of the Na-
tional Museum of Vietnamese History in Hanoi, made
pictures available from the archaeological site and of two

Of course, these three masks from Giong Lon site, are
more modest than the masks of Agamemnon, or of Tu-
tankhamun. They measure only 10.9 x 4.5 cm, 9.7 x 6.0
cm (both masks from 2003, following No. 1 and 2) and
9.1 x 7.5 cm (2005, No. 3) and are made from thin gold
metal sheet. The first two specimens (No. | and 2) are not
masks which covered the whole face of the dead person,
but only of the eyes and nose. The third one shows bushy
eyebrows, wide eyes, a great nose and a mouth with full
lips. If the masks really do show the faces naturalistically,
then their owners must have had relatively narrow skulls.
All four corners of the mask No. | (1) have a small
punched hole. The same seems to be so with the face
mask No. 3 (3), although two corners have not survived.
Mask No. 2 (2) has three holes along the edge at both
sides. These holes point to the possibility that the masks
were attached to a wood or cloth base, similar to the gold
plaques of the Scythians.?

How did the first masks of Vietnam come to the sand
dunes of Giong Lon in the midst of the picturesque salt
fields near Vung Tau? The first impression is deceptive,
as the burials and masks have nothing to do with the salt
fields, which came to this region of Ba Ria~Vung Tau
later after the end of the 17th century, established by Chi-
nese salt makers from Canton.* Of gold deposits in south
Vietnam, nothing has been found. Only some places with
alluvial gold are known, whose meaning in that period re-
mains unclear. The raw gold would have been acquired
from central Vietnam and Cambodia or from farther
away. However, up to now, no gold masks have ever been
discovered in these areas.

In East Java some gold masks were found and, as is well
known, Indonesia owned some very profitable gold mines.
However, the prehistoric gold masks from Java of unes-
tablished date show no similarities to the Vietnamese
items.* Another “fanciful eyes-nose gold mask found on a
skull from the central Bisayas” in the Philippines has been
mentioned. This mask is said to have been excavated by
the Philippine National Museum at Oton Iloilo and has
been dated in the 14th to 15th century.°

Quite obviously, the masks arrived in the Vung Tau
area as a result of long-distance trade. Giong Lon site
owes its unusual archaeological finds to its top location
only 10 km away from the most southern point of the pen-
insula of Vung Tau—Cap St. Jaques (4, 5). This hill, 245
metres high, was like a landmark for early navigators,
and certainly also the determining reason why the interior
behind the bay of Ganh Rei at Prei Nokor (“village in the
forest”) could have developed as an important Cambodian
trading centre. As of the 18th century it flourished, along
with Saigon the major economic centre of south Vietnam.
Indeed, it can be said, Saigon would not have existed until
today, if like a gigantic magnet the hill at the south point
of Vung Tau had not attracted all ships coming from far
distant seas.

Already Zhou Daguan, who travelled in 1296—1297 as
a member of a Chinese legation from China to Cambo-
dia, had mentioned in his Customs of Cambodia the area of
Vung Tau as a subsequent station after departing Quy
Nhon. He called it Zhen Pu, located at the border be-
tween Champa and Cambodia (6).

John Crawfurd and George Finlayson described in their
travel memoirs the special situation of this corner of the
land quite impressively. Together they sailed in August
1822 from Siam along the coast of Cochinchina to the
north with bearing to Tourane (Danang). Who sails like
these past seafarers from the island of Pulo Condore
(today Con Son) and passes the 300 km long extremely
low-lying south Vietnamese lands of the Mekong Delta,
finds with Vung Tau the first visible from afar orientation
point and with the Coconut Bay (4) an ideal, well pro-
tected shelter and resting place.®

However, this headland is more than just a landmark
with a snug anchorage for seafarers. From here it is only a
short jump to the mouths of the largest rivers of this re-
gion. To the estuary of the Soai Rap which leads to the
Saigon and Dong Nai River in the north direction; to the
Vam Co Dong and Vam Co Tay River in the northwest;
and last but not least to the northernmost Mekong arm
via the west. Here in the Vung Tau area, anchored ships

The first grave goods came to light by chance in 2002 at
the Giong Lon dune site in Long Son community, when
the inhabitants were collecting sand. From March to May
2003, an excavation of five units with a total area of 344
sq. m was carried out, followed by a second campaign
in June 2005 of two units consisting of 192 sq. m. Alto-
gether, seventy-two inhumations and seven jar burials
were discovered in the sandy soil (7). From the skeletons
nothing remained, but from the arrangements of the
burial offerings, the excavators presume either an east-
west or a north-south orientation of the bodies. However,
it is not often clearly recognisable. As at many other burial
sites in this region, the graves were equipped with numer-
ous funeral potteries such as globular vessels, food bowls,
goblets or vases. Moreover, the excavators listed more
than two thousand pieces of jewellery, mostly being glass
beads of different colours, as well as 178 gold beads or
beads made from carnelian, rock crystal, garnet or clay.
Besides nephrite, carnelian and rock crystal bracelets, a
double-winding gold wire (length 2 cm) with a phallic-
shaped end was found.

Regarding this rich collection of jewellery, one is
amazed by the small number of iron and bronze objects.
Only twenty-two iron offerings were discovered (a twisted
sword, a lance-head, three daggers, eight knives and nine
chisels). Only two bronzes, a rattle and a Wu Shu coin,
were found. Publications up to now give only fragmentary
information about the offerings, and provide just some
pictures of selected finds and plans.

three gold masks we are informed so far about only two

About 7 km to the north of Bit Meas, in spring 2007,
one of the richest prehistoric burial sites of Southeast
Asia was found in Prohear village. During the period up
to the beginning of 2008, the villagers dug up the whole
area of the burial site and countless burials dating back
2000 years were systematically destroyed (22). Hundreds
of gold objects and dozens of bronze Heger-I drums were
immediately channelled into the illegal antiquities trade.
Although these are looters’ stories, Cambodian students
visited the looters and were able to take pictures from rich
collections which later were sold.

From February to May 2008 and in February/March
2009, the German Archaeological Institute together with
the Memot-Centre Phnom Penh conducted two salvage
excavation campaigns of fifty-two burials lying under the
main road through the village—the last place which was
partly spared from looting. Many of the discovered buri-
als were left intact by the looters in the upper layers, but
below they had been undercut and destroyed by 2.5 m
long lateral tunnels dug about 0.8 m under the surface of
the path. Despite so many partly looted and destroyed in-
humations, more than a half of all excavated burials still
contained gold ornaments forming a unique collection of
about 100 specimens (23). Mostly finger rings or spiral
earrings, although one gold and silver bracelet and an ear-
ring of the Lai Nghi type were found too. Three burials
were equipped with bronze drums or parts of them, in
two other burials a mirror and a bowl, both of bronze,
were used to cover the face in a similar way as the gold
masks in Giong Lon. In Southeast Asia, never before was
such a rich burial site of the Pre-Funan period discovered
(24).'* The pottery of this site is very close to ceramic
shapes of Go O Chua, Giong Ca Vo and Giong Lon. Ac-
cording to the first two radiocarbon dates, most burials in
Prohear should belong to the last two centuries BC.
