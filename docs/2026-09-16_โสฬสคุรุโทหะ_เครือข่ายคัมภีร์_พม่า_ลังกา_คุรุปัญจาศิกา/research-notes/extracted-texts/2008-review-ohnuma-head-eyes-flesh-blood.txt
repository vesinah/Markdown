Head, Eyes, Flesh, and Blood: Giving Away the Body in Indian Buddhist
Literature. By Reiko Ohnuma. Columbia University Press, 2006. 392

In Head, Eyes, Flesh, and Blood, Reiko Ohnuma presents a wide-ranging
exploration of the distinguishing characteristics and central themes of a body
of Buddhist narrative literature, and situates that exploration within broader
conversations in the study of religion about “the gift” and conceptions of the
body. At the same time, however, she also seeks to develop an ambitious argu-
ment, positing “two entire sides of the Buddhist tradition and complexes of
thought and practice that seem to be brought together, over and over again, by
the gift-of-the-body genre” (271): the “ethos of the jātaka” (characterized
by the “perfections” undertaken by the bodhisattva) and the “ethos of the

which Ohnuma analyzes the narratives in question.
Although the precise contours of this argument are not made fully explicit
until the conclusion of the book, Ohnuma initially delineates the “two sides”
in question in the course of proposing that “gift-of-the-body” stories constitute
a genre (Chapter 1). While noting that “genres are always grounded in the
critic’s explanatory purpose, defined by the critic herself in order to achieve
certain ends, and constitutively powerful [sic] of the texts being examined,”
Ohnuma grounds her relatively ahistorical approach in an attempt to imagine
how a “qualified and competent Indian Buddhist reader” might interpret these
stories (33–34). She joins other scholars in positing a basic distinction between
the two broader genres in which “gift-of-the-body” stories are found: jātakas
and avadānas. While these genres overlap in several respects, they can be dis-
tinguished, Ohnuma argues, in terms of ethos. The jātaka genre is defined by
its focus on the radical path of moral perfection undertaken by the bodhisattva,
whereas the avadāna genre typically demonstrates the benefits of devotional
acts undertaken by Buddhists—the salvific efficacy of which is made possible
only through the bodhisattva’s more radical actions. The bodhisattva of the
jātakas, who lives in a world without Buddhism and so must perfect himself
through extreme acts of morality, thereby becomes a Buddha and makes poss-
ible the merit-generating acts of ritual devotion that epitomize the “ethos of
the avadāna.” In this sense, narratives in which the bodhisattva undertakes the
most extreme acts of perfection, giving away parts of his body or even his life,
are “‘super-jātakas’ that occupy the highest rungs in an implicit jātaka-hierar-
chy” (46), providing the ultimate contrast with the devotional ethos of the
avadānas.
Gift-of-the-body narratives are nonetheless characterized precisely by the
dialectic between these “two sides” of the Buddhist tradition and the concomi-
tant dichotomies to which they give rise. Chapter 2 (“Conventions of Plot”)
distinguishes between plotlines in which the recipient of the bodhisattva’s gift
is an ordinary being and those in which the deity Śakra disguises himself in
order to test the bodhisattva’s resolve. Although both varieties of story entail
the bodhisattva giving his body (or a part thereof) to another, Ohnuma argues
that those stories featuring Śakra, which characteristically conclude with the
restoration of the bodhisattva’s body parts, emphasize the rewards of giving,
thus providing an imitable model (however indirectly) for the devotional prac-
tices of ordinary Buddhists. Stories in which the recipient is an ordinary being,
on the other hand, emphasize the extreme “otherness” of the bodhisattva’s
radical commitment to the perfection of giving. Similarly, Ohnuma’s investi-
gation of “Conventions of Rhetoric” in chapter 3 highlights the ethical conflict
in gift-of-the-body narratives between the “universalistic demands of perfect
generosity and the particularistic demands of family, community, and
kingdom” (94), a conflict that Ohnuma ultimately correlates with the dialectic
between the “ethos of the jātaka” and the “ethos of the avadāna” (268).

generate ethically efficacious ambivalence in the reader. Ohnuma discusses two
Chinese stories—ostensibly translations—through which the rhetorical consist-
ency of the genre as a whole is “brought into greater relief” (136). In these
stories, the bodhisattva uncharacteristically goes to great lengths to avoid
giving away his body—a plotline so anomalous that Ohnuma “cannot imagine
an original Indic version of such a tale” and on that basis postulates deliberate
alteration by “a Chinese author” (136–137), whose tolerance for ambivalence
was presumably not as great as that of Indic readers.
Chapters 4 (“Dāna: The Buddhist Discourse on Giving”) and 5 (“A
Flexible Gift”) function as a unit: the first provides a detailed survey of relevant
scholarly literature and proposes two conceptual schema for understanding
Buddhist conceptions of dāna, while the second applies the insights gained
thereby to the gift-of-the-body genre. Here again, Ohnuma invokes the distinc-
tion between the “two sides” of the tradition, arguing that the shifting temporal
perspective of these narratives accounts for the “slippery magic” of the gift:
framing past-life stories within “some ritual or devotional situation occurring
within the surrounding ‘story of the present’” (180) renders “the extreme ideal
of the gift of the body … capable of serving as a model for more ordinary
forms of giving” (182). Ohnuma identifies a similar “slippery magic” at work
in the bodhisattva’s self-aggrandizing claims of selfless generosity in gift-of-
the-body narratives and correlates this paradoxical assertion of self with the
putative rise of “a new conception of the self as an individual and autonomous
agent fully capable of acting on its own behalf” in the “social and cultural
context in which Buddhism arose” (197).
In chapter 6, Ohnuma identifies dialectic between worthlessness and worth
that characterizes discourse on the body in these narratives. This dialectic is
present in the tradition as a whole, Ohnuma suggests: the body itself is con-
ceived of as inherently worthless, but the recognition of its worthlessness para-
doxically makes the body useful for the attainment of “desirable rewards,” thus
transforming the “worthless body into something of incalculable worth”—but
a worth that can only be realized through the acknowledgement of the body’s
essential worthlessness (211). Ohnuma identifies the same emphasis on the
functional worth of the body in the widespread “cliché” in gift-of-the-body
stories of the bodhisattva “extracting the essence from the essenceless body”
through his gift (213). The dialectic between worth and worthlessness is also
embodied in the point of view of different characters: the bodhisattva frequently
speaks of his own body as ordinary and worthless (the “ethos of the jātaka”),
while other characters identify it as an “ideal body” that enables the merit-
making practices characteristic of the “ethos of the avadāna.” The shifting tem-
poral perspective of the narratives also comes into play here; the bodhisattva
usually speaks from his perspective in a “pre-Buddha past,” while the other char-
acters embody “the perspective of the reader in the post-Buddha present,” in
which the attainments of the bodhisattva are already assured (233).
In chapter 7, Ohnuma treats four additional topics relevant to the interpret-
ation of gift-of-the-body narratives: kingship, sacrifice, ritual offering, and death.

Head, Eyes, Flesh, and Blood contains some insightful readings and erudite
syntheses; readers with an interest in “the gift” and discourse about the body,
in particular, will find the book thought-provoking. Unfortunately, it lacks the
kind of cogent, systematic, and fully articulated argument that might make its
concluding thesis compelling. Moreover, the methodological approach intro-
duced in the first chapter is never realized. Paying attention to the “worklike
aspects” of these narratives (34) would entail engaging much more deeply and
consistently with what these narratives might do to a reader in addition to
what they might say. Despite Ohnuma’s assertions regarding the value of these
stories as sites for ethical reflection (138–139), the book deals only occasionally
and rather superficially with how or why the narratives might generate such
reflection. Indeed, awareness of these stories as narratives that shape and are
shaped by their readers falls away to such a degree at times that (for example)
Ohnuma can attribute the bodhisattva’s apparently self-aggrandizing state-
ments to the rise of individualism in ancient India (196)—a highly problematic
argument in any case—without ever imagining the “worklike aspects” of such
statements for a reader. Conversely, when she deals most explicitly with the
imagined reader, that reader seems not to possess either basic knowledge
about the bodhisattva or higher-order interpretive skills: unlike Śakra, a com-
petent Indian Buddhist reader would, for instance, surely know in advance the
answer to the question, “Will the bodhisattva really give the gift?” (133), and
might even wonder about the rhetorical function of asking questions to which
the audience already knows the answer.
Ohnuma is surely right to insist (citing John Strong) on the value of “the
exegetical exploration of a world of meaning,” an exploration that need not be
grounded in a specific historical context (34)—but the grounding that might
be provided instead by imagining an Indian Buddhist reader’s response is
insufficiently developed in this study. As a result, the book is perhaps best
approached as a heuristic tool, the value of which will depend to a great extent
on whether the reader finds its central dialectic useful for understanding not
only gift-of-the-body narratives, but also “the Buddhist tradition” (271).
