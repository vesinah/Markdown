## Content notes (verbatim quotes)

### Three-tier typology (with IDP-linked images)

- Top tier: "the thousands of carefully copied and corrected sūtras which were the products of organized scriptoria working during the Tibetan imperial period. Here variations are at a minimum and have been eradicated as much as possible in the editorial process" (image: IOL Tib J 310.1209).
- Middle tier: "manuscripts that have been copied carefully, but outside of the scriptorium and usually without revisions. Most of these would have been for personal use, and contain texts like short treatises, prayers and rituals. Handwritings are individual and sometimes quite stylish" (image: IOL Tib J 1).
- Bottom tier: "manuscripts that are much more scruffy. The individual letters are poorly formed, as if by children, and the spelling varies quite noticeably more than in most of the Tibetan Dunhuang manuscripts" (image: IOL Tib J 716).

### The teaching-context hypothesis

- "One possibility is that these weren't Tibetans, that they had only just learned Tibetan in order to be able to write and understand Tibetan Buddhist prayers and practices."
- "Another possibility – one that doesn't exclude the first – is that these manuscripts came out of teaching situations... In a manuscript culture, the only textbooks students would own were the ones they wrote themselves."
- Method caveat from the comment thread: van Schaik tried to prove oral origin "to look for 'errors of hearing', i.e. homonyms", but a palaeographer objected that scribes copying from written sources also "memorize short segments... mentally repeating them back to themselves as they write—and this is also a source of homophonic error."

### Related teaching documents mentioned in the post

- IOL Tib J 754: passport with "a series of letters of introduction written in Tibetan" for a Chinese monk pilgrim bound for Nālandā (10th c.).
- Pelliot tibétain 849: journey account of Indian teacher Devaputra; "contains some written notes perhaps taken by a Tibetan student of this Devaputra".
- Pelliot 5386: Khotanese-Sanskrit phrasebook with student-teacher dialogues ("Student: 'I'd like Vajrayāna. Please teach it!'").

### Script development comment (comment thread, July 31, 2008)

- "In the case of Tibetan, I would argue that the 'headed' (dbu can) style began to develop into the 'headless' (dbu med) style once writing with pen and ink became widespread."
- On semi-educated writers: "they will only have the basic letter forms, which in this case are the 'headed' letters, and when they attempt to write them fast, without the cursive forms in their repetoire, the results are quite different from the attractive tachygraphic writing of the better educated scribes." Also: two kinds of scruffy writing must be distinguished — scholars' hasty tachygraphy (good textual witnesses) vs. non-native laboured hands.
- Genre term offered by commenter "Junglo": zin bris = "lecture notes".

### Colophon evidence of layered composition (from Cabezón, quoted in thread)

Colophon of a bKra shis lhun po dharma-college text: "...The scribe was the master of ten treatises... sNying stobs rgya mtsho. Afterwards, the text was completed by the scribe rJe drung Blo bzang dbang rgyal, a Sa skya novice of excellent analytical [abilities], who inserted some scriptures and reasoning in the interstices, and so forth..." — van Schaik counts four human layers: lecturer-author; cleaning-up scribe; a second scribe who added arguments/citations "in the interstices"; and block-carvers.
