## Content notes (verbatim quotes from extracted full text)

### Sam van Schaik's Turfan chapter — script description of named items ("The Uses of Early Tibetan Printing")

- Divination fragments TibHT 31-34: "The writing is in a rough version of the square style, generally seen in documents from, or associated with the Tibetan imperial period." Parallel: "A similar text is found in the Dunhuang scroll IOL Tib J 740, where it is paired with a legal text; Brandon Dotson (2007) has suggested that the divination text... were used to settle legal disputes".
- Mahābāla sūtra pages (TibHT 88, 89; D.757): "The somewhat rounded headed (dbu can) letters with 'blobby' heads are characteristic of a group of 10th century Buddhist manuscripts from Dunhuang (e.g. IOL Tib J 492)."
- 11th-12th c. fragments (TibHT 63, 75-79): "a headed style that appears to be a further evolution of the 10th century Buddhist manuscripts... written making full use of a flat-nibbed pen to create shaded and angular letter forms... that are not found in the Dunhuang manuscripts. They do continue to use old orthography, including the tick on the 'a chung and the subfixed ya on ma."
- dbu med items: "Later styles are also seen in the Buddhist manuscripts written in headless (dbu med) styles. For example, TibHT 36 and 104 (both on tantric empowerment...) are written in a style seen in manuscripts from the 12th and 13th centuries, and have scribal contractions and Arabic numerals, not seen in earlier manuscripts." TibHT 71 shows "the striking long descenders seen in official documents from the period of Mongol rule over Tibet".
- Earliest Tibetan print: prayer printed at Khara Khoto 1153 (Helman-Ważny chapter; preserved in St Petersburg IOM).
- Printed page margins: fragment TibHT 119 "has only the left margin, containing the Tibetan page numbers ka so bzhi" — i.e., margin carried foliation in Tibetan numerals.

### Helmut Eimer's chapter — "Observations Made in the Study of Tibetan Xylographs"

- Sino-Tibetan blockprint layout: "on the right margin a colophon in Chinese provides information about the [fascicle]" — Heissig called these "Pekingese xylographs" (xylographs with Chinese marginal entries); Lithang Kanjur also has Chinese marginals.
- Chinese marginal structure on the 'Phags pa bDe ba can smon lam copies: "above the fascicle number is given, thereunder the short title of the text is written, de yi wen jing, and below follows the number of the folio" — differences here distinguish different block sets.
- Beijing Kanjur revision history: "Further alterations appear in the Tibetan marginals on the recto: One inserted the short title of the Kanjur section in question and replaced the section-wise volume count by a consecutive numbering of all volumes. Tibetan marginals were inserted on the verso of leaves, marks for the recto (gong) and verso ('og) were added in counting the folios."
- User correction practice: an owner "marked the mistake and tried to correct it in the lower margin, but instead of srid the correction reads sir or sid" (Thabs mkhas thugs rjes ma, chos spyod nga/4, fol. 3r).
- Early print layout continuity: "The early prints from Tibet show this layout as well, cf. Diemberger 2012, 23 (specimen of a print from 1407)... the well-known Deb ther sngon po printed in 1481".

### Comparative frame (Indic chapters)

Pāla-script Sanskrit pothīs described with "running marginal titles": "on the left margin of each recto and verso, abbreviated running title (including chapter titles) in Pāla script; on the right margin..., part of the chapter number and foliation"; catalogue entries note "Marginalia: running marginal title (??? ka?), top left margin, verso" and foliation "top left margin (under marginal running title)". One Gilgit/SHT witness has "multilingual marginal titles" (Sanskrit, Chinese, Uighur running titles on the same book).
