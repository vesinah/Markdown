## Substance captured

### Kugyol discovery and the end of Japanese uniqueness claims
- Until the 1970s it was believed that kundoku - reading classical Chinese texts in a vernacular - was a uniquely Japanese invention.
- Analysis of the kugyol written on The Old Translation of Renwangjing (구역인왕경) discovered at Munsusa Temple 文珠寺 in western Korea in 1973 showed vernacular reading of classical Chinese also existed on the peninsula; further documents followed, advancing kunten-shiryo studies and Goryeo-language studies, "chiefly encouraged by the Society for Kugyol Studies led by prof. Emeritus Nam Punghyeon of Dankook University."
- Footnote definition given: "Kugyol is the Korean equivalent to kunten in Japan, a generic term meaning glosses and marks to read classical Chinese."

### Stylus (kakuhitsu/gakpil) discoveries
- 7 July 2000: stylus-glossed texts found by Kobayashi Yoshinori at Sungam Archives of Classical Literature 誠庵古書博物館, Seoul.
- 2002: Kobayashi found an eighth-century stylus impression evidence in 판비량론, a Silla handwritten sutra owned by Otani University, Kyoto.
- 2003: Fujimoto Yukio convened the International Academic Conference on the Adoption of the Chinese Language in Japan and Korea ("Where does the Japanese kanbun kundoku come from?") at Toyama University; 30 Korean researchers plus 54 Japan-based participants.
- 2009-2012: Symposium of Kundoku in Japan and Korea (Reitaku University); yearly joint study of kunten materials of both countries.

### Mutual-influence debate (research question 2 core)
- First achievement of post-2000 joint research: several researchers proposed that the kanbun kundoku reading method devised by the Huayan school of Buddhism in eighth-century Korea may have influenced Nara-period Japanese kanbun kundoku.
- Techniques for reading/writing vernacular with borrowed characters developed on the fifth-century Korean peninsula were likely transmitted to Japan (per studies discussed).

### Comparative extension beyond East Asia (research question 6)
- October 2014 workshop of the Society of Japanese Linguistics reported kundoku in Vietnam alongside Japan, Korea, China, and Europe.
- John Whitman's NINJAL project "A Typological and Historical/Comparative Research on the Languages of the Japanese Archipelago and its Environs" reviewed kundoku globally; NINJAL seminar "Deciphering Classical Literatures in Vernaculars" (31 July-1 Aug 2014) brought together Japanese/Korean kunten researchers and European annotators of Latin glosses (France, UK, Ireland, Switzerland).
- Whitman (2015) compared Japanese and Korean kanbun kundoku with Anglo-Saxon annotations to Latin literature and translation-order numbering, framing all as "vernacular reading of world languages." Latin-specialist colleagues had no native notion "kundoku"; exchange produced its "rediscovery" in Europe.
- King (2006), citing Korean peninsula kundoku, confirmed Pollock's vernacularization claims; Walraven (2004) had objected to Anderson's print-capitalism model.
