## Key extracted content

On what the colloquia are: "these imaginary monologues and conversations (hence, colloquia), rendered in a normally straightforward Greek and Latin and bundled with other bilingual materials (thereby composing the hermeneumata ...), can provide both a pleasant diversion to beginning students of these languages and a wealth of information for the scholar."

On the pseudonym: "(The last element of the title, pseudodositheana, needlessly perpetuates the memory of a scholarly attribution of the texts to the grammarian Dositheus that was definitively discredited by the mid-nineteenth century.)"

Sample schoolbook passage translated in the review: "I awoke from sleep before dawn, got out of bed, sat down, ... and put on my shoes. I asked for water for my face: I wash my hands first, then I washed my face. ... I anointed my head and combed it. ... I left the bedroom with my pedagogue and nurse to say good morning to my father and mother. I greeted and kissed them both and then left the house and go to school." (= Gloss. III 645-646 = ME 2a-4 Dickey)

Previous state of the texts: "Until now, the majority of these texts could be found scattered throughout the third volume of Goetz's Corpus Glossariorum Latinorum of 1892." Dionisotti quoted: "the material [is] in a much more raw and provisional state than we are used to in the classical field" (JRS 72 [1982], 86).

Design evidence for bilingual instruction: "It seems clear that these colloquia were intended from an early stage to provide bilingual instruction, since the compilers avoid constructions in one language that have no obvious counterpart in the other (e.g., the Greek sections avoid the articular infinitive and present participle of 'to be,' while the Latin avoids gerunds and gerundives, or constructions using quin)."

Transmission character: "...since these materials functioned as pragmatic tools rather than as literature, the texts freely underwent changes, additions, and omissions during the course of transmission (the Colloquium Stephani even contains what seems to be a rudimentary 'teacher's guide' at sections 18-19)."

Schoolbook vs phrasebook, with geography: "The 'schoolbook' ... tends to present a coherent narrative of a child's day and ... seems designed to aid children learning both to read their native tongue and to acquire basic familiarity with a second language. The 'phrasebook' ... contains narratives that are frequently interrupted by sets of disconnected phrases and vocabulary lists, and its adult situations—going to the baths, visiting a sick friend—imply that the principal audience consists of adults who are acquiring a second language. Dickey further distinguishes between these two types by demonstrating in convincing detail that the 'schoolbook' probably developed in the West as a resource for learning Greek, while the 'phrasebook' was originally designed to teach Latin (44-48)."

Textual tradition facts: stemma constructed for two of the three traditions in this volume; "the third text, the Colloquium Stephani, survives only in printed editions dating back to 1573". Plates of selected manuscripts at pp. 59-97, 187-194.

Volume apparatus: introduction to all six colloquia (pp. 3-56) surveying ancient second-language teaching; conspectus of surviving Latin-teaching material (grammatical treatises, glossaries, annotated Latin texts); tabular appendix comparing capitular-gloss titles across the six colloquia ("an important element for determining their relationship"); concordance with previous editions. Vol. 2 announced to contain Harleianum, Montepessulanum, Celtis (the last not in Goetz), and papyrus fragments.

Formatting note relevant to layout history: "Press and author are also to be commended for the formatting of the texts of the colloquia to allow easy comparison between the corresponding Greek and Latin portions (another welcome improvement over Goetz)."

## Relevance notes for the book chapter

- Supplies RQ3 substance: definition, six versions, editorial history Goetz 1892 to Dickey 2012.
- The construction-avoidance argument is quotable evidence that the word-lists/colloquies were engineered teaching tools, i.e. a glossary-textbook culture predating medieval glossing.
- The West-schoolbook / East-phrasebook split feeds the book's argument about which direction (Greek for Romans vs Latin for Greeks) generated bilingual pedagogy.
