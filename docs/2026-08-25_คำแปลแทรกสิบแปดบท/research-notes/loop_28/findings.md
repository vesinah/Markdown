## Key findings (with URLs)

1. **Servius: identity, source, and the Danielis problem (RQ1).** The 2019 Casali-Stok synthesis states: Marius or Maurus Servius Honoratus (the fuller name appears in manuscripts only from the ninth century onward) was a grammarian active in Rome late fourth-fifth c.; his celebrated commentary "is generally held to be based on a commentary (now lost) by the earlier fourth-century commentator Aelius Donatus (the teacher of St Jerome)". Some manuscripts contain an enlarged version, the so-called Servius Danielis (DServius/DS), "published in 1600 by Pierre Daniel and thought to be a seventh-to eighth-century expansion made on the basis of material from Donatus' commentary not used by Servius himself." Dedicated literature maps the problem: Steele, Servius and the Scholia of Daniel (https://doi.org/10.31826/9781463222000); Savage on SD manuscripts (https://doi.org/10.2307/310668) and Republican authors in Servius vs SD (https://doi.org/10.2307/310840); Savage, "Was the Commentary on Virgil by Aelius Donatus Extant in the Ninth Century?" (https://doi.org/10.1086/361397); Daintree, "Black Hole or Eminence Grise?" (https://doi.org/10.1017/s0017383500029570). Base text: Thilo-Hagen Teubner scans public-domain at archive.org (https://archive.org/details/serviigrammatici01bons etc.); Harvard Servianorum vols 2-3 (1946) lending-restricted (https://archive.org/details/servianoruminver0002serv).

2. **Other Virgil commentators verified (RQ1).** Tiberius Claudius Donatus has an Oxford Classical Dictionary-research entry "late 4th-early 5th cent. CE?" for his Interpretationes Vergilianae (https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.2287; OUP page blocked, metadata verified), plus a 2021 Hermes study proving Carolingian emendation activity in his text ad Aen. 3.118 (https://doi.org/10.25162/hermes-2021-0023) — direct evidence that his commentary was actively copied and corrected in Carolingian classrooms. Iunius Philargyrius likewise has an OCD-research entry as commentator on Virgil (https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199381135.013.4959). The claim that Aelius Donatus' full commentary survives intact nowhere: (ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน) beyond the secondary framing above.

3. **Horace scholia: two late-antique strata, classroom-born (RQ2).** Amado Roman 2022 (OA, full text harvested): "la primera interpretacion de la que se tiene noticia es la realizada por Porfirion en el siglo III d.C., seguida por la de Pseudo-Acron, obra cuyo nucleo casi con toda seguridad se conformo en el siglo V, aunque fue objeto de diversas alteraciones a lo largo de epoca medieval." Both are tied to the grammaticus school where reading auctores — "sobre todo Virgilio, pero tambien Terencio, Horacio, Persio, Lucano, Estatio o Juvenal" — played the central formative role; the notes focus on lexical, syntactic, etymological questions, treating Horace's verses "como un modelo practico de ensenanza de la latinitas". In Porphyrio the book order is not chronological — Carmina placed first. Four ancient Vitae of Horace circulate: Suetonius', one embedded in Porphyrio, two in Pseudo-Acronian mss (Formenti 2018, OA: https://doi.org/10.7358/erga-2018-002-form). The transmitted Porphyrio is an excerpted text (OUP 2023 chapter, metadata: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198848738.003.0020).

4. **Lucan scholia: two named corpora, one manuscript (RQ2).** Scholarship distinguishes Commenta Bernensia and Adnotationes super Lucanum; Werner's HSCP study is titled precisely "On the History of the Commenta Bernensia and the Adnotationes Super Lucanum" (https://doi.org/10.2307/311330), and a 2022 Hermes note edits "Commenta Bernensia ad Lucan. 8, 824-826" (https://doi.org/10.25162/hermes-2022-0025). The standard edition is Endt, Adnotationes super Lucanum (Teubner 1909, repr. 1969, 445 pp.), subject-classified by archive.org as "Lucan, 39-65. Scholia" (https://archive.org/details/adnotationessupe0000endt, lending-restricted). Esposito surveys "Early and Medieval Scholia and Commentaria on Lucan" in Brill's Companion to Lucan (https://doi.org/10.1163/9789004217096_025). Precise identification of ms Bern Burgerbibliothek 370 as sole witness of the Commenta: (ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน) this session.

5. **Juvenal scholia (RQ2).** The vetera are defined by Wessner's Teubner Scholia in Iuvenalem vetustiora (1931, repr. 1967; https://archive.org/details/scholiainiuvenal0000wess, lending-restricted). An insular (British-Irish) strand inside the ancient Juvenal scholia was argued in CP 1934, "Traces of an Insular Tradition in the Ancient Scholia of Juvenal" (https://doi.org/10.1086/361748). Amado Roman confirms Juvenal belonged to the same grammaticus auctores-cycle as Horace and Virgil (see finding 3). Fuller description of the scholia's lost-commentary origin theory: (ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน) this session.

6. **Hermeneumata Pseudodositheana: structure and status as oldest Western glossary-textbook (RQ3).** Marek 2017 (full OA text): the Hermeneumata are "a collection of bilingual (i.e. Greek-Latin) didactic tools and texts"; most were published by Goetz in CGL III (1892); the attribution to Dositheus Magister (found bundled with his grammar in e.g. MS Sangallensis 902, ascribed 16th-19th c.) "has been reliably disproved". Nine recensions per Dickey: Monacensia, Einsidlensia, Leidensia, Stephani, Montepessulana, Amploniana/Hygini, Bruxellensia, Vaticana, Celtis/Vindobonensia. Typical parts: (1) Greek-Latin glossaries in roughly alphabetical order; (2) glossaries in thematic capitula; (3) the colloquia, scenes of everyday life; (4) bilingual reading texts. Some sections may go back to the 1st c. AD, gathered as teaching packages by 3rd-4th c. Dickey's Cambridge edition: vol. 1 CCTC 49 (2012; https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/9781139096706/type/book), vol. 2 (2015; https://doi.org/10.1017/9781107065390); TLS verdict "surely the definitive edition of these texts for the foreseeable future"; SCS Charles J. Goodwin Award 2016. Her popular spin-off: Learning Latin the Ancient Way (https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9781316145265).

7. **Schoolbook vs phrasebook: two audiences, two directions of teaching (RQ3/RQ5 bridge).** Corbeill's BMCR review of vol. 1: most colloquia divide into a coherent child's-day narrative ("schoolbook") and adult dialogues interrupted by phrase lists ("phrasebook"); Dickey demonstrates "the 'schoolbook' probably developed in the West as a resource for learning Greek, while the 'phrasebook' was originally designed to teach Latin" (pp. 44-48). Compilers avoided constructions without cross-language counterparts (no Greek articular infinitive/be-participle; no Latin gerunds/gerundives/quin) — evidence of deliberate bilingual instructional design. Colloquium Stephani even contains "what seems to be a rudimentary 'teacher's guide'" (S 18-19) and survives only in printed editions dating back to 1573. Before Dickey, the texts lay scattered in Goetz's CGL III (1892), whose state Dionisotti (JRS 72 [1982], 86) called "in a much more raw and provisional state than we are used to in the classical field."

8. **Interlinear/columnar layout inside the Greco-Latin glossary world (RQ3/RQ6).** Marek, citing Dickey 2012:55, notes Dickey "advocates division of the text into columns to create Greek and Latin parallels" while his own edition prints consecutive lines; he adds that although Hamilton popularized interlinear translations of the classics (1769-1831), in the ancient colloquia "interlinear translation ... did not gain acceptance and ... the above-mentioned mirror layout was preferred" — i.e., facing/mirrored columns rather than between-the-lines glossing. Dickey's CQ 2015 abstract documents the parallel phenomenon in literary papyri: "numerous bilingual texts of Virgil and Cicero, with the Latin original and a Greek translation arranged in distinctive narrow columns," evidently "used by Greek-speaking students when they first started to read Latin literature" (https://doi.org/10.1017/S0009838815000087). Also relevant: Foster 2017 on Virgil as language-teaching tool (https://doi.org/10.1017/S0009838817000295); Dickey's legal texts newly extracted from the Hermeneumata (https://doi.org/10.1163/15718190-08212p04).

9. **Festus-Paulus and Nonius: the late-antique lexicographic reservoir (RQ4).** Festus' De verborum significatione abridges Verrius Flaccus; its transmission runs through Paulus Diaconus' epitome — the unitary edition formula "De verborum significatu quae supersunt cum Pauli epitome" (Lindsay Teubner e-reprint: https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783110962253/html; classic Mueller edition 1839 full-view: https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_blixzu5TGAkC). Nonius' De compendiosa doctrina (20 books): Pavlov 2020 (harvested) gives the architecture — etymologico-lexicographic books I, II, IV, V, XII vs grammatical books III, VI-XI; Books II-IV alphabetical, the rest grouped implicitly by quoted authors; Book IV alone occupies nearly one third of the work; a Book I gloss typically = synonym definition, then etymology, then citations. Quotation value: roughly half of ALL surviving Lucilius lines come through Nonius (Cadoni 1987), and per Riposati 95% of Varro's surviving poetic fragments. Editions: Lindsay Teubner 1903 in three parts (DOIs 10.1515/9783110974539, 10.1515/9783110974522, 10.1515/9783110964028); Quicherat 1872 collating five very old codices (full view: https://archive.org/details/noniimarcelliper00noni).

10. **Isidore's Etymologiae as glossary-compilation culture (RQ4).** The complete Lindsay (1911) Latin text is online at LacusCurtius (https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Isidore/home.html), structured exactly like an enlarged glossary-dictionary: Book I opens the liberal arts with De grammatica and includes I.29 De etymologia AND I.30 De glossis — Isidore theorizes glosses inside the encyclopedia itself; Book X is De vocabulis, a free-standing alphabetical glossary of nouns; the first printed edition appeared at Augsburg (Guntherus Zainer, 1472). English translation Barney et al. 2006 (https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511482113). This makes Isidore the hinge where antique gloss collections become the medieval reference genre.

11. **Bridge to Carolingian glossing (RQ5).** Verified anchors for the next loop: O'Sullivan's case study "Problems in Editing Glosses: A Case Study of Carolingian Glosses on Martianus Capella" (Toronto, The Arts of Editing Medieval Greek and Latin, 2016; https://doi.org/10.1515/9781771103770-018); the Brepols volume Teeuwen-O'Sullivan (eds.), Carolingian Scholarship and Martianus Capella: Ninth-Century Commentary Traditions on De nuptiis in Context with Lozovsky's chapter on perceptions of the past (https://doi.org/10.1484/m.celama-eb.4.3007) and the Scholica graecarum glossarum chapter (https://doi.org/10.1484/m.celama-eb.4.3014); plus the 2021 proof of Carolingian emendation inside Tiberius Claudius Donatus (https://doi.org/10.25162/hermes-2021-0023) and Savage 1931 asking whether Donatus' Virgil commentary still existed in the ninth century (https://doi.org/10.1086/361397). Late-antique lore inside medieval glossaries: Lindsay-Thomson, Ancient Lore in Medieval Latin Glossaries (1921, full view: https://archive.org/details/ancientloreinmed00linduoft) and the British Academy Glossaria Latina series incl. the Glossarium Ansileubi (1926; https://archive.org/details/glossaria-latina-i.-glossarium-ansileubi).
