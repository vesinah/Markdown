# Jean Mabillon — De re diplomatica libri VI

## Metadata
- Author: Jean Mabillon (1632-1707), Congregation of Saint-Maur (French Benedictines)
- Work: De re diplomatica libri VI ("Six books on diplomacy", i.e., the critical study of charters/documents)

## Context (secondary)
- Britannica, "Jean Mabillon": De Re Diplomatica (1681; supplement 1704) "established the principles for determining the authenticity and dates of medieval manuscripts"; it "founded the science of diplomatics" and "practically created Latin paleography". It was provoked by the Jesuit Daniel Papebroch's claim that nearly all Merovingian documents were spurious. Mabillon entered Saint-Rémi Abbey, Reims (1653); moved to Saint-Germain-des-Prés (1664), headquarters of the Maurists; co-edited works of St Bernard of Clairvaux (1667) and the Acta Sanctorum Ordinis S. Benedicti (9 vols., 1668-1701). https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jean-Mabillon
- Jeremy Norman, HistoryofInformation.com: "In his book on medieval documents, De re diplomatica libri sex, published in Paris in 1681, Benedictine monk Jean Mabillon founded the formal study of palaeography and diplomatics, laying down the principles for dating scripts and ornament in manuscripts. At this time the term palaeography did not exist." The term was coined later by his pupil Bernard de Montfaucon. Even Papebroch acknowledged the work and conceded that his skepticism about Merovingian charters had been excessive. https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=396
- Method per Britannica summary of scholarship: he examined ink, parchment, handwriting style, and compared one charter with others. https://www.britannica.com/topic/De-Re-Diplomatica
- Continuation: Montfaucon's Palaeographia graeca (1708) — digitized at https://archive.org/details/b30455601 and https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_8cGt5gCRvvIC — first applied "palaeographia" as a term to Greek scripts.

## Primary-text samples (OCR from the 1709 edition; spelling normalized here only in brackets)
Book VI discusses classes of documentary instruments. Sample passage (on chirographa, syngrapha, and indented charters):
> "...chirographum privatam, syngrapha publicam scripturam stricte designant... Et tamen chirographum nonnumquam usurpatur pro syngrapha... Subsequentibus vero saeculis, id est saeculo XII, XIII et XIV, chirographorum paullo alia fuit significatio, nempe ad designandas chartas indentatas, quas alii indentatas vocant."
> "VII. Indentatarum origo nata est ex chartis particulis seu particlis. Particulae sunt chartae a paribus factae, uno tenore scriptae, id est omnino pares, quae diversis partibus dabantur..."
> Example cited: Charlemagne's donation of the Duchy of Spoleto to the Roman church, written by the notary Etherius and placed on the altar of St Peter.
(From De re diplomatica, 1709 ed., OCR; archive.org/details/gri_33125008690477)

The full subtitle of the work announces its program: it explains everything concerning "veterum instrumentorum antiquitatem, materiam, scripturam, & stilum... sigilla, monogrammata, subscriptiones, ac notas chronologicas," i.e., age, material, script and style of old instruments, seals, monograms, subscriptions and chronological signs, with specimens of old scripts in 60 plates and a collection of over 200 monuments.

## Notes for the book chapter
- Institutional setting: royal abbey of Saint-Germain-des-Prés, whose library (Bibliothèque de l'abbaye) and the Maurist congregation supplied the collaborative infrastructure (colleagues aided him, per Britannica).
- Trigger event: Papebroch's Propyleium antiquarium circa quo (1675) in Acta Sanctorum Aprilis II doubted Merovingian charters — the Benedictine-Jesuit controversy that produced both De re diplomatica and, eventually, reconciliation (Papebroch's retraction letter to Mabillon).
- Direct relevance to interlinear gloss/scholia theme: Mabillon's apparatus treats manuscripts as physical, datable artifacts (script, abbreviation, punctuation, ink) rather than as mere text containers; this is the founding gesture of manuscript studies as a discipline.
