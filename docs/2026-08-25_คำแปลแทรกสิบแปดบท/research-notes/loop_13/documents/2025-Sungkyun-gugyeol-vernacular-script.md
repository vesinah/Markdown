## Content notes (verified from fetched excerpt)

- Pre-alphabet Korean vernacular glossing tradition: glosses conveyed through signs (puho kugyol 符號口訣) or characters (muncha kugyol 文字口訣). Sign glosses include stylus depressions (kak'pil 角筆 — same instrument as Japanese kakuhitsu 角筆) or ink marks (muksŏ puho kugyol 墨書符號口訣), positioned around sinographs as morphosyntactic glosses. Character glosses (chat'o 字吐) often use simplified sinographs (yakcha 略字).
- Two interpretative methods regardless of transcription system:
  1. sŏktok kugyol 釋讀口訣 (vernacular reading glossing; also hundok kugyol 訓讀口訣) with word-order inversions;
  2. udok kugyol 音讀口訣 (Sino-Korean reading glossing; also sundok kugyol 順讀口訣, consecutive glossing without inversion).
- Mechanics of sŏktok kugyol: insertion of t'o 吐 reading marks right/left of sentence elements plus inversion glosses (yŏktokchŏm 逆讀點); reading proceeds by taking right-marked elements first, then returning to skipped portions on encountering an inversion mark (following Whitman 2011: 108).
- Dating: earliest extant sŏktok kugyol 10th-13th c.; system complexity and conservative linguistic traits suggest earlier origin; eighth-century Buddhist glossed texts found in Japan suggest Silla influence, dating peninsular use to 7th-8th c. Seol Ch'ong 薛聰 (655-?) credited with reading the Nine Classics "using the local speech".
- Shift: by late 13th c. sŏktok kugyol fades, replaced by udok kugyol maintaining hanmun order with functional morphemes between clauses; possible factors: prestige of cosmopolitan language, rising literacy, formulaic interpretation of Buddhist texts, Yuan-era Chinese influence (Whitman 2011: 99).
- Chosŏn period: vernacular editions of Four Books/Five Classics under T'aejong/Sejong; Buddhist vernacular editions (ŏnhae 諺解) late 15th c. under the Kan'gyŏng togam 刊經都監 (Directorate of Buddhist Publications) with King Sejo's support — glossed hanmun versions followed by vernacular translations, with sinograph pronunciation per the Tongguk chŏngun 東國正韻.
- Cited comparative literature: Whitman 2011 "The Ubiquity of the Gloss", Scripta 3: 95-121; Whitman 2015 "Old Korean", Handbook of Korean Linguistics.
