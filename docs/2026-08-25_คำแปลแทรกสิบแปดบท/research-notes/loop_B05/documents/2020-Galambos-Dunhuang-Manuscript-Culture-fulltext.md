## Key claims relevant to interlinear additions / glosses / scholia project

### Cave history and corpus profile (Introduction)
- Library Cave (Cave 17) was sealed "shortly after 1006"; contents closely tied to Guiyijun history (851-ca. mid-11th c.). Pelliot's theory that the cave was sealed on the eve of the Tangut conquest (closing date 1030) is called "now discredited" (n. 186).
- Corpus is overwhelmingly Buddhist; research historically over-focused on non-Buddhist texts despite this.
- Multilingual scale claim: over thirty languages/scripts attested around Turfan, approximately twenty at Dunhuang, at least a dozen at Khara-Khoto; multilingualism "an everyday reality" on the societal level.
- Guiyijun-period manuscript culture shows heavy Central Asian influence: appearance of pothi, codex, and concertina forms; conversion of scroll sheets into codices; concertina dimensions later match Tangut Khara-Khoto concertinas (n. 150).
- Writing-tool shift: Fujieda Akira first drew attention to the switch from Chinese brush to Tibetan-style (hard-point/reed) pen after the Tibetan occupation; per n. 34 (citing Fujieda 1961, 206; 1968; 1969, 19-22; Li Zhengyu 2005), "Fujieda estimates that more than 60% of all Dunhuang manuscripts were written by pen." Palaeographic signal usable for distinguishing annotation layers.
- Paper-quality decline during Tibetan period challenged as an assumption; scientific paper analysis cited (van Schaik, Helman-Wazny and Noller 2015).

### Chapter 1: Multiple-text Manuscripts
- Multiple-text booklets (codices/concertinas bundling short texts: Fomu jing, Baixing zhang-type primers, prayers etc.) assembled from reused scroll sheets; several different hands within one booklet detectable by character-form comparison and character size (8-9 vs more characters per line).
- Direct relevance to annotation culture: manuscripts are palimpsest-like social objects accumulating texts and hands over time; single-sheet-to-codex conversion implies active re-use of writing surfaces (verso reuse economy).

### Chapter 2: Manuscripts Written by Students (core evidence for didactic annotation practice)
- Corpus definition: colophons self-identifying as xuelang/xueshilang/xuesheng(liang); Mair 1981 catalogued nearly 600 student manuscripts; Li Zhengyu 1987 inventoried 144 student colophons (72 firmly dated).
- Production scenario evidence of oral-dictation teaching: P.2825 colophon (Taigong jiajiao, Dazhong 4/gengwu 850): read aloud by student Song Wenxian, copied by An Wende; second part in larger characters possibly by different hand and a possible later addition clarifying division of labour - i.e., colophon itself shows layered accretion. Same pair produced Kaimeng yaoxun copy S.705 in 851.
- Practice-workload quantification: S.2703 contains rows of identical characters from the Qianziwen (Thousand Character Classic) with day counts; student copied 12-13 lines/day, 12-18 characters/line = ca. 150-230 characters per day; lighter than Xiaojing segments in S.728/P.3698, implying younger pupil.
- Zuercher's (1989) model cited: master wrote e.g. Qianziwen horizontally across the top of the sheet, pupils copied each character down vertical columns - physical layout signature of early-stage handwriting drills.
- Text-length logic: primers chosen by length and character difficulty (Kongzi Xiang Tuo xiangwen shu ca. 1500 chars; Qin fu yin 1666; Xiaojing ca. 1800; Taigong jiajiao 2200).
- Verso scribble culture: versos of student scrolls carry disconnected jottings - primer titles, quotes (Xiaojing, Baixing zhang chapters 18/19 on P.3698 verso as study references), fragments of association circulars, names/dates echoing recto colophons; unskilled hands with errors; too short to be exercises themselves, plausibly reminders of assigned work. Conclusion: "scrolls or booklets we see today are the product of years of collaborative engagement" - multi-user, multi-session annotation accumulation.
- Copyist-humour poem about deliberately leaving mistakes "to my successors" survives in four manuscripts (P.2621, BD08668, P.3305, P.2937 dated 884), with phonetic/graphic substitution variants tabulated - scribal error culture itself transmitted between copies.
- Red punctuation marks segment clauses in first 17 lines of the Xiaojing on one scroll's recto, disappearing thereafter - pedagogical segmentation applied only to studied portion.
- Monastic schools functioned as private schools for the local elite; student colophons attach almost exclusively to secular texts (per Zuercher).

### Chapter 3: Writing from Left to Right
- Some Guiyijun circulars and student-related texts write Chinese characters in left-to-right order, opposite normal right-to-left columns - interpreted against Central Asian (multi-script) environment influence; direction anomalies are datable cultural signals.

### Chapter 4: Circulars and Names
- Association (sheyi) circulars on manuscript versos give first-hand data on daily life; fragments recur with predictable consistency across student manuscripts, again evidencing systematic rather than random annotation.

## Tangut question (Gap 3 closure) - footnote 42 (Introduction)
Verbatim core (short quote): "the so-called Northern Zone 北區 at the Mogao Caves yielded handwritten and printed fragments with additional languages not represented in the library cave (e.g. Tangut, Mongolian, Syriac)".
- Supporting citations given there: Peng 2001 (Northern Zone survey); Matsui 2012 (Uighur scribble on a Tangut Buddhist fragment from Dunhuang, in Popova ed., Festschrift Kychanov, 238-243); Rybatzki 2009 (Mongolian); Duan 2001 (Syriac); Yakup 2006 (Old Uighur).
- Combined with closure date shortly after 1006 (before Tangut script creation 1036 and before Xixia annexation of Hexi), this supports: Tangut-language material is NOT part of the Cave 17 cache; it derives from Mogao Northern Zone caves (esp. 464/465 area) and Khara-Khoto. Galambos 2015c (Translating Chinese Tradition and Teaching Tangut Culture, SMC 6, De Gruyter) treats Khara-Khoto Tangut books; n. 126 notes a Dunhuang text surviving in Tangut translation FROM Khara-Khoto.
