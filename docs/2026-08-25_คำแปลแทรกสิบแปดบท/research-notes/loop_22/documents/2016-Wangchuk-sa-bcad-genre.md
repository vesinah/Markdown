## Content notes (from the fetched full text)

Definition: sa bcad is often translated "outline" and is the name of a Tibetan literary genre. The Tshig mdzod chen mo (s.v.) gives two meanings: (a) "compendium of text of a treatise" (gzhung tshig gi sdom) and (b) a chronological list/order of tasks (las don gyi go rim), the latter recent and secondary. Etymological reading: sa ("ground") + bcad ("cut/marked") — like a milestone or signpost marking crucial points on the path one traverses, "cut by leaps" like a lioness or tigress rather than crawling like a snail. Tantric exegesis knows the "five ligatures" (chings lnga), among them sa bcad rnam 'byed kyi chings / sa gcod tshom gyi chings — an aid for obtaining an overview of the entire content and structure of a work.

Origin debates:
- Ernst Steinkellner suggested the origin of sa bcad could have been China and not India (Steinkellner, "Who is Byaṅ chub rdzu 'phrul?", Berliner Indologische Studien 4/5, 1989, pp. 229-251). If so, the commentary on Bodhicittabhāvanā ascribed to Mañjuśrīmitra, which seems to contain what may be called a sa bcad, could not be Mañjuśrīmitra's; conversely if that commentary is Indian, Steinkellner's suggestion may be questioned.
- sa bcad occasionally seems to render Sanskrit ṭipppaṇī, though this Sanskrit-Tibetan correspondence remains doubtful.
- Wangchuk proposes exploring the Tantric exegetical concept of chings lnga as one venue for the genre's origin; cites Mi-pham's bKa' brgyad rnam bshad (pp. 17-18) and sMin-gling lo-chen's gSang bdag zhal lung on the tigress's leap (stag mchongs) as metaphor for dividing a tantra's words and meanings into segments, and the lion's leap (seng ge mchongs) for gathering many divisions into a single synopsis.
- Cites Ri chos sa bcad (p. 443): sa bcad gathers the meaning of a treatise into parts like a great tree with evenly measured branches.

Features of the genre listed in the post:
(a) a good sa bcad can be equivalent to or better than some commentaries;
(b) two authors may write completely different sa bcad for the same text;
(c) a commentator may adopt an existing sa bcad;
(d) a sa bcad may be embedded in the main commentary or exist independently;
(e) not all commentarial forms contain one — mchan 'grel would not have a sa bcad embedded;
(f) types vary in detail and scope;
(g) primary and tertiary parts; the rtsa ba'i sa bcad (root outline) cannot normally be dispensed with;
(h) can be of Indian or Tibetan treatises, of others' or one's own works;
(i) physically may occur in the form of a mchan (marginal note); possibly sa bcad originated as a kind of mchan integrated into the main text, conceivably developed from teachers' lecture notes used in oral exposition;
(j) the sa bcad of a commentary may be written by someone other than the commentary's author (some sa bcads in Mi-pham's commentaries were inserted by compilers);
(k) a sa bcad can be written in verse (Mi-pham's sa bcad to the Pramāṇavārttika).
Finally: following the sa bcad is among the first things a monk learns in a bshad grwa; students asked to explain a text must recite the preceding outlines; ability to follow the sa bcad equals ability to follow the text.
