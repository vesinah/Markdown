## Content notes (from the fetched full text)

(i) The double tsheg: In standard orthography from the 10th century onward each syllable is separated by a single dot (tsheg) level with the tops of the main letters. In early inscriptions a double dot (colon-like) is often used instead; it gradually fell from use in the early 9th century. It appears consistently in the two major 8th-century inscriptions (Zhol and Samye pillars); on early 9th-century pillars reduced to marking line ends and as decoration between two shad. Appears in Dunhuang manuscripts including the Old Tibetan Annals and legal/divination texts. Brandon Dotson's study of IOL Tib J 740 tabulated occurrence after final syllables: single tsheg used about 75% of the time after final nga, na, da, ra but only ~5% after other endings (double tsheg preferred elsewhere) — probably because lower parts of those letters obstruct the lower dot.
Dating: double tsheg appears sporadically at line ends in the 805/6 Temple of the Hat inscription but is absent in the 812 inscription there, suggesting its use was discouraged in Senaleg's (Sad na legs) 812 language reforms; not found regularly in later inscriptions nor in post-imperial Dunhuang manuscripts — potentially useful for dating Dunhuang manuscripts, though copyists can reproduce obsolete forms.

(ii) The mid-line tsheg: a tsheg sitting mid-height rather than at top occurs in pillar inscriptions and Dunhuang manuscripts; dominant form in some (e.g., a Pratimoksa-sutra fragment, IOL Tib J 13). Advantage: allows tighter spacing, fitting into open right side of nga, ta, da. Author's impression that it does not occur in 10th-century Dunhuang manuscripts — hypothesis unproven.

(iii) The long tsheg: lengthened into a short stroke between tsheg and shad; irregular, likely a scribal quirk, possibly from a double tsheg written without pen lift. Not useful for dating.

Sources cited on page: Dotson 2007 ("Divination and Law in the Tibetan Empire", Contributions to the Cultural History of Early Tibet, Brill, 3-77); de la Vallée Poussin 1962 catalogue of IOL Tibetan manuscripts.

Supplementary conventions for blockprint/modern formatting (separate source, Digital Tibetan formatting rules, https://digitaltibetan.github.io/DigitalTibetan/docs/tibetan_formatting.html):
- shad ། never starts a line; verse/paragraph endings use double shad; when a syllable breaks across lines its closing shad becomes rin chen spungs shad ༑.
- yig chung: commentaries and annotations printed at roughly 25-30% smaller font size; headlines in pechas also set small.
- sbrul shad ༈ marks start of new texts/chapter boundaries and marks insertions in pechas.
- ScriptSource (https://scriptsource.org/scr/Tibt): dbu-can "with head" is the less cursive book script; dbu-med "headless" includes careful dpe-yig and rapid 'khyug-yig; spaces are not used between words so the tsheg functions as word separator; shad divides sections; hooked shad marks grammatical break with continuous recitation.
