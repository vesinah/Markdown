## Content notes (from retrieved excerpt)

Core thesis as stated verbatim in the accessible excerpt: "dbu med was originally a cursive script that developed out of dbu can. We can show that this early dbu med was in use throughout the Tibetan empire, and that it was mainly taught to official scribes. We will also show how a variety of calligraphic dbu med styles developed..." [excerpt truncates here].

Implications relevant to annotation traditions:
- The cursive/headless script was the working hand of trained official scribes across the empire — hence expected in administrative documents, notes and informal writing, while dbu can served formal/book contexts.
- Related palaeographical framework: van Schaik 2013 "Dating early Tibetan manuscripts: a paleographical method" (in Iwao, Takeuchi, Dotson eds., Scribes, Texts, and Rituals in Early Tibet and Dunhuang, Reichert, 119-135) and van Schaik 2014 "Towards a Tibetan palaeography: developing a typology of writing styles in early Tibet" (Manuscript Cultures: Mapping the Field, De Gruyter, 299-338) — the latter is the explicit "typology" reference for early Tibetan writing styles.
- Channa Li has applied typological palaeography to a single Dunhuang scribal hand: BuddhistRoad Paper 1.2, "Toward A Typology of Chodrup's (Tib. Chos grub, Chin. Facheng) Cursive Handwriting: A Palaeographical Perspective" (2021), https://omp.ub.rub.de/index.php/BuddhistRoad/catalog/book/191
- Orthographic/script observations from a Dunhuang manuscript study (Zorin 2018, Written Monuments of the Orient, https://journals.eco-vector.com/2410-0145/article/view/35146): Dunhuang ms Дх. Тиб. 217 shows my- pattern for mi-/me- words, inverted gi gu, tsheg before 'i/'o particles, an sp- ligature similar to dbu med forms (shared with later Ablaikit Kangyur), peculiar spellings (byang cub, rin cen, las stsogs), syllables split across line ends around folio holes, and no tsheg before shad even after final ng.
