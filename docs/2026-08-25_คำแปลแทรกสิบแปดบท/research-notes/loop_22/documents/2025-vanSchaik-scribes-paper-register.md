## Content notes (from retrieved excerpts)

- Among the Tibetan manuscripts from Cave 17, several thousands were copied during the early ninth century on order of the Tibetan emperor. Two scriptural texts — the Perfection of Wisdom Sutra and the Limitless Life Sutra (Aparimitayur-nama) — were copied repeatedly in a huge operation exploiting Dunhuang's role as a papermaking centre, employing hundreds of local people as scribes. Perfection of Wisdom copies are on large loose-leaf pothi pages (e.g., IOL Tib J 105); some were sent elsewhere, even to Central Tibet.
- Paper audit system: scroll IOL Tib J 1359 is a register of the paper owed by scribes. Each scribe received packages of blank paper which they returned filled with the copied text; pages were carefully counted; missing pages had to be made up or punishment followed: "The scribes must pay off their debts in person. Until they have paid off the debt, up to twice its value should be taken from their possessions, livestock and the like as a security and deposited with the clerk."
- Punishment: a scribe who could not pay was taken to the scribal office and whipped, ten lashes for every ten sheets lost. The register uses the obscure term rgyab chad ("strike on the back"), guessed to mean whipping; confirmed by legal document Pelliot tibetain 1078 concerning a local papermaker who borrowed paper without repaying, threatening "punishment by hitting with a whip".
- Distributed scriptorium model: the official title li ceng in the register is likely a Chinese loanword (lizheng, village head). Since many scribes were not monastics, work likely happened in their own homes: a central office gave out blank paper, received copied manuscripts, audited lost paper, and hosted public punishments — "a kind of distributed scriptorium at Dunhuang".
- Scribes were multilingual/multicultural: Chinese and Tibetan names plus other origins; several had Tibetan personal names with Chinese surnames.
- References cited on the page include: Dotson, Iwao, Takeuchi (eds.), Scribes, Texts, and Rituals in Early Tibet and Dunhuang (Reichert, 2013), incl. "Glegs tshas: Writing Boards of Chinese Scribes in Tibetan-ruled Dunhuang" pp. 101-110; Thomas 1951, Tibetan Literary Texts and Documents Part II.

Corroborating material fetched from earlytibet.com ("The Chinese under Tibetan rule", https://earlytibet.com/2011/05/10/tibetan-history-i/):
- After Dunhuang fell in 786, Chinese inhabitants wore Tibetan dress; bilingual generation produced translator Go Chodrup (Chos grub / Facheng) whose translations later entered the canon.
- Early ninth century: thousands of sutras written under Tibetan oversight; discipline tough — wasted paper punished by flogging; failure to deliver could mean property impounded or family held hostage.
- Perfection of Wisdom manuscripts recently found in Central Tibetan monastic libraries identified as Dunhuang products because signed by the same Chinese scribes seen in Dunhuang colophons.
- Scrap paper called legtse (wrapping paper for bundles of blank pages delivered to scribes) bears ownership threats and rebellious verse by scribe Lenho Wenman ("We are the subjects of Tibet...").
