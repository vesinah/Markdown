## Section 2.1 - key content (verified from fetched PDF)

Thesis of the section: the earliest witnesses of Coptic biblical translations are Egyptian glosses/"annotations" in Greek biblical manuscripts; the glossing custom may continue a longstanding pharaonic tradition still active in the 2nd century CE (citing Feder 2020c: 644-646).

Three concrete manuscript witnesses documented:

1. Chester Beatty Papyri codex VII, Greek Isaiah (TM 61951; LXX MS 965), dated mid-3rd to early 4th century.
   - ca. 30 Coptic annotations written in Greek letters only (no Demotic-derived signs), irregularly spaced.
   - Dialect: barely standardized Fayyumic with Mesokemic influence ("typical for the region between the Fayyum Oasis and the nearby Nile Valley").
   - Glosses possibly added in the 3rd or early 4th century.
   - Reference given: Wagner 2021: 293-295, 406-407.

2. Freer Collection codex of the Minor Prophets, Washington (TM 61966; LXX MS W), dated 3rd-4th century.
   - ca. 20 glosses showing well-standardized Sahidic close to 4th-century Sahidic biblical manuscripts.
   - Wagner's suggestion (quoted): "W's annotators copied them ... perhaps all, of their Sahidic notes from one or more Sahidic biblical manuscripts" (Wagner 2021: 274-275).
   - Two chronological options discussed: date W to the 4th century, or assume the Sahidic glosses were added considerably later than the 3rd-century Greek text.

3. British Museum papyrus leaf EA 10825: Greek-Coptic glossary to Hosea and Amos (TM 61982).
   - Recto: Greek land register dated ca. 200 CE; verso glossary dated 3rd-4th century; presumably from the Oxyrhynchite region.
   - Format: columns each containing Greek words/phrases (often abbreviated) from Hosea and Amos, each followed by a Coptic translation - i.e., a bilingual glossary format.
   - Dialect: clearly Mesokemic; editors Bell & Thompson 1925.
