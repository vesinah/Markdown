## Content (full-text notes preserving substance)

Framing: current work on "marginal scholarship" is characterized by concentrated focus on practices of reading and writing plus an associated nomenclature/taxonomy for annotation practices. Credit goes to the Department for the History of Knowledge, Huygens Institute, which housed the NWO-funded project "Marginal Scholarship: The Practice of Learning in the Early Middle Ages (c. 800 - c. 1000)" directed by Mariken Teeuwen 2011-2016. Volume = 26 papers from a three-day conference at The Hague, June 2015.

Section I "Scholars and Their Books":
- Teeuwen, "Voices from the Edge: Annotating Books in the Carolingian Period" (13-36): genesis of project; impact of digitized manuscripts; conceptual tools to use annotations as sources for medieval intellectual life; principal findings of the project database of 353 manuscripts (https://www.marginalscholarship.nl).
- Evina Steinová, "Technical Signs in Early Medieval Manuscripts Copied in Irish Minuscule" (37-85): 13 Irish-minuscule manuscripts surveyed; crosses, lege signs, quotation signs, chrismons, m- and T-shaped signs; "early medieval Irish readers were vigorous users of technical signs"; community-specific signs alongside Carolingian ones; Irish signs copied into non-Irish Carolingian manuscripts can point to Irish prototypes. Steinová PhD: Notam superponere studui: The Use of Technical Signs in the Early Middle Ages (Utrecht 2016).
- Giorgia Vocino, "A Peregrinus's Vade Mecum: MS Bern 363 and the 'Circle of Sedulius Scottus'" (87-123): the Bern Horace; layout and note layers suggest derivation from an earlier book connected to Sedulius Scottus; owner-scholar added own observations incl. references to Lorsch and Liege; book kept evolving under its owner who consulted, designed, and copied it.
- Warren Peze, "The Making of the De praedestinatione of Ratramnus of Corbie (Including the Identification of a New Personal Manuscript)" (125-155): Ratramnus had a team of assistants follow his marginal instructions when copying excerpts; he marked usque / usque hic / Nota while reading; two signaling systems reflect different compositional goals. Evidence of authorial work-practice ("dictata"-style composition).
- Giacomo Vignodelli, glosses to Atto of Vercelli's Perpendiculum (157-196): nearly 2900 self-glosses vindicated as the bishop's own.

Section II "Textual Scholarship by Means of Annotation":
- Franck Cinato, "The Earliest Anonymous Exposition of Priscian: Two Manuscripts and Their Glosses" (199-236): dates/traces origins of the oldest Priscian gloss set; layers going back possibly to Priscian himself, to Cassiodorus's Vivarium, or to Theodore and Hadrian's Canterbury school; someone who knew Homer added Greek material to the growing tradition.
- Markus Schiegg, "Source Marks in Scholia" (237-261): source marks (AM, AVG, HI...) popularized by Bede, used idiosyncratically.
- Martin Hellmann, "Tironische Tituli" (263-283): Tironian shorthand used for marginal tituli gauging reader interaction; sometimes mixed with Caroline minuscule; sometimes entire comments/summaries purely in Tironian notes; idiosyncrasies make them valuable indicators of location and time.
- Andreas Nievergelt, "Glossen aus einem einzigen Buchstaben" (285-304): single-letter glosses; example: dry-point a over pe of tempestas plausibly pointing to an OHG equivalent; single letters also contract phrases or serve mnemonic functions.
- Justin Stover, blank spaces as paratext in the two ninth-century Ammianus manuscripts (305-321).
- Erik Kwakkel, "The Margin as Editorial Space" (326-341): gleaning from Marginal Scholarship database: the 353 manuscripts average marginal space of 47% to 50% of full page size (excluding intercolumnar space) - i.e., almost half the page was available for annotations. Monte Cassino Dioscorides alphabeticus: require-sign corrections in 192 places, 8 variant notices, 30 lacunae; likely supervised by Constantine the African's team.
- Alberto Cevolini, Robert Grosseteste's topical table of signs (~440 topics); medieval knowledge seen as closed universe of subject matters.

Section III "Private Study and Classroom Reading":
- Sinead O'Sullivan, "Reading and the Lemma in Early Medieval Textual Culture" (371-396): reading glossed manuscripts was "slow, complex, open-ended, non-linear, fragmentary, multivalent, and requiring effort"; glosses ambiguously linked to lemmata, running into each other, stacked on top of each other; this satisfied an interior reading valuing contemplation over speed.
- Silvia Ottaviano, "Reading between the Lines of Virgil's Early Medieval Manuscripts" (397-426): two layout formats: (a) poet's text in one column with running comments alongside in flanking column(s); (b) marginal glosses keyed to specific passages; some manuscripts combine both. Comparing similar glosses shows how glosses travelled, were reworked, or had materials added.
- Anna Grotans, Notker Labeo's translation/commentaries (c. 950-1022): Latin text + OHG translations + commentary merged in continuous running format instead of interlinear/marginal glosses; surviving manuscripts nearly pristine; possibly memorial volumes made by Ekkehart IV after Notker's death.
- Ad van Els, MS Leiden VLO 15 of Ademar of Chabannes: teacher's composite teaching manuscript, continually collected and adapted.
- Micol Long, monastic shared reading as cooperative learning; Paulina Taraskin on BL Harley 2724 Horace scholia.

Section IV "Annotating Orthodox and Heterodox Knowledge":
- Irene van Renswoude, obelus as censor's rod (Origen's editorial mark transformed into sign of judgment during Jerome-translation controversies).
- Janneke Raaijmakers, Studying Jerome in a Carolingian Monastery (early ninth-century Lorsch codex): monks' marks show interest in Jerome's rhetoric, proverbs, classical learning, textual criticism guidelines rather than the relic question.
- Cinzia Grifoni, "Reading the Catholic Epistles: Glossing Practices in Early Medieval Wissembourg" (705-742): Otfrid of Wissembourg's personally annotated biblical manuscripts usually arranged in three columns with the biblical text in the middle column, leaving extensive space for annotations in the other two columns; notes pulled from exegetes incl. Josephus Scottus and Hrabanus Maurus; meant primarily for private reflection and instruction.
- Patrizia Carmassi: paratext guiding reading; Fischbeck Jerome manuscript (female community) - volume's only explicit female reader/writer evidence.

Epilogue: David Ganz, "The Search for Glossed Clauses: An Autobiographical Account of a Corbie Study" (767-771); his Corbie in the Carolingian Renaissance (Sigmaringen: Thorbecke, 1990) jumpstarted systematic attention to marginal scholarship.
