## Key findings (with URLs)

1. Editorship verified — "Glossae Aevi Carolinii" was NOT edited by Louis Holtz or "Siney": the volume Glossae aeui Carolini in libros I-II Martiani Capellae De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii is Corpus Christianorum Continuatio Medievalis vol. 237, ed. Sinead O'Sullivan (Brepols, Turnhout 2010, 472 pp., ISBN 978-2-503-53453-4). It collates fourteen glossed manuscripts, organizes glosses by lemma and content category, notes second/third-hand additions, and documents a 'core' corpus plus accretions across the tradition. URL: https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503534534-1 ; review by Hicks JML 22 (2012): https://doi.org/10.1484/J.JML.5.100870

2. Dunchad/Lutz tradition clarified — Cora E. Lutz's Dunchad Glossae in Martianum (American Philological Association, 1944) is a partial edition based on Paris BnF lat. 12960. Attribution to Dunchad (Irish master at Saint-Remi, Reims) and then to Martin of Laon were both rejected; the tradition is now considered anonymous, produced by a group of collaborating scholars c. 820-840. The digital continuation (martianus.huygens.knaw.nl, ed. Teeuwen et al., online Nov 2008 on Huygens' eLaborate platform) presents a semi-diplomatic edition of Leiden Vossianus Lat. Fol. 48 — one of the four key witnesses; the commentary survives in 16 mss (12 from the 9th c.); VLF 48 also carries annotations by Bishop Ratherius of Verona (10th c.). URLs: https://martianus.huygens.knaw.nl/path ; https://ride.i-d-e.de/issues/issue-1/carolingian-scholarship/ ; https://www.e-laborate.nl/en/carolingian_scholarship/the_manuscripts/

3. Three major Carolingian commentary traditions on De nuptiis are identified: the oldest gloss tradition, John Scottus Eriugena's Annotationes (Lutz ed. 1939, from late-9th-c. Corbie ms Paris lat. 12960), and Remigius of Auxerre's commentary (Lutz ed., based on 10th-15th c. mss). Eriugena and Remigius both rest on the oldest tradition. Source: BMCR review of Ramelli, Tutti i commenti a Marziano Capella: https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2007/2007.09.39 ; JSTOR "Greek and the Carolingian Reception of Martianus Capella": https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/45019702.pdf

4. Priscian glossing at St Gall — Cod. Sang. 904 contains Priscian's Institutiones grammaticae written in Insular script, dated 851 (written AD 850-1, probably in Ireland; e-codices suggests origin Bangor? or Nendrum?), carrying over 9,400 interlinear and marginal glosses (e-codices counts >9000, incl. 3478 Old Irish) plus c. 4,000 construe marks. First complete digital transcription v2.1 by Hofman/Moran/Bauer (2023); earlier partial print publication Hofman, The Sankt Gall Priscian Commentary Part 1 (Munster 1996). Note: this codex is an IRISH (Hiberno-Latin) witness rather than a Frankish palace-school product, but it circulated within the Carolingian world (it reached Reichenau/St Gall circles) and is central to Carolingian-era grammar glossing culture. URLs: https://stgallpriscian.ie/glosses ; https://www.e-codices.unifr.ch/en/list/one/csg/0904 ; Vienna companion database covering also Karlsruhe Aug. CXXXII, Paris lat. 10290, Milan Ambr. A 138 sup., Leiden BPL 67: https://indogermanistik.univie.ac.at/priscian

5. Layout practices quantified — In Teeuwen's Marginal Scholarship database of 353 manuscripts (c. 800-1000), marginal space averages 47-50% of the full page size (excluding intercolumnar space): nearly half the page was reserved for annotation. Observed formats include: text column with flanking comment columns vs. passage-keyed marginal glosses (Virgil mss, Ottaviano); three-column biblical layouts with text in the middle column and extensive gloss space either side (Otfrid of Wissembourg's personal copies, Grifoni); interlinear glosses between the lines (St Gall Priscian; O'Sullivan CCCM 237 "from between the lines and in the margins"); stacked and ambiguously anchored glosses producing slow, non-linear reading (O'Sullivan). URLs: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/26205 ; https://www.brepolsonline.net/doi/pdf/10.1484/M.USML-EB.5.115016?download=true

6. Non-alphabetic glossing devices — Tironian shorthand served as a gloss medium: Martin Hellmann ("Tironische Tituli", in The Annotated Book, pp. 263-283) shows marginal tituli sometimes mixed with Caroline minuscule memorializing reader reactions, sometimes entire summaries purely in notae; their idiosyncrasies date and locate hands. Technical signs (lege signs, crosses, chrismons, require-signs, obelus) form a parallel apparatus studied by Steinová (Notam superponere studui, Utrecht PhD 2016) and van Renswoude (obelus as censor's rod). Single-letter glosses (Nievergelt) can point to vernacular equivalents. Display-script hierarchy (Square Capitals > Uncial > Rustic Capitals > Caroline minuscule) structured page navigation. URLs: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/26205 ; https://hmmlschool.org/latin-caroline/

7. Teeuwen's "Marginal Scholarship" argument (RQ2/RQ5) — The NWO Vidi project "Marginal Scholarship: The Practice of Learning in the Early Middle Ages (c. 800 - c. 1000)" (Teeuwen dir., Huygens, 2011-2016) reframed glosses from clutter to first-order evidence of intellectual life: annotations serve education but also textual criticism, mnemonic aids, discussion, and critique; the 2015 Hague conference yielded The Annotated Book in the Early Middle Ages (Teeuwen & van Renswoude eds., USML, Brepols 2017). Teeuwen's Veni project preceded it ("Carolingian Scholarship and Martianus Capella"), and her monograph Harmony and the Music of the Spheres (Brill 2002) treats the ars musica in ninth-century Martianus commentaries. URLs: https://www.marginalscholarship.nl ; https://www.universiteitleiden.nl/en/staffmembers/mariken-teeuwen ; https://knaw.academia.edu/MarikenTeeuwen

8. Classroom/workshop context (RQ3) — Evidence that glosses record teaching activity: (a) the oldest Martianus commentary arose 820-840 through exchange among scholars at monasteries and courts (palace-school/court milieu included; specific palace-school attribution of particular gloss layers remains debated); (b) Warren Peze shows Ratramnus of Corbie dictating excerpt instructions to assistants via marginal Nota/usque systems — authorial work-practice visible in margins; (c) Hofman 1988 attributes a 9th-c. Priscian gloss dossier probably to Heiric of Auxerre (d. c. 876), a named master's classroom material; (d) Contreni's Laon oeuvre (e.g., Codex Laudunensis 468: A Ninth-Century Guide to Virgil, Sedulius, and the Liberal Arts, Brepols 1984; "The Carolingian School: Letters from the Classroom") reconstructs schoolroom book use; (e) trajectory note: Anselm of Laon (d. 1117): the Glossa ordinaria grew from his cathedral-school teaching, assembled after his death by students such as Gilbert de la Poree — i.e., students copying masters' dictata into systematic gloss collections culminates in the twelfth-century Glossa ordinaria (see also Andreé, "Laon Revisited", JML 22 (2012)). URLs: https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/26205 ; https://www.cla.purdue.edu/academic/history/documents/directory-documents/contreni-publications-2023.pdf ; https://medieval.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/catalog/person_77109407

9. Latin-vernacular bridge (RQ4) — Reichenau and St Gall produced numerous OHG interlinear versions rendering Latin word-for-word between the lines (BLB Karlsruhe context: Mayer, Die Reichenauer Handschriften); Reginbert of Reichenau's library catalogue even lists carmina diversa ad docendum Theodiscam linguam. A Reichenau psalter of c. 825 survives as Zurich, Zentralbibliothek Rh. 34 (per BLB Reichenau Malschule listing). Notker Labeo's St Gall translations (c. 950-1022) moved beyond interlinear glossing to continuous Latin+OHG+commentary running format (Grotans). However, the specific shelfmark and description of the classic "Psalterium Reinense" OHG psalter could not be pinned down this loop — see gaps. URLs: https://www.blb-karlsruhe.de/files/user_upload/PDF/4_Sammlungen/Dorothea%20Mayer_Die_Reichenauer_Handschriften.pdf ; https://www.blb-karlsruhe.de/sammlungen/handschriften/bestand/reichenau ; https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/26205

10. Digital infrastructure (RQ6) — e-codices provides complete St Gall digitizations with scholarly descriptions, DOIs and IIIF manifests (e.g., Cod. Sang. 904: DOI 10.5076/e-codices-csg-0904, online since 2006); Huygens' eLaborate platform hosts multi-panel collaborative editions (martianus.huygens.knaw.nl; elaborate.huygens.knaw.nl); the ERC Consolidator GlossIT (No. 101123203, PI Bernhard Bauer, Graz) now scales comparative Celtic-Latin gloss editing with HTR/network-analysis/NLP/DNA-alignment methods; OEAW's Wissembourg Priscian Glosses edition (~2,000 Latin + ~150 OHG glosses, Grifoni et al.) extends coverage to East Francia; Network for the Study of Glossing (glossing.org) indexes the field. URLs: https://www.e-codices.unifr.ch/en/list/one/csg/0904 ; https://www.elaborate.huygens.knaw.nl/?page_id=113 ; https://glossit.uni-graz.at/en ; https://www.oeaw.ac.at/acdh/research/dh-research-infrastructure/resources/editions/priscian-glosses ; https://www.glossing.org/articles/digital-editions

11. Scholar map (RQ5) confirmed: Mariken Teeuwen (Huygens/Leiden; Martianus, Marginal Scholarship), Sinead O'Sullivan (QUB; CCCM 237, Prudentius Psychomachia glosses, lemma reading), David Ganz (Corbie in the Carolingian Renaissance 1990; epilogue in Annotated Book), Warren Peze (Ratramnus chapter in Annotated Book; editor of Reading Boethius around 900 vol. containing Teeuwen's "Reading Boethius Around 900"), Franck Cinato (earliest Priscian exposition), Cinzia Grifoni (Wissembourg), Bernhard Bauer/Padraic Moran/Rijcklof Hofman (Celtic-Latin gloss corpora), John J.
