## Extract 1 — Zenodotus of Ephesus, first Librarian and first editor (pp. 119–121)

"His pupil Zenodotus of Ephesus (c. 325 — c. 234 B.C.) was made the first Librarian of the great Alexandrian Library early in the reign of Ptolemy Philadelphus. As Librarian, Zenodotus classified the epic poets, while Alexander Aetolus dealt with the tragic and Lycophron with the comic drama. He compiled a Homeric glossary, in which he was apparently content with merely guessing at the meaning of difficult words. Shortly before 274 he produced the first scientific edition of the Iliad and Odyssey."

"Zenodotus is described as the earliest editor (diorthotes) of Homer; his edition was founded on numerous MSS; each of the two poems was probably now for the first time divided into 24 books, and spurious lines marked with a marginal obelus."

"His recension of Homer was the first recension of any text which aimed at restoring the genuine original." ... "He deserves credit, however, for making the comparison of MSS the foundation of his text. Our knowledge of his criticisms rests almost entirely on statements recorded in the scholia on the Venice MS (A) of Homer."

[Jebb quotation on Zenodotus:] "In the dawn of the new scholarship, he appears as a gifted man with a critical aim, but without an adequate critical method... Still, he broke new ground; his work had a great repute; and to some extent, its influence was lasting."

## Extract 2 — Aristophanes of Byzantium and his new critical signs (pp. 127–128)

"Aristophanes was certainly the originator of several new symbols for use in textual criticism. To the short horizontal dash called the obelos or 'spit' -, which had already been used by Zenodotus to denote a spurious line, he added the asterisk * to draw attention to passages where the sense is incomplete, and, in lyric poets, to indicate a change in the metre; also the keraunion T, to serve as a collective obelus where several consecutive lines are deemed to be spurious; and, lastly, the antisigma, or inverted sigma, D, to draw attention to tautology. These symbols were used in his edition of the Iliad and Odyssey, which marked an advance on that of Zenodotus and the next editor, Rhianus. He agreed with Zenodotus in obelising many lines, but he also reinstated, and obelised, many which had been entirely omitted by his predecessor. Thus he appears to have had some regard for manuscript evidence..."

[Also from p. 129 area:] Aristophanes divided the texts of the lyric poets into metrical kola; "The test of metre was thus easily applied, and interpolations detected." On Pindar "he produced what was probably the first collected edition."

[Aristophanes' canons (lists of chief poets in each genre) are reproduced by Sandys pp. 128–129: Epic (5): Homer, Hesiod, Pisander, Panyasis, Antimachus; Iambic (3); Tragic (5); Old/Middle/New Comic; Elegiac (4); Lyric (9); Orators (10); Historians (10).]

## Extract 3 — Aristarchus of Samothrace: hypomnemata and the six signs (pp. 131–133)

"Aristarchus of Samothrace (c. 220 – 145 B.C.), who lived in Alexandria under Ptolemy Philometor (181 – 146)... His continuous commentaries (hypomnemata) filled no less than 800 volumes, partly as notes for lectures, partly in finished form. These were valued less highly than his critical treatises (syggrammata)... As a commentator he avoided the display of irrelevant erudition, while he insisted that each author was his own best interpreter."

"He had a profound knowledge of Homeric vocabulary, and was the author of two recensions of the Iliad and the Odyssey, with critical and explanatory symbols in the margin of each. These symbols were six in number: (i) the obelus - to denote a spurious line, already used by Zenodotus and Aristophanes; (2) the diple >, denoting anything notable either in language or matter; (3) the dotted diple (diple periestigmene), drawing attention to a verse in which the text of Aristarchus differed from that of Zenodotus; (4) the asterisk, marking a verse wrongly repeated elsewhere; (5) the stigme or dot, used by itself as a mark of suspected spuriousness, and also in conjunction with (6) the antisigma, in a sense differing from that of Aristophanes, to denote lines in which the order had been disturbed, the dots indicating the lines which ought immediately to follow the line marked with the antisigma."

"In his criticisms on Homer three points have been noticed, (i) His careful study of Homeric language... (2) His strong reliance on manuscript authority, and, in cases of conflicting readings, on the poet's usage. In contrast with Zenodotus, he abstained from merely conjectural readings, and was even censured by later critics for excess of caution. (3) His comments on the subject-matter, comparing the Homeric versions of myths with those in other writers..."

## Extract 4 — Dionysius Thrax and the transmission chain (pp. 138–139)

"Another eminent pupil of Aristarchus was Dionysius Thrax (born c. 166 B.C.)... his main title to fame is that he was the author of the earliest Greek Grammar. This is still extant. It is a work of less than 16 printed pages. It begins by defining Grammar, and stating its six parts (anagnosis, exegesis glosson kai historion apodosys, etymologia, analogias eklogismos, krisis poiematon)..."

"It remained the standard work on grammar for at least 13 centuries. It was known to the great grammarians of the imperial age, Apollonius and Herodian. Among its many commentators may be mentioned Choeroboscus (end of cent. 6), Stephanus (early in cent. 7), and (not much later) Heliodorus and Melampus. It became the source of the grammatical catechisms (erotemata) of the Byzantine age, e.g. that of Moschopulos, and also of the manuals introduced into Italy during the Renaissance by Byzantine refugees such as Chrysoloras, Gaza, Constantine Lascaris and Chalcondylas."

[Footnote:] "Engl. trans. by T. Davidson (reprinted from Journal of Speculative Philosophy, St Louis, U.S.A.), 1874: the best text is that of Uhlig, 1883."

## Extract 5 — Didymus, Aristonicus, Herodian, Nicanor and the scholia of Venice MS A (pp. 140–142)

"The most versatile and industrious of all the successors of Aristarchus was Didymus (c. 65 B.C. — 10 A.D.)... He is said to have written between 3500 and 4000 books... we have first to mention his elaborate attempt to restore the Homeric recension of Aristarchus in his work Peri tes Aristarcheiou diorthoseos. Aristarchus had produced two recensions of the text; but both were lost, and Didymus had to restore their readings with the help of transcripts together with such evidence as could be derived from the critical monographs and the continuous commentaries of Aristarchus."

"At the end of each book of the Iliad in the Venice MS of Homer known as A, Didymus is mentioned, together with his younger contemporary, Aristonicus, and Herodian, the author of a treatise on the prosody and accentuation of the Iliad (c. 160 A.D.), and Nicanor, the writer on Homeric punctuation (c. 130 A.D.), as one of the sources of the scholia in that MS."
