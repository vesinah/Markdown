## Translator's preface (verbatim, OCR-corrected lightly)

"This famous little pamphlet, the first attempt at a systematic grammar made in the Western World, and for many generations a text-book in the schools of the Roman Empire, appears, I believe, now for the first time in English. Pretty nearly all that we know about the person of Dionysios is what we are told by Suidas, who says:

'Dionysios the Alexandrian, called the Thracian from [the native country of] his father Teros, was a disciple of Aristarchos, and a grammarian. He was a public professor (grammatikon) in Rome in the time of Pompey the Great, and was preceptor to Tyrannion the Elder. He composed a very large number of grammatical works, as well as set treatises and commentaries.'

The Grammar of Dionysios was first printed (I believe...) in 1816, in Immanuel Bekker's Anecdota Graeca (pp. 629–643) along with the scholia of Charoboskos, Diomedes, Melampous, Porphyry, and Stephanos... The genuineness and authenticity of the work have been impugned, but have been defended by Lersch... and are now generally admitted."

## Sections 1–6 (verbatim translation)

"1. On Grammar. (Grammatike).

Grammar is an experimental knowledge (empeiria) of the usages of language as generally current among poets and prose writers. It is divided into six parts:

1deg. Trained reading with due regard to Prosody;
2deg. Explanation according to poetical figures.
3deg. Ready statement of dialectical peculiarities and allusions (historiai).
4deg. Discovery of Etymology.
5deg. An accurate account of analogies.
6deg. Criticism of poetical productions, which is the noblest part of grammatic art.

2. On Reading (anagnosis).

Reading is the rendering of poetic or prose productions without stumbling or hesitancy. It must be done with due regard to expression, prosody, and pauses. Through the expression we learn the merit (arete) of the piece; from the prosody, the art of the reader; and from the pauses, the meaning intended to be conveyed...

3. On Tone (tonos).

Tone is the resonance of a voice endowed with harmony. It is heightened in the acute, balanced in the grave, and broken in the circumflex.

4. On Punctuation (stigme).

There are three punctuation marks: the full stop, the semicolon, and the comma. The full stop denotes that the sense is complete; the semicolon is a sign of where to take breath; the comma shows that the sense is not yet complete, but that something further must be added.

5. Wherein does the full stop differ from the comma?

In time. At the full stop the pause is long, at the comma, very short.

6. On Rhapsody (rhapsodia).

A Rhapsody is a part of a poem including a certain definite argument. It is called a rhapsody, that is, rhabdody, because those who recited the Homeric poems were girt with a laurel branch (rhabdos)."

## Later sections (summary of verbatim content present in the file)

Sections 7–25 define: Elements/stoicheia (24 letters, vowels/semivowels/mutes), Syllables (long, short, common), the Word (lexis), the Sentence (logos) with eight parts of speech (Noun, Verb, Participle, Article, Pronoun, Preposition, Adverb, Conjunction), Noun with its five accidents (genders, species, forms, numbers, cases) and extensive species taxonomy, Verb (eight accidents incl. five moods), Participle, Article, Pronoun, Prepositions (eighteen), Adverbs (semantic classes), Conjunctions (classes).

Note for the project: section 1's six parts of grammar include "explanation according to poetical figures" (exegesis) and "criticism of poems" (krisis) — the two activities out of which ancient glossing and scholia grew; the treatise itself circulated with multiple ancient scholia (Charoboskos, Diomedes, Melampus, Porphyry, Stephanos per the preface).
