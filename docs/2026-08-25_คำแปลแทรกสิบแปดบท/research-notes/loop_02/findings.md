## Key findings (with URLs)

1. Layout of the Masorah as a two-tier protective apparatus. In the great 10th-11th c. Oriental codices (Codex Cairensis, Aleppo Codex, Leningrad Codex), the Masora Parva runs as heavily abbreviated statistical notes beside the columns (right/left margins), while the Masora Magna expands them in the top and bottom margins; a third layer, the Masora Metsarefet (collative Masorah), closes Oriental/Sephardi volumes. Notes are Aramaic shorthand counts (e.g., lamed-with-dot = hapax; dalet-with-dot = occurs 4x) plus Magna lists identifying verses by snippet quotations - a pre-concordance cross-reference net designed so that any altered spelling, dropped word, or normalized rare form would break the counts. Sources: https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/en/corpusmasoreticum/projekt/beschreibung.html ; https://www.ancientjewreview.com/read/tag/masorah ; https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/jsource/judaica/ejud_0002_0013_0_13385.html

2. Ben Asher family and the great codices - including the dating controversy of Cairo Codex 895. The Cairo Codex of the Prophets carries a colophon attributing it to Moshe ben Asher and dating it 895/896 CE, but radiocarbon testing places it ca. 990 CE and most scholars now deem the colophon a forgery; the manuscript disappeared in 1984, surviving through the Perez-Castro facsimile-edition series (1979-1992). The Aleppo Codex (ca. 930 CE, scribe Shlomo ben Buya'a, vocalization associated with Aaron ben Asher, endorsed by Maimonides) remains the benchmark Tiberian model codex; new work shows its Mp and Mm hands differ (Beiler, Textus 34, 2025). Sources: https://www.ancientjewreview.com/read/tag/masorah ; https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/jsource/judaica/ejud_0002_0013_0_13385.html ; https://brill.com/view/journals/text/34/1/article-p27_3.xml

3. Modern digital Masorah projects. Corpus Masoreticum (Heidelberg, DFG-funded 12-year project since 2018) studies the Ashkenazi Masorah (11th-14th c.) including Masora Figurata micrography, publishing diplomatic editions linked to layout via BIMA 2.1 (IIIF + SVG text paths + graph database; 112 mss, 300,000 transcription lines, open CC-BY-SA) and its own journal CMWP. Open Masorah applies machine learning to extract and collate Masoretic notes and variants across all manuscript witnesses, documenting consonants, vocalization, accents, layout, and scribal practices. Sources: https://corpusmasoreticum.de/ ; https://www.hfjs.eu/en/chairs/bible-and-jewish-interpretation-of-the-bible/research/corpus-masoreticum/project-description.html ; https://www.openmasorah.com/

4. Geonic commentary culture and Saadia's Tafsir. The geonic academies (Sura, Pumbedita) institutionalized Talmud study through yarchei kallah sessions and responsa; Saadia Gaon (882/892-942) wrote the Tafsir, a Judeo-Arabic translation-with-commentary of the Pentateuch that became the standard Bible of Arabic-speaking Jewry (Yemenite communities copied it into nearly all their codices), alongside grammatical tools such as the Agron and a list of rare/hapax biblical words explained from rabbinic Hebrew. Sources: https://www.sefaria.org/topics/saadia-gaon ; https://www.sefaria.org/Tafsir_Rasag ; https://bneiisrael.com/story/saadia-gaon (adapted from R. Brody, The Geonim of Babylonia, Yale UP 1998)

5. Karaite exegetical annotation tradition. Karaites produced a dense Judaeo-Arabic translation-and-commentary culture (Yefet ben Eli on the Prophets; Yeshuah ben Judah's "structural literalism"); Byzantine Karaites added Greek glosses; Judah Hadassi's Eshkol ha-Kofer (12th c.) embedded exegetical argument against rabbinic midrash in encyclopedic form. Modern anchors: Polliack (Brill 1997; SBL 2019 anthology), Frank, Search Scripture Well (Brill 2004), Lasker on Hadassi. Sources: https://english.tau.ac.il/profile/meirap ; https://telaviv.academia.edu/MeiraPolliack ; https://bibliotekanauki.pl/articles/63519088.pdf

6. Andalusi Hebrew philology built on Arabic models. Hayyuj (Fez ca. 950 - Cordova early 11th c.) imported Arabic grammatical theory to found scientific Hebrew grammar, proving all roots are triliteral (Kitab al-Af'al Dhawat Huruf al-Lin; Kitab al-Tanqit on Masoretic vowels); his autograph tradition survives in Cairo Genizah fragments now digitized (T-S K7.51). Ibn Janah (c. 990-1050) critiqued/supplemented him (al-Mustalhaq) and wrote Kitab al-Tanqih (al-Luma = grammar/syntax; al-Usul = root lexicon exploiting Hebrew-Arabic comparison); via ibn Tibbon's Hebrew translation it shaped Qimhi's Sefer ha-Shorashim and hence European Hebraism. Sources: https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/8992-judah-hayyuj ; https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-TS-K-00007-00051/2 ; https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ibn-Janah ; https://sefarad.revistas.csic.es/index.php/sefarad/article/view/735

7. Arabic lexicography as documentation of manuscript study. Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad's Kitab al-'Ayn (8th c., Basra), the first Arabic dictionary, ordered entries phonetically from 'ayn ('deepest' articulation) to mim, drawing examples from Qur'an, hadith, pre-Islamic poetry and proverbs - i.e., a systematic instrument for reading classical texts; it seeded the root-permutation dictionaries culminating in Lisan al-'Arab. Ibn al-Nadim's Fihrist (987-8 CE), compiled by a Baghdad bookseller, is the era's great self-documentation of book culture (authors, titles, papermakers, copyists, sects). Sources: https://arabic-for-nerds.com/history/first-arabic-dictionary/ ; https://sites.dlib.nyu.edu/viewer/books/cornell_aco000133/1 ; https://digitallibrary.al-furqan.com/our_publications_item/bookid/102525 ; https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/9s161650f

8. Annotation in early Qur'an manuscripts (before 14th c.). Earliest mushafs are rasm-only (Hijazi/Kufic, minimal dots); disambiguating i'jam dots appear in the late 7th c. tradition (Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali narrative) and a red-dot vocalization system predominates from the mid-8th c. until the 10th-11th c. shift to harakat signs; colored-dot and verse-divider practices show Syriac parallels (Fedeli). Variant-recitation evidence: the Sana'a palimpsest lower text carries non-standard readings (Sadeghi-Bergmann 2010); regional codices underlie the seven canonical readings fixed by Ibn Mujahid (936 CE); medieval Qur'an manuscripts carry marginal qira'at variant notes (e.g., a 15th-c. illuminated Qur'an whose margins give readings according to different qira'at). The familiar letter-symbol waqf stop-sign set (م ط ج ص لا ...) is a later standardization (Madinah mushaf printing tradition), distinct from the ornamented verse separators of early codices. Sources: http://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/5864/ ; https://www.aai.uni-hamburg.de/voror/personen/fedeli.html (InterSaME project with G. Khan) ; https://uplopen.com/chapters/5090/files/a898257c-f1f7-4607-888e-b6ec4ca1556d.pdf ; https://madainproject.com/historical_quranic_manuscripts ; https://qaari.org/waqf-symbols

9. Isnad verification and ijazah certificates in Islamic book culture. Knowledge texts circulated with chains of named guarantors: hadith collections, qira'at, and books alike required ijazah (license) granted after hearing/recitation (sama'/talaqqi); formal ijazah documents append the isnad back to the author or Prophet. HMML documents a 1687 Medina ijazah appended after the colophon of a Zarruq commentary, licensing a Timbuktu pilgrim to teach the book - showing transregional West African-Medinan scholarly networks. Scholarly framing of ijazah vs licentia docendi: Makdisi (1989) vs Stewart (2005) (literature identified; items themselves paywalled). Sources: https://hmml.org/stories/postscript-a-teaching-license/ ; https://blogs.cuit.columbia.edu/islamicbooks/tag/islamic-manuscript/ (sama'-style reading notes "qira'at jami' hadha al-sharh" in majalis)
