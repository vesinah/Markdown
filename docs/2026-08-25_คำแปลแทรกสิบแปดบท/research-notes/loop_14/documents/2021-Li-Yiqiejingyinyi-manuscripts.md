## Verified facts from the fetched profile and abstracts

1. The work: Xuanying compiled the Yiqiejing yinyi around 660 within Xuanzang's translation bureau in Chang'an; about 400,000 characters in 25 juan covering more than 400 scriptures with over 8,000 entries; the earliest extant Buddhist sound-and-meaning dictionary.
2. Japan transmission: introduced to Japan together with Chinese translations of Buddhist texts in the Nara period and copied extensively; the oldest Japanese copying record dates to 729.
3. Survival pattern inverted: only printed editions survive in China, whereas Japan preserves more than ten old manuscripts - named by holding: Shoso-in 正倉院本, Kongoji 金剛寺本, Horyuji copy dated Daiji 3 法隆寺大治三年写本, Nanatsudera 七寺蔵本, Saihoji 西方寺蔵本, Hiroshima University library copy, University of Tokyo Historiographical Institute copy, Kyoto University Japanese linguistics seminar copy, Tenri Library copy, Koshoji 興聖寺本; printed witnesses include Goryeo first and re-carved editions, Qisha canon, palace copy; Dunhuang-Turfan fragments are held at the British Library and the Bibliotheque nationale de France; a St. Petersburg fragment (Phi230) studied separately.
4. Heian reception: the Zushoryo-bon Ruiju myogisho 類聚名義抄 (compiled ca. 1100 by a Hosso-sect monk) quotes about 1,300 character-radical entries from the Yiqiejing yinyi, the largest such borrowing; Li traces which manuscript line underlies it; her monograph Yiqiejing yinyi koshahon no kenkyu 汲古書院 2021 collects this work.
5. Database: with Liu Guanwei she built the "Yiqiejingyinyi Full-text Database" transcribing nine then ten Japanese manuscripts (adding Koshoji and Dunhuang fragments), released via JSPS project 17J02838.
6. New print source: in 2019 the International College for Postgraduate Buddhist Studies and the National Library of China jointly published the Song Sixi zang 思渓蔵 printing of the Yiqiejing yinyi, valuable as a collation base for the Taisho text (which uses the Goryeo re-carved edition as base).
7. Textual-critical angle: Xuanying's formula jingwen zuo 経文作 ("the scripture writes...") occurs 1,073 times, only in juan 1-20 (old translations), functioning as recorded emendations - useful for our discussion of glosses as textual correction rather than mere phonetics.
