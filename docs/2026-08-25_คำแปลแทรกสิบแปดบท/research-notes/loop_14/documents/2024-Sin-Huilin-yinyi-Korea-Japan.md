## Content summary (from fetched abstract and open-access excerpt text)

Scope: the complete process by which the Yiqiejing yinyi 一切經音義 (also called Huilin yinyi 慧琳音義) was propagated in Korea, preserved there, and reintroduced to China.

Key verified points:

1. Huilin 慧琳, a monk originally from the land of Shule 疏勒, completed the Yiqiejing yinyi in 807; it enriched/expanded Xuanying 玄應's Yiqiejing yinyi completed in 649. Huilin's peculiarity as a foreigner was to favor the linguistic aspect in investigating terms - plausibly written for foreigners understanding Chinese.
2. Qing philologists cite Xuanying yinyi rather than Huilin's edition, implying they had no access to the latter or did not know of its existence; existing research shows Huilin yinyi was lost in China until the late Qing, when it was reintroduced through the Koryŏ Chaejo Taejanggyŏng 高麗再雕大藏經 (re-carved Koryŏ Tripitaka).
3. Early Korean demand: quoting Zanning, in the Xiande 顯德 era of Zhou (954-960) Koryŏ sent an envoy with gold to Zhezhong 浙中 to request the Huilin yinyi, but no print existed, so it was left out - predating even the completion of the Kaibao Dazangjing.
4. Liao route: Huilin yinyi was distributed in Liao after Tang/Five Dynasties and included in the Qidan 契丹 Tripiṭaka (engraved Chongxi era 1032-1054 to Qingning 5 = 1059); Koryŏsa records Qidan Tripiṭakas sent to Koryŏ in Munjong 17 (1063), Munjong 26 (1072), Sukchong 4 (1099), Yejong 2 (1107).
5. Japan connection: the Chaejo Taejanggyŏng was delivered to Japan in response to continued requests. Zenrin kokuhōki 善鄰國寶記 preserves a letter by Zuikei Shūhō 瑞溪周鳳 (1392-1473) written to Chosŏn in Ōei 應永 16 (1409): "I have heard that your noble country is in possession of the Tripiṭaka and its printing plates... if, pitying our poor country's lack, you would grant us the complete canon of seven thousand scrolls..."
6. Copies of the canon were kept in various places in Japan such as Kenninji 建仁寺 (Kyoto) and Zōjōji 増上寺 (Tokyo); the Huilin yinyi was selected from the Tripiṭaka kept at Kenninji and printed as separate books.
7. Chinese reintroduction: Yang Shoujing obtained such a copy in Japan, recognized it as the Huilin yinyi by its difference from Xuanying yinyi, corrected it philologically, and had it sold/reintroduced in China (Shanghai). His own note records finding the printing plates still in Xijing 西京 (Kyoto) and ordering dozens of copies printed through a book dealer.
8. Comparative lost-book cases cited: Longkan shoujing 龍龕手鏡 (Liao original preserved via Koryŏ reprint, National Treasure No. 291 in Korea) and Huang Kan's Lunyu yishu 論語義疏 - books lost in China but preserved and returned via neighboring countries.

## Notes for drafting

- Use this as the spine of the "Huilin branch" of the transmission bridge: China -> (Liao canon) -> Koryŏ -> (Koryŏ re-carved canon) -> Japan (1409 request letter; Kenninji/Zōjōji copies) -> late-Qing return via Yang Shoujing.
- Contrast with the Xuanying branch (see findings.md): Xuanying yinyi reached Japan directly in the Nara period and survives only in Japanese manuscripts, while its Chinese textual line continues only through Song prints (Sixi zang) and Qing scholarly editions (Zhuang Xin 1786 etc., per chinaknowledge.de).
