## Verified content

1. Origins: digitization led by Shimoda Masahiro with support from a temple-based support association organized by Takasaki Jikido (1926-2013) and Nara Yasuaki (Bukkyo Gakujutsu Shinkokai), which paid about half of expenses; roughly 250 collaborators served as typists.
2. Technical challenge: OCR could not handle the Taisho's approximately 12,000 types of old character forms, so input was manual.
3. Notes handling: because plain text cannot represent notes, deliberate font-size changes, and similar apparatus, pages of the Taisho were scanned into images and made directly available alongside the text - directly relevant to our discussion of interlinear/apparatus layers in digital editions.
4. 2012 edition features: revamped search engine, textual correction, zoomable original page images, Digital Dictionary of Buddhism linkage, INBUDS article search linking to CiNii PDFs, SARDS bibliographic links, BDK English Tripitaka parallel-line pop-ups, CHISE and Unihan ideographic resources.
5. International links: parallel viewing of texts against the Tripitaka Koreana; IIIF used to present images; text-to-image links expanding to Waseda University, University of Tokyo, British Library, National Diet Library.
6. CBETA story: it was SAT that made CBETA's edition possible - SAT representatives persuaded the president of Daizo Shuppan (whose postwar pirated-edition losses had made it refuse CBETA) to relax restrictions, reasoning that the canon database should be shared as common property even where CBETA competed with SAT.
7. Digital humanities legacy: SAT acted as a pivotal factor for DH in Japan; JADH formed 2011 with Shimoda as chair, joining ADHO in 2013.
