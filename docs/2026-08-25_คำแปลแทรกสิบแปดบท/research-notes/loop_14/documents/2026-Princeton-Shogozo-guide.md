## Verified content (page fetched in full)

1. Identity and location: the Shogozo is a repository for sutra manuscripts in the Todai-ji compound, next to the Shoso-in treasure house; it was originally part of Sonsho-in 尊勝院, a subtemple built in 955 that was the center of Kegon and Shingon studies at Todai-ji; the present structure dates to the late twelfth century; building and scrolls were relocated to the Shoso-in compound in 1896; managed today by the Imperial Household Agency.
2. Textual importance: the Taisho canon rests primarily on the thirteenth-century second Koryo woodblock edition and introduced many new errors; Shogozo holds around 1,500 Nara-period scrolls including manuscripts imported directly from Sui-Tang China and Silla, hence a better witness to seventh-eighth century text states; Nara-period copies were proofread multiple times and collated against other manuscripts, making them highly accurate.
3. Coverage comparison: Dunhuang materials contain only about thirty percent of the Buddhist canon, whereas Nara and Heian manuscripts can be gathered to comprise nearly the entire canon.
4. Reading marks: some manuscripts show white marks (hakuten 白点) used to trace early Japanese reading practices - key evidence for pre-kana glossing stages.
5. Holdings statistics: 783 titles in 4,960 scrolls, divided into copied sutras 写経之部 (Sui sutras 8 titles/22 scrolls; Tang sutras 30 titles/221 scrolls; Tenpyo 12 [740] gogan kyo vow canon 126 titles/750 scrolls; Jingo Keiun 2 [770] gogan kyo 171 titles/742 scrolls; two miscellaneous copied groups), printed sutras 版経之部 (Kanji-era 1088 print; Song prints 12 titles/114 scrolls; two miscellany groups), and miscellanea 雑書之部 (15 titles/18 scrolls).
6. Revision of attribution: research by Iida Takehiko (2012, Shoso-in kiyo 34) determined that most scrolls labeled Jingo Keiun gogan kyo actually come from the Imako ichibu 今更一部 canon sponsored by Emperor Konin.
7. Key references listed on page: Iida Takehiko 2011 (Pulgyohak ribyu 9); Kunaicho facsimile publication Kunaicho Shoso-in Jimusho shozo Shogozo kyokan (Maruzen, 2000-, currently 101 discs); Nara Imperial Museum catalog 1930; link to the Nihon koshakyo database 日本古写経データベース http://koshakyo-database.icabs.ac.jp/index.seam

## Companion facts verified elsewhere this loop (Nanatsudera)

- Kyoto National Museum entry: Nanatsudera sutras are copies of the entire Buddhist canon transcribed between Jo'an 5 (1175) and Jisho 3 (1179), commissioned as a Buddhist vow by Onakatomi Yasunaga, a local official of Owari Province; 3,398 handscrolls and 1,556 accordion-style books extant; red top/bottom ruling throughout, both horizontal and vertical rules only in the Daihannya scrolls; museum note flags recent discoveries of disputed long-lost sutra copies attributed to the set. https://knmdb.kyohaku.go.jp/eng/8052.html
- Series: Nanatsudera koitsu kyoten kenkyu sosho 七寺古逸經典叢書, Daito shuppansha (as cited by Keyworth 2020). Ochiai Toshinori's discovery of previously lost texts at Nanatsudera in 1990 is described as one of the most important finds of the twentieth century.
- Scale context: over twenty sets of the Issaikyo were copied during the Nara period (KCI article).
