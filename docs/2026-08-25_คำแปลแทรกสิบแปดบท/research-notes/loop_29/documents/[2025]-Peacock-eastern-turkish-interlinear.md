## Abstract (verbatim)
"This article discusses interlinear translations in Middle Turkic/Medieval Eastern Turkish (also known as Qarakhanid and Khwarazmian Turkish). The article surveys the extant interlinear Qur'anic translations that have come down to us, and argues these are closely connected with a slightly earlier Persian tradition, exemplified by the Samanid-era translation of al-Tabari's Tafsir. The article discusses the evidence for the context of production and circulation of these texts, and concludes by adducing some evidence for later non-Qur'anic interlinear translations in Khwarazmian and Chaghatay. It argues that these interlinear manuscripts represent an elite form of cultural production."

## Key content extracted
1. First attestation and framing:
   - "The practice of interlinear translation in the Islamic world is first attested in Central Asia in the tenth to eleventh centuries, and interlinear Qur'ans represent one of the earliest forms of textual production in both Persian and Turkish, constituting two closely linked traditions."
   - Linked to vernacularisation under Samanids of Transoxiana (r. 892-999) and Qarakhanids (r. 999-1211).

2. Census of Eastern Turkish interlinear Qur'an manuscripts (Eckmann 1971 knew five; now eight with three Mashhad codices):
   - John Rylands Library, Manchester, MSS Arabic 760-773: copied second half of 14th c., no later than 1400; stylistically dated c. 735/1335 (after David James, Qur'ans of the Mamluks); TRILINGUAL Arabic-Persian-Turkish; 14 volumes survive of a 30-volume set; endowed as waqf by Mamluk amir Aytamish to his madrasa in Cairo before/around 802/1400; art-historical provenance suggested: eastern Anatolia, northwestern Iran, or Karamanid south-central Anatolia; affinities with a Qur'an stand (rahla) made for the shrine of Jalal al-Din Rumi in Konya. Further fragments: Chester Beatty Library, Dublin (identified by Eckmann 1969) and Los Angeles County Museum of Art M.73.5.490 (first folio of juz' 4).
   - St Petersburg Tafsir, Institute of Oriental Manuscripts RAS, S 197: Qarakhanid Turkish text, Khwarazmian commentary, 17th-c. manuscript; edition by Ibrahim Halil Usta, Orta Asya Kur'an Tefsiri (Poyraz, 2011); lexicon studied by A.K. Borovkov (1963; TDK Turkish trans. 2002).
   - TIEM 73 (Turkish and Islamic Arts Museum, Istanbul): mixed Khwarazmian and Qarakhanid text (Eckmann 1959, Ural-Altaische Jahrbucher 31: 72-85).
   - Hekimoglu Cami 2: copied 762/1363, possibly Konya or Erzincan.
   - Three Mashhad codices unknown to Eckmann, incl. Astan-i Quds 293 (gives multiple Turkish equivalents per Arabic word), Astan-i Quds 2229, Astan-i Quds 1007; plus a Tashkent manuscript.
   - Consensus: single Ur-text between the tenth and twelfth centuries; Transoxianan origin supported by Persian orthography ("Khurasani verbs", 2nd pl. ending -at for standard -ad) in the Rylands Persian text.

3. Persian background:
   - Persian model: Samanid-era Persian translation of al-Tabari's Tafsir; cf. Travis Zadeh, The Vernacular Qur'an: Translation and the Rise of Persian Exegesis (OUP 2012), 312-321.
   - The Rylands Persian sometimes diverges toward the exegetical paraphrase tradition of the Tafsir-i Shanqashi (11th-early 12th c., Khurasan/Transoxiana, itself an interlinear text), e.g. martial reading of Q 3:200.
   - Following Eckmann: the Turkish most commonly follows the Persian even where Persian differs from the Arabic.

4. Audience/context:
   - Luxury codices: Rylands has only three lines of Arabic thuluth per page, broad margins, gold rosettes marking reading pauses; Tashkent ms in generously spaced thuluth, six lines/page.
   - By the time Aytamish commissioned/acquired it, its Qarakhanid Turkish would have been archaic for Qipchaq/Oghuz audiences in Egypt: comprehensibility was not the sole motive but "Turcophone cultural prestige"; multilingualism associated with Sufi ideas of divine communication; connections with Sufi milieus (Kutadgu bilig, Atabetu'l-hakayik analogies).
   - Later non-Qur'anic interlinear translations attested in Khwarazmian and Chaghatay (elite practice continuing beyond the Qur'an corpus).
