## Key findings (with URLs)

1. **The biblia glosata layout, defined from the sources:** "The Gloss is made up of the complete Bible text presented integrally in a central column and surrounded by an apparatus of authoritative, mostly patristic and Carolingian exegetical excerpts; on either side of the biblical text we find the longer, so-called marginal glosses, and in-between the lines the shorter, so-called interlinear glosses" (Ekman 2019, quoting the Froehlich-Gibson facsimile introduction). The two layers are functionally coupled: Smalley showed "the Marginal Gloss is unintelligible except as a complement to the Interlinear" (Williams 2016, n.50). Gibson adds that interlinear glossing entered biblical books ("double glossing") from the grammatical teaching of pagan classics, earlier psalters carrying only marginal apparatus (Ekman 2019, citing Gibson, "Carolingian Glossed Psalters"). https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/4393c977-3898-40a0-be9e-91b8f648ed78/content ; https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0513E60BA78C2E9BC97BA387B6A9CCBD/S0362152916000106a.pdf/glossa_ordinaria_and_glossa_hebraica_midrash_in_rashi_and_the_gloss.pdf

2. **Attribution debate resolved into layers, not authors:** the old attribution to Walafrid Strabo (d. 849) was floated by Johannes Trithemius around 1500, entered the Gloss itself only in the 1590 Paris edition, was split in the 1617 Douai/Antwerp edition into "marginal = Strabo, interlinear = Anselm," and was perpetuated by Migne PL 113-114 (which printed only the marginal gloss under Strabo's works). Berger (1893) noted no pre-twelfth-century manuscripts exist ("There are, in fact, none."); de Blic (1949) closed the case; Smalley reassigned the compilation to Anselm of Laon (d. 1117), his brother Ralph (d. ca. 1133) and Gilbert Universalis (d. 1134, firmly credited with Lamentations). Gibson's one-line summary: "Every schoolchild knows that the Glossa Ordinaria consists of two parts ... But every schoolchild also knows that this is wrong." Andree's current revision: Anselm composed continuous commentaries (e.g. Glosae super Iohannem, ed. Andree) that pupils transformed into glossed books. https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/4393c977-3898-40a0-be9e-91b8f648ed78/content ; https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file-4669631 ; https://www.jstor.org/stable/45019708

3. **Manuscript evidence pins genesis at Laon c. 1120-1135:** per Stirnemann the three earliest Gloss mss (Laon BM 51 Job; BM 74 Matthew+Canticles; BM 78 John+Catholic Epistles) date 1120-1135, were produced AND still stand at Laon; nineteen further mss pre-1135/40 cover Genesis, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Apocalypse; ten of these twenty-two were written at Laon. The John-Gloss can only be Laon work because it uses Eriugena's unique John-commentary manuscript kept at Laon since the ninth century (Andree). Scale: an estimated 2000 manuscripts of parts of the Gloss survive; from c. 1135 glossed bibles are among the commonest surviving twelfth-century manuscripts, outnumbering unglossed Bibles. Same URLs as above; https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/new-cambridge-history-of-the-bible/glossed-bible/5A34F27F69257E86A44D3FFE2BE99E15

4. **Giant pandect Bibles are the monastic counter-format, not the Gloss format:** England's Giant-Bible high point falls in the later twelfth century — generous dimensions, pandect format (whole Bible in one volume), Romanesque display decoration; named exemplars: Bury Bible (CCCC 2), Dover Bible (CCCC 3), Lambeth Bible, Winchester Bible. By the early thirteenth century fashion shifted "towards more compact volumes that prioritised comfortable consultation and transport over monumental effect" — i.e., the commercial multi-volume glossed set and then the pocket Paris Bible displaced the lectern pandect. https://cudl.lib.cam.ac.uk/view/MS-DD-00008-00012/2

5. **Print history: editio princeps Strasbourg 1480/81 by Adolph Rusch for Anton Koberger** (Pitts Library: "printed in 1480 in Strasbourg by Adolf Rusch for the Nuremberg-based publisher Anton Koberger"); four-volume Biblia latina cum glossa ordinaria, 2415 pp., facsimile reprint with introductions by Froehlich and Gibson (Brepols 1992). Follow-ups: Basel Amerbach 1494/95 adding Nicholas of Lyra's Postilla; Venice Paganinus 1495 whose extra canon-treatise by Bernardino Gadolo was later misattributed inside PL to "Strabo." The Rusch print is now the base of the CNRS electronic edition (Gloss-e/Sacra Pagina: 500,000 indexed sentences, ISTC ib00607000) and of the ongoing Emmaus Academic English translation. https://pitts.emory.edu/tag/glossa-ordinaria ; https://gloss-e.irht.cnrs.fr/php/introduction.php ; https://glossaordinaria.com/

6. **Reformation use verified in detail — and it is a story of replacing the Gloss with blank space:** Luther's first Psalms lecture course (Wittenberg 1513-15) worked "on the basis of and in discussion with ancient, Church Fathers' and medieval authorities as well as common biblical glosses" (SLUB, on the autograph Mscr.Dresd.A.138). For that course he had the university printer Johann Grunenberg produce a Latin Psalter with broad margins and generous interlinear space so students could write down HIS comments instead of reading through the inherited apparatus (Cortez 2020). His own hand-annotated copy of that 1513 Grunenberg Psalter — autograph glosses on all 150 psalms — survives at Wolfenbuttel (HAB Cod. Guelf. 71.4 Theol.); both objects UNESCO Memory of the World since 2015. Kolb confirms Luther "used the Glossa ordinaria," citing Froehlich's dedicated essay (Lutheran Quarterly 23 [2009]: 29-48). The specific claim that Luther annotated a personal copy OF THE PRINTED GLOSSA itself: (ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน). https://www.slub-dresden.de/en/explore/manuscripts/luthers-psalmenkommentar ; https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2336&context=pubs ; https://promissio.ilt.edu/index.php/pj/article/download/99/99

7. **Law faculties built their own Glossa ordinaria on the same architecture:** Accursius (c. 1184-1263) gathered 97,000 annotations into the Magna glossa on Justinian's Corpus Iuris Civilis, usable "both for teaching and for judicial procedures," treated ever after as "the official interpretation"; Britannica: its authority was for nearly a century "no less than that of the original Roman texts" and it remained indispensable "for the next 500 years" (i.e., until humanist jurisprudence). Canon law parallel: Johannes Teutonicus's Apparatus on Gratian's Decretum, corrected by Bartholomew of Brescia (d. 1258), became the standard Glossa ordinaria on the Decretum (post-1234); the Rome 1582 Corpus Iuris Canonici cum Glossa is digitized at UCLA; the decretals' ordinary gloss began with Bernard of Botone. https://www.unibo.it/en/university/who-we-are/our-history/famous-people-and-students/Accursius ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Glossa-ordinaria-by-Accursius ; https://web.colby.edu/canonlaw/decretum/ ; https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02315b.htm

8. **Interlinear gloss and university lecture practice are two sides of one pedagogy:** Andree: "the essential function of the Glossa has been overlooked: it was a taught text" — Peter Lombard, Comestor, Langton, the Chanter and Hugh of St-Cher all lectured from it. Comestor's ca.-1160 lectures on the glossed Gospels "survive in the form of student transcripts" (reportatio form), edited from Troyes BM 1024, preserving conversational Latin and direct address to students. Even "ordinaria" is retrospective: twelfth-century users simply said glosa; no standard text existed before perhaps the late twelfth or more likely the thirteenth century. The CNRS Aquinas-postilla editorial file even reads textual lacunae as "stigmates de la reportation" — transcription traces inside commentaries descended from lectures. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/peter-comestors-lectures-on-the-glossa-ordinaria-on-the-gospel-of-john-the-bible-and-theology-in-the-twelfthcentury-classroom/8FCBAABCB7FA0A1142B178042ED2EE2F ; https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9780888444875-1 ; https://gloss-e.irht.cnrs.fr/php/introduction.php

9. **Modern scholarship wave confirmed and mapped:** Smith 2009 (Brill Commentaria 3) is "the first history of the twelfth-century Glossa Ordinaria ... and the first university textbook," based on manuscript evidence; critical editions exist for Genesis-related prologues, Lamentations (Andree 2005), Song of Songs (Dove CCCM 170, 1997), Ecclesiastes (Kostoff-Kaard ST 224, 2021), plus English translations (Emmaus 2023-24). Ekman 2019 (Toronto) tests Anselm's authorship across the whole Psalms-commentary web and argues against single-source stemmas and against attributing one commentary to Anselm directly. Giraud, Per verba magistri (2010) is the comprehensive Anselm/Laon school study. Kostoff-Kaard preserves Smalley's founding call (1935-1961 articles) for editions from EARLY manuscripts as the field's programme. https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/people/professor-lesley-smith ; https://pims.ca/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/ST224-intro.pdf ; https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/items/2b8ac047-07b9-412f-9232-e9a912f6c296

10. **Jewish parallel apparatus (Rashi/Mikraot Gedolot) as comparative horizon:** Gloss (ca. 1110-20) and Rashi's commentaries emerged within decades in northern France; both transmitted midrashic material (Abraham in the furnace: Nimrod/idols in Rashi, refusal of the Chaldean cult in the Gloss) largely through shared late-antique channels (Genesis Rabba, Jerome's Hebrew Questions). Layout influence between biblia glosata and Rabbinic Bible is disputed (Sirat: Jewish imitation of Christian books; van Liere: reverse via print; Salomon: Talmud-page model; Beit-Arie/Lobrichon/Matter: caution). https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0513E60BA78C2E9BC97BA387B6A9CCBD/S0362152916000106a.pdf/glossa_ordinaria_and_glossa_hebraica_midrash_in_rashi_and_the_gloss.pdf
