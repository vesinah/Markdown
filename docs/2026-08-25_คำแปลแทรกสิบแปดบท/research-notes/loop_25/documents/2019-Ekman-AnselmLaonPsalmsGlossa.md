## Abstract (verbatim)

> This thesis studies the textual relationships between a group of related early scholastic commentaries on the Psalms. At the centre of the discussion stands the commentary which is often said to epitomize the developments in teaching which emerged within the cathedral schools in the twelfth century, namely the so-called Glossa ordinaria on the Bible, and its association with Anselm of Laon, one of the most celebrated theologians of the period. Despite its central place in twelfth-century intellectual culture, relatively little has been able to be conclusively established regarding the origins of the Gloss.

> Chapter Three also begins to examine the relationships that exist within the larger group and introduces a new hypothesis for the place of the Gloss in the family tree and for Anselm's involvement in its creation. The fourth chapter analyses the relationships of the group as a whole, demonstrating how they are all related to one another, but that none can be the sole source of the rest of the group.

## Key extracted passages (verbatim from PDF extraction)

### The physical format of the Gloss (quoting Froehlich-Gibson facsimile introduction)

> The Gloss is made up of the complete Bible text presented integrally in a central column and surrounded by an apparatus of authoritative, mostly patristic and Carolingian exegetical excerpts; on either side of the biblical text we find the longer, so-called marginal glosses, and in-between the lines the shorter, so-called interlinear glosses.

### The Walafrid Strabo myth and its dismantling

> [The Gloss came] to be the work of the Carolingian abbot Walafrid Strabo (d. 849) [by an] attribution [due to] the sixteenth-century German humanist Johannes Trithemius, who is responsible for a number of spurious attributions of medieval works to German authors; this attribution appears for the first time in the Gloss itself in the 1590 Paris edition, which also names Anselm as a later co-author. This double authorship became, in the 1617 Douay/Antwerp edition, a division between the authorship of the marginal and the interlinear glosses, the former being attributed to Strabo and the latter to Anselm. These attributions were accepted by Jacques Paul Migne, who, as he was only interested in printing the "original" form of the Gloss, consequently printed in the Patrologia Latina only the marginal glosses among Strabo's works.

> The attribution to Strabo was first brought into question in 1893 by Samuel Berger, who noted that there are almost no manuscripts of the Gloss that can be dated to before the twelfth century. (There are, in fact, none.) Berger's lingering reservations [...] were finally dismissed in 1949 by Jean de Blic [...]

### Earliest manuscripts (Patricia Stirnemann's dating)

> According to Patricia Stirnemann, the three earliest manuscripts of the Gloss should be dated to 1120-1135. All three of these were produced and are currently found at Laon; they contain the Gloss on Job (Laon, BM, 51), on the Gospel of Matthew and Canticles (Laon, BM, 74), and on the Gospel of John and the Catholic Epistles (Laon, BM, 78). In addition to these codices, there are nineteen other manuscripts that Stirnemann dates to before 1135-1140, which, in addition to the books already mentioned, contain also the Gloss on Genesis, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and the Apocalypse. Out of these twenty-two manuscripts, ten were, according to Stirnemann, produced at Laon.

### Gibson on interlinear vs marginal functions in Carolingian psalters

> Originally, interlinear glosses were used predominantly in the texts of pagan authors employed in the teaching of grammar and rhetoric, while early glossed biblical books would include only a marginal apparatus. Gibson associates the introduction of interlinear glosses into biblical books ("double glossing") with [the explanatory materia tradition].

### Meaning of "ordinaria"

> Guy Lobrichon notes that, from the time of Isidore of Seville (d. 636) to Hugh of St Victor (d. 1141), 'glosa' had been understood simply as a single explanatory word [...] but that a new definition appeared [in the twelfth century].

### Anselm of Laon's contemporary fame (quoted by Ekman from Giraud, Per verba magistri)

> [Chronicler Robert of Auxerre:] "De quo dixisse fertur Eugenius papa quia Deus spiritum eius suscitaverit, ne scriptura periret." ("About whom pope Eugene is told to have said that God stirred up his spirit in order that Scripture not perish.")
> "[...] splendidissima lumina Galliarum, Lauduni gloria [...]" (epitaph material gathered in Giraud, Per verba magistri: Anselme de Laon et son ecole au XIIe siecle, Turnhout: Brepols, 2010.)

> Anselm is attested as chancellor in 1095, as dean from at least 1109 (but possibly already from 1098), and, finally, as archdeacon in 1114.
