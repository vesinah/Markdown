## Key findings (with URLs)

1. DEFINITION CONFIRMED VERBATIM. Herngseng 2023 (Manusya 26.1, pp. 1-23, DOI 10.1163/26659077-25010027, open access): "a type of translation in which each Pali word is followed by its relevant annotative interpretation, known as nissaya". Braun (2013, 67, 174 as cited there): a Burmese text that translates and explains Pali works on a systematic word-by-word basis; most Pali-to-Burmese translation happened through nissayas. Nissaya is not mere translation but an encyclopaedic compact of interpretive resources. https://brill.com/view/journals/mnya/26/1/article-p1_004.xml

2. ETYMOLOGY FROM VINAYA VERIFIED VIA FETCHED SOURCE. Pali nissaya = concept in Mahavagga of Vinaya-pitaka (Oldenberg 1879 ed., p. 58): a newly ordained monk/nun must live under the guidance of the preceptor (upajjhaya) at least five years; if he loses his preceptor he must seek nissaya from a teacher. Equivalent to Sanskrit nisraya/asraya "that on which anything depends"; Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism: "requisite, reliance, support" (the four requisites list follows). Genre sense = "guidance/reliance". Same URL as above.

3. HISTORY / OLDEST EXTANT. Per Tin Lwin 1961 (unpublished MA thesis, University of London; cited in Herngseng 2023, 5-11): oldest extant Burmese nissaya is the Khuddakasikkha Nikaya nissaya, written during the reign of Narapatisithu (1174-1211). NOTE: within the same fetched article the attribution appears twice with different authors (Mahakassapa in the Tin Lwin passage; "Minister Thanbyin, a Pali scholar at the court of Narapatisithu" in a later section fragment) - the author discrepancy must be flagged, not resolved silently. Walker (JAOS 140.3 [2020], p. 678 as cited by Herngseng): Pali-Burmese nissayas extant from the LATE THIRTEENTH century; several Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts listed in the 1442 inscription of the Thaungdwing governor (lengthy list of Pali/Sanskrit works with their nissayas donated to a monastery). Ruiz-Falques (2015, 10, quoted in Herngseng): no grammatical nissayas attested from the Pagan period; popularization plausibly from the 15th c. Sri Lankan parallel verified: Suttanipata-sannaya, Pali-Sinhala word-to-word translation of the 12th century, earliest sannaya (Pruitt 1992, 278 as cited). Pyu antecedent: 7th-c. Sanskrit-Pyu nissaya found at Sriksetra excavation (Tin Lwin 2014 as cited). https://brill.com/view/journals/mnya/26/1/article-p1_004.xml ; https://lockwoodonlinejournals.com/index.php/jaos/article/view/679

4. GENRE SYSTEM. Terminology verified in fetched sources: nissaya (word-for-word support); kyan = treatise/book (e.g., "Pali-Myanmar Nissaya-kyan myar", title of Tin Lwin 2014 in Myanmar Studies Journal 3: 147-161, from Herngseng's reference list). SEVEN INTERPRETIVE METHODS VERIFIED as reported from Karunika (2008, 10) in the fetched Herngseng full text: (1) pathanusari - plain rendering without added words; (2) pathasesa - authoritative inserted words ("extra text"), never personal opinion; (3) saddattha - literal; (4) sauketattha/sanketattha - traditional precedent; (5) pannattyattha - conventional terminology; (6) voharattha - conventional usage; (7) adhippayattha - direct/intuitive meaning. SCHOOLS: Mandalay method vs Pakhokku (Pakokku) method differ pedagogically (months-long analysis of one formula vs other approach; detail in Dhammasami 2004 dissertation, pp. 134-147, as cited); historical recensions: Pagan/Ava-period nissayas terser (Ne Yin Sayadaw nissaya), later Pakokku nissayas more expansive (Gandhasarabhivamsa interview in Herngseng). Janakabhivamsa's Bhāsātikā series (1900-1977 reformer) = dominant modern graded nissaya handbooks; his eight-step pedagogy from nissaya to independent Pali reading reproduced in full. Same Brill URL.

5. MANUSCRIPT EVIDENCE. Palm-leaf (pe sa) culture documented: Pitakataik at Old Bagan (monument 1587, attributed Anawrahta, repaired Bodawpaya 1783; 1273 donor built monastery+library for 2,300 ticals silver, set of Pitaka cost 3,000 ticals - baganpaper.com, use cautiously); scholarly anchor = G.H. Luce & Tin Htway, "A 15th Century Inscription and Library at Pagan, Burma," Malalasekera Commemoration Volume (1976): 203-256 (title confirmed by NLA Luce finding aid folder listing; JPTS 2009 PDF variant "Fifth Century" appears to be a typo). Classical Pitakataik catalogue available in English: Peter Nyunt trans., Catalogue of the Pitaka and Other Texts in Pali, Pali-Burmese, and Burmese by Mang-kri Mahasirijeyasu (PTS Bristol, 2012). Gilded kammavaca texts (Pali ordination formulae, not nissayas but same writing culture): black lacquer "tamarind-seed"/square script on gilded lacquered palm leaf, stiffened cloth (often monks' robes), ivory, metal sheets; BL Add. 15289 (18th c.), Or. 12010E/H/A, Or. 13896 (19th c.). https://www.bl.uk/stories/blogs/posts/kammavaca-burmese-buddhist-ordination-manuscripts ; http://sealang.net/archives/luce/UThawKaung.pdf ; https://baganpaper.com/pitakataik

7. CONTEMPORARY PRACTICE VERIFIED WITH PRIMARY EVIDENCE. Herngseng 2023 documents present-day classroom mechanics: teacher reads aloud, zaso ("repeater") repeats louder/clearer; other students perform ane'-de = marking annotations in margins of printed Pali/Atthakatha textbooks (photo: Veluvan Monastery, Taunggyi; Fig 3 caption explicitly: "student-monks interlineating nissaya's interpretation in their printed textbook"). Related techniques named: ane' kau' (interpretation), ane' pjan' (translation), ane' pwa (extended meaning), ane' hpwin (definition), ane' hpo (elucidation), ane' adei' be (interpreted meaning). Sequence: memorize Pali first, then consult nissaya (Dhammasami 2004, 42 as cited: Mangala-sutta then Sigalovada-sutta). Production scale: 22+ nissayas on Abhidhammattha-sangaha alone in the 19th century (Abhidhamma Tha-main Chok 1965 as cited); Shin-kyint-wut novice-rules nissaya compiled 30+ times (Dhammasami 2018, 21 as cited). Official Chatthasangiti Tipitaka Burmese translation reproduces nissaya wording nearly verbatim, adding pāṭhasesa insertions in parentheses (Clark 2015, 114; Pruitt 1992, 278). Same Brill URL.

8. COMPARATIVE FRAME. Parallel genres: sannaya (Sri Lanka), nissaya/namasadda/vohara (N. Thailand, Laos; McDaniel 2008, 119, 131-136: vohara cites 12-25 words + creative commentary; Thai-style nissaya cites 4-10 words; Burmese style closest to namasadda density). Thai gambhi-nisai (Kaccayana Gambhi-nisai; Saddasaratthajalini nisai) exist but nissaya is marginal in Thai examinations (Pathompong 2016, 30-32 as cited); von Hinuber 1997, 100-101 on namasap < nama-sabda. Walker 2020 abstract (fetched): mainland SE Asian bitexts arranged "in both interlinear and interphrasal formats" enabled vernacular literatures to grow from Pali genres - directly usable comparative paragraph for the book.
