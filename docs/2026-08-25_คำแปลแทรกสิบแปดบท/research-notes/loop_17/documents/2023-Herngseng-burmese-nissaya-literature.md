== ABSTRACT ==

In some of the earliest Buddhist literature found in Myanmar, a type of translation in which each
Pali word is followed by its relevant annotative interpretation, known as nissaya, can be found.
Previous studies (Tin Lwin 1961; Pruitt 1992; McDaniel 2008; Clark 2015) only examined what a
nissaya is and to what literary genre nissaya literature belongs. In this paper, I will explore the
various ways in which Burmese nissaya literature evolved into a mainstream monastic learning
method, its role in the monastic education in Burma in particular, and further investigate whether
the nissayas can be applied more generally and what the relationship is between nissaya and
official Burmese Tipitaka translation. Lastly, this study argues that the Burmese nissaya literature
can be compared to be on par with the encyclopaedic compilation on a specific subject.

Keywords: nissaya literature; translation; monastic learning method; encyclopaedia; Burma history.

== 1 INTRODUCTION ==

Since the introduction of Theravada Buddhism to Pagan in the late 12th ce by Arahan Thera, a native
of Thaton, Burmese Buddhism began to grow in tandem with the flourishing Buddhist civilization.
Before long the monarch and the people embraced the new faith, Pali and Abhidhamma, and learning
was soon at the forefront of the populace's interests in this period (Bode 2014, 12-14). There was a
wide range of grammatical treatises written by scholar monks. The best known among them was
Saddaniti, authored by Aggavamsa in 1154 ce, a landmark of its kind that points to the level of Pali
language and grammar skills at the time (Ruiz-Falques 2017, 227).

Subsequently, Burmese literature ... flourished in the late 13th century ce of Pagan and 15th ce of
Ava. The motifs of early Burmese poetry were most commonly religion, royalty, nature and love
(Hla Pe 1985, 4-6). Dhammasami (2018, 18, 159) asserts that Mahá Ratt-hasara (1468-1529) and his
contemporary Mahá Silavamsa (1453-1518) were well-known for their masterpieces of Pyo poetry in
the royal court of Ava. Along with this poetic literature, it is believed that a kind of Pali-Burmese
translation work developed in this medieval period, which came to be known as nissaya, a literary
genre of commentary on Pali texts. Such a literary genre, consisting of a word-to-word translation,
was already popular in Sri Lanka and found among Pyu and Mon literature. Archaeological excavation
in Myanmar found that the Sanskrit-Pyu nissaya at Sriksetra in the 7th ce, and Kyansittha's
inscriptions at Pagan in 12th ce were the earliest forms of nissaya. The nissaya in its modern form
presents different ways of interpretating and understanding Pali texts. In the traditional Burmese
monastic learning method, the nissaya plays an important role on account of the different levels of
meaning that it can extract from a text. McDaniel (2008, 131-136) calls this method of learning
"lifting words" in similar monastic learning institutions in northern Thailand and Laos.

In traditional Buddhist learning, Pannabhoga (2020, 18) states that although nissaya is regarded as
a sort of translation work, it would be difficult to categorize it as mere translation in the modern
academic sense of the term because nissaya may offer more meanings in the monastic education
system. On the other hand, standard Burmese translations of Buddhist texts have been unpopular as
the language appears unnatural and not refined enough for general readers or academics. The reason
is that current Tipitaka Burmese translations were obviously adopted from old nissayas. Similarly,
Clark (2015, 114) notes that the Chatthasangayana official annotated translation of Pali texts is
nearly identical to that of nissaya wording; the translations are merely reproductions or adoptions
from a certain nissaya by simply removing the Pali words. Until today, traditional Burmese scholars
who provide monastic education in institutions throughout the country consider the textbooks of
nissaya as either guidebooks, manuals or handbooks for students and teachers alike. Modern Buddhist
scholars: Okell (1965), Pruitt (1992), McDaniel (2008), Lammerts (2010), Blackburn (2010), Walker
(2020) and others have noted that nissaya literature has been important in monastic education since
at least the 15th century if not long before and continues to be so today.

== 2 RESEARCH METHODOLOGY, SCOPE AND FRAMEWORKS ==

Qualitative techniques: interviews with experts and students, primary and secondary literature, and
the author's personal observations in monastic classrooms over almost two decades as monastic
student and teacher. As a novice he was instructed to memorize a Pali passage, then the trainer
produced the interpretation in nissaya format.

Scope: canonical Pali texts, Atthakatha (commentaries), tika (sub-commentaries) and Anutika
(sub-sub-commentaries), all translated or interpreted using nissaya methods. Central claim: nissaya
literature is something more than translation and no less than an encyclopaedic source of knowledge.

== 3 BURMESE NISSAYA LITERATURE: BACKGROUND ==

=== 3.1 Nissaya Origin ===

Theravada Buddhist literature developed from as early as the 3rd century bce in India, translated
into Sinhalese as Sinhalatthakatha in the 1st ce; Buddhaghosa re-translated the Sinhalese
commentaries into Pali under Mahavihara supervision (Malasekera 2014, 11, 38).

The Pali term nissaya denotes a concept found in the Mahavagga Pali of the Vinaya-pitaka texts as a
requisite rule for the newly ordained novice and nun (Oldenberg 1879, 58). In this sense, nissaya is
a "guidance". A newly ordained monk or nun is required to live under the guidance of his or her
preceptor (upajjhaya) for a minimum of five years; if the new monk loses his preceptor, he must seek
nissaya from a teacher; until independent, one must live under both upajjhaya (preceptor) and
acariya (teacher).

Per the PTS Pali-English Dictionary and Soothill & Hodous, Pali nissaya corresponds to Sanskrit
nisraya and in meaning to asraya, "to sit on, or that on which anything depends." The Princeton
Dictionary of Buddhism defines Sanskrit nisraya as "requisite," "reliance," or "support," i.e., basic
possessions required of monk or nun. In Mahavagga Pali the four requisites are listed: alms food
(pindiyalopabhojana), rag robe (pamsukulacivara), foot-of-tree dwelling (rukkhamulasenasana),
fermented urine as medicine (putimuttabhesajja).

The concept of nissaya as "guidance," "reliance," "support" or "mentor" likely came from the
Mahavagga usage and entered Pali literature generally. Nissaya refers to the literary genre of
word-to-word translation of canonical Pali or Atthakatha texts; they are not simply renderings but
provide various interpretive resources in compact encyclopaedic style. Modern example: Bhasatika
titles by Ashin Janakabhivamsa (1900-1977), 20th-c. monastic education reformer.

Janakabhivamsa's eight steps for using nissaya (2000b, 594):
1. First produce word-to-word nissaya interpretation for the beginner.
2. When students can produce good nissaya fluently, translate every word from Pali into Burmese as prose.
3. Those with adequate skill translate the prose without using all Pali words.
4. Focus on specific meanings of the Pali word, summarize and expand the context.
5. Read the Pali slowly and carefully without nissaya.
6. Compose Pali sentences, master verse and well-spoken Pali.
7. Devote faith, wisdom and energy to the Buddha's virtues and qualities.
8. When the Sutta Pitaka has been translated, it will benefit Burma and promulgate the Sasana.

The emergence of nissayas in Burma preceded proper Burmese translations; their existence arguably
precluded development of standard translations (Clark 2015, 114). Per McDaniel (2008, 119): parallel
genres in Sri Lanka = sannaya; Thailand/Laos = nissaya, namasadda, vohara. The Suttanipata-sannaya,
a Pali-Sinhala word-to-word translation written in the 12th ce, is the earliest example of the
genre in Sri Lanka; further sutta-sannayas followed (Pruitt 1992, 278).

According to John Okell (1965, 186-227) nissaya is a linguistic analysis with special emphasis on
Pali grammar features in the Burmese language; since its early development in the late Pagan period
Burmese has been heavily influenced by Pali grammatical syntax.

It is almost impossible to state with certainty when the first Pali-Burmese nissaya was written.
Tin Lwin (1961, 5-11) claims the oldest extant nissaya in Burma is the Khuddakasikkha Nikaya
nissaya, written by Mahakassapa during the reign of Narapatisithu (1174-1211). Tin Lwin's
unpublished master's thesis (University of London microfilm) classifies nissayas as: canonical works;
non-canonical works; secular nissaya works; other types of written nissaya; and the place of nissaya
in Buddhist Education in Burma. Development dated before and after 1448; origin chain proposed:
Pyu nissaya (Sriksetra) -> Mon nissaya (Pagan) -> Sinhalese sannaya -> Burmese nissaya. Pyu king
Vikrama founded Sriksetra in 637 ce; a 7th-c. excavation found a Pyu nissaya interpreting a Sanskrit
passage at Sriksetra near modern Pyay (Tin Lwin 2014, 14-15; 43-49). Ruiz-Falques (2015, 10):
"nissayas are later than the Pali grammatical texts. We actually have no record of grammatical
nissayas from the Pagan period." Popularization plausibly from the 15th century.

Walker (2020, 678): Pali-Burmese examples of nissayas extant from the late thirteenth century, and
several Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts are listed in an inscription from 1442 — the Thaungdwing governor's
inscription, containing a lengthy list of Pali and Sanskrit works together with their nissayas
offered by the pious governor and his wife to the monastery they built and dedicated that year. The
list fixes dates of Pali and nissaya works and clarifies authorship and popularity.

=== 3.2 Nissaya and Pali Literature Development ===

Pruitt (1992, 278): "Sinhalese Sanne and Burmese Nissayas have been important in Pali studies in the
West." Shwe Zan Aung (1871-1932) and Pe Maung Tin (1888-1973) used the nissaya method to translate
Abhidhammattha-sangaha (1910) and Atthasalini (1920) into English for the Pali Text Society (Pruitt
1992, 294ff). "[The] texts are given word-by-word translation into Burmese, a Tibeto-Burman
language, in the texts called nissayas."

Braun (2013, 67, 174): nissaya is a Burmese text that translates and explains Pali works, often on a
systematic word-by-word basis; most Pali-to-Burmese translation took place through nissayas.

King Thalun (1629-1648) first introduced a curricular system of monastic education and formal
examination (Dhammasami 2004, 11); nissaya literature developed widely then, relying more on Vinaya
texts. Dhammasami (2004, 21, 43-77): "the works on the nissaya literature were produced in almost
all the reigns from the time of Thalun... because a nissaya was mainly, and still is, for students
rather than scholars". The nissaya on Shin-kyint-wut (novice disciplinary rules) was compiled more
than 30 times (Dhammasami 2018, 21).

=== 3.3 Nissaya Interpretation Versus Standard Translation ===

Clark (2015, viii): a word-for-word Burmese nissaya bears similarities to an annotated English
translation critically referencing the text's primary Pali commentary. Quote (Clark 2015, 114):
"The official Chattasangiti Pitaka Burmese translation of the Apadana so frequently reproduces the
interpretation of this nissaya with identical, or near identical, wording that it might be supposed
that its translators partially based their publication upon it."

Pruitt (1992, 278): only slight adjustment needed to raise a Pali-Burmese nissaya to the level of
the Chattasangiti Pitaka Burmese Translation; added words called pathasesa ("extra text") appear in
parentheses in the official translation.

== 4 NISSAYA LITERATURE IN SOUTHEAST ASIA ==

Genres in Northern Thailand and Laos: nissaya ("support"), namasadda ("glossary"), vohara ("lifting
words"). Per McDaniel (2008, 6) nissayas used by monastic teachers in pre-modern period 16th-19th c.
in Thailand and Laos; (2008, 131) the genres overlap there: nissaya/namasadda = close word-for-word
glosses; vohara cites 12-25 words with extensive creative vernacular commentary; Burmese-style
nissaya cites four to ten words before glosses.

Gandhasarabhivamsa (interview, 6 Feb 2020), Thai scholar-monk trained in Burma: Thai nissayas are
called gambhi-nisai (from Pali gambhira-nissaya "deep support"), e.g. Kaccayana Gambhi-nisai,
Saddasaratthajalini nisai; Thai nissayas limited to canonical texts and commentaries vs broader
Burmese coverage; less influential in Thailand. Burmese recension history: Pagan/Ava-period nissayas
have fewer explanations (e.g., Ne Yin Sayadaw nissaya); later ones more (e.g., Pakokku nissayas).
Hinuber (1997, 100-101): Thai nissayas may be considered vohara or namasap (from Sanskrit
nama-sabda 'noun-word'); difference between vohara and nissaya unclear. Pathompong (2016, 30-32):
Thai Sangha examinations (Naktham, Pariyatti) make no mention of nissaya interpretation.

=== 4.1 Nissaya Influence in Burmese Monastic Education ===

Nissayas influential among monastics and laity; e.g., Triple-Gem formula nissaya ("Buddham saranam
gacchami..." glossed word by word) known to all Burmese Buddhists; secular plays include nissaya
expressions. Learning is sequential: memorize Mangala-sutta, then its nissaya, then meaning via
Burmese poems (Dhammasami 2004, 42). Khamphorn/Pannabhoga (2020, 26): pure Burmese translation
considered inferior for uneducated people; nissaya considered superior, for scholars and educated
people.

== 5 SALIENT FEATURES OF NISSAYA INTERPRETATIVE METHODS ==

Seven methods commonly employed in Burma per Karunika (2008, 10):

1. pathanusari nissaya method - simple translation of Pali sentences without adding words;
2. pathasesa nissaya method - words added to better explain actual meaning; additions drawn from
   the original Pali text, prior statements, canon, commentary/sub-commentary, or Tipitaka authority
   experts; never personal opinion, strictly authoritative sources;
3. saddattha method - interpretation based on the original, like literal translation;
4. sanketattha method - follows precedent traditionally or conventionally accepted;
5. pannatyattha method - focus on conventional terminology for better understanding;
6. voharattha method - focus on conventional usage;
7. adhippayattha method - direct or easily understood meaning.

nissaya as guidebook, manual, handbook, even teacher. More discussion: Dhammasami 2004, 134-147.
Government Dhammacariya examination guidelines allow one Pali word/phrase/sentence to be interpreted
by different methods and grammatical skills.

== 6 RESEARCH FINDINGS AND LIMITATIONS ==

Author's observation over a decade at two leading institutes in Mandalay and Pegu. Nissaya authors
are learned monks trained in Kaccayana, Moggalana-vyakarana, Padarupasiddhi etc.; most were zaso
("repeaters", tutelage students; literally poet/composer/annotator; verb zate = learning scriptures
under mentor's tutelage; zaw-a lecture = monastery course). Learning by heart required. Ashin
Janakabhivamsa was a zaso in Pakokku courses before producing his many nissayas.

Classroom practice: interaction between teacher and zaso; teacher reads aloud leading, zaso repeats
louder and clearer; other students listen and note interpretations. Process known as ane'-de =
'marking annotation in the margin': making notes around available space of a Pali or Atthakatha
textbook. Other techniques: ane' kau' (interpretation), ane' pjan' (translation), ane' pwa (extended
meaning), ane' hpwin (definition), ane' hpo (elucidation), ane' adei' be (interpreted meaning).
Methodological maxim verse:

    pathamam kare padacchedam, samas-adim tato kare;
    samasado kate paccha, attham niyyatha pandito.

"The wise one, first of all, should break up the compound words and, hereafter, the meaning should
be brought out." Sample gloss: pandito - the wise one [sub]; pathamam - at first; padacchedam -
cutting the word; kare - should make; samasado - combination etc. [obj]; kate - when made; paccha -
afterwards; attham - the meaning; niyyatha - should bring out (Ashin Janakabhivamsa 2000a).

Production volume: per Abhidhamma Tha-main Chok (History of the Abhidhamma, 1965, cha), the 19th
century saw over twenty-two nissayas on the Abhidhammattha-sangaha alone. Bode: Ariyalankara and
Tilokaguru produced Burmese nissayas (interpretations and paraphrases) of Abhidhamma texts in the
17th century, especially Sagaing monasteries (Bode 2013, 49).

== 7 CONCLUSION ==

Nissaya methods pervade curriculum, dhamma-talks, scholarly works; textbooks serve as compulsory
handbooks/encyclopaedic references. Burmese nissaya has become "not only a tool of interpretation,
but a resource of reference, a handbook for students, and a syntactical text, apart from merely a
word-for-word translation or bitext as Walker (2020) puts it."
