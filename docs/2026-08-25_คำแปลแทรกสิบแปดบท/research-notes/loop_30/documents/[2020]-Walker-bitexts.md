## Abstract (verbatim from journal)
"In the late first and early second millennia, mainland Southeast Asians created sophisticated techniques to accurately and efficiently render Pali into local vernaculars, including Burmese, Khmer, Khün, Lanna, Lao, Lü, Mon, and Siamese. These techniques for vernacular reading, parallel to approaches for reading Latin in medieval Europe and Literary Sinitic in Japan, Korea, and Vietnam, led to the development of bitexts that contained a mix of Pali and vernacular material. Such bitexts, arranged in both interlinear and interphrasal formats, gradually allowed second-millennium Southeast Asian writers to sprout a vernacular literature from the established branches of Pali genres. Bitexts themselves formed the basis for a new literary style that stemmed from the techniques of vernacular reading, a style that set the standard for belles-lettres until the early twentieth century. The spread of Pali-vernacular bitexts in Southeast Asia allowed for the literary elevation of the vernacular without renouncing the cosmopolitan idiom of Pali."

To support these arguments the article draws on: a hitherto undeciphered form of manuscript annotation in seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Siam (interlinear philological mode); two of the oldest palm-leaf documents surviving in any Tai language, from sixteenth-century Lanna (exegetical/homiletic); and the oldest known Pali-Siamese literary work, thought composed 1482 (poetic).

## Key definitions (verbatim from full-text excerpts)
- "At the crux of this meeting between Indic and local vernaculars are Indic-vernacular bitexts. I define bitexts as texts that are presented bilingually, with portions in one language mixed together with portions in another, typically in an interphrasal or interlinear arrangement."
- "In most cases, the vernacular portions of a bitext provide an analytic reading of the Pali or Sanskrit portions, sometimes accompanied by an interpretive or literary commentary."
- Three-step compositional model common across Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia: (1) selection (an existing Pali/Sanskrit treatise quoted in full, abbreviated, or omitted; or a text invented for the purpose); (2) analysis (up to five techniques of analytical reading and translation; in Siamese and Lanna contexts all five may apply per passage); (3) presentation in four primary modes: philological (3a), exegetical (3b), homiletic (3c), poetic (3d).
- Positioning within "vernacular reading" studies: "Following the work of John Whitman and Peter Kornicki on East Asia, we might classify Indic-vernacular bitexts in Southeast Asia under the heading of 'vernacular reading.'"
- Contrast with European/East Asian parallels: Southeast Asian bitext techniques "supported a wide variety of textual genres, including linguistic study, scholastic commentary, public sermons, and versified belles-lettres."

## Chronology claimed
- Interphrasal Sanskrit-Pyu inscription tentatively dated 6th century; Sanskrit-Khmer inscriptions with parallel content largely early second millennium.
- A handful of reliably dated first-millennium Pali-Sinhala bitexts include the three steps; earliest surviving mainland Southeast Asian example: Pali-Mon inscription late 11th or early 12th century; Pali-Burmese extant from late 13th century; Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts listed in an inscription of 1442; earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext thought to date from 1482 in Ayutthaya.
- Prior scholarship engaged: William Pruitt (Pali-Burmese particles, building on Tin Lwin and John Okell); Assanee Poolrak (Pali-Siamese technical particles); Justin McDaniel (claims bitext conventions are idiosyncratic per teacher — Walker argues instead for strict, regionally shared conventions from the 11th century onward).
