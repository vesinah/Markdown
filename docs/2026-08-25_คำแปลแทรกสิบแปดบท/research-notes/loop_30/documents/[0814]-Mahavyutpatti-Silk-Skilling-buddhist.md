## Tibetan standardized terminology infrastructure
- Mahāvyutpatti ("Great Volume of Precise Understanding"), compiled under imperial patronage (traditionally reign of Ralpacan; scholarship places conception under Sadnalegs, c. 800-815; commonly dated to the horse year 814 CE): bilingual Sanskrit-Tibetan glossary to standardize translation of Buddhist scriptures; companion treatise sGra sbyor bam po gnyis pa explains translation principles.
- Digital editions/uses verified: DILA Glossaries (Mahāvyutpatti with Sanskrit-Tibetan-Chinese, downloadable TEI XML/PDF/StarDict): https://glossaries.dila.edu.tw/glossaries/MVP?locale=en ; Bibliotheca Polyglotta hosts Mahāvyutpatti with sGra sbyor bam po gnyis pa: https://www2.hf.uio.no/polyglotta/index.php?page=volume&vid=263
- This is the closest Buddhist parallel to a "reference infrastructure" of glossing: an official, canonized glossary regulating interlinear/exegetical practice.

## Nissaya literature as bitextual glossing (Theravada)
- "Burmese Nissaya Literature: A Mainstream Monastic [pedagogical genre]", Brill journal article published online 15 June 2023: https://brill.com/view/journals/mnya/26/1/article-p1_004.xml
- Substance verified: genre spectrum within Burmese monastic manuscripts — nāmasadda and nissaya characterized by close word-for-word glosses; vohāra cite longer passages (12-25 words) with extensive vernacular translation/commentary; nissaya typically cite 4-10 Pali words before glossing. Nissaya function as compulsory pedagogical handbooks in Burmese monastic education; Thai nissaya tradition narrower (canonical texts and commentaries). The article explicitly frames nissaya against Walker's 2020 bitext concept ("a word-for-word translation or bitext as Walker (2020) puts it").
- Historical frame given: Pali commentaries (atthakatha) reworked by Buddhaghosa at the Mahavihara; nissaya lineage traced from Sinhala commentary practice into Burmese classroom use.
