## Ad candidum lectorem (Prolegomena opening) — the method statement

Scaliger writes in the work's fifteenth year since first publication ("VINTVSDECIMVS hic annus agitur, candide Lector, postquam opus nostrum de Emendatione Temporum emisimus"), complaining of envious critics who preferred "to be ignorant of anything rather than learn something from us", and then lays out the plan:

- "Subjectum operis nostri est ratio Temporum civilium... finis, Emendatio." (The subject is the reckoning of civil years; the goal is their emendation/correction.)
- Emendation has two parts: "Prior versatur circa epocharum investigationem; posterior circa verum annum tropicum et periodos Lunares." (The former concerns the investigation of epochs; the latter the true tropical year and lunar periods.)
- Books I-III plus most of IV treat civil calendars ("quos Computos posterior aetas vocauit"); book VII their method; books V-VI with rest of IV the astronomical emendation.
- He then attacks rival reconstructions of the world epoch (Septuagint-based vs Olympiad-based systems) as "puerilis sententia multis absurditatibus eluditur" — a boyish opinion refuted by many absurdities — demonstrating critical testing of received chronological authorities (Cyrus' accession year, Sedekias, Sabbatical years from Jeremiah/Ezekiel are cross-checked in the following columns).

Sample of the argumentative texture kept verbatim (normalized): "Sed haec puerilis sententia multis absurditatibus eluditur. Primo computatione non recta annorum septuaginta a capto Sedekia..."

## Why this matters for the chapter

1. The word emendatio is here applied not to a text but to TIME itself: chronology as emendation of corrupt traditions — the same philological instinct Valla used on the Donation's anachronisms, scaled to universal history.
2. The preface documents scholarly quarrel culture (odium criticum) as the engine of refinement.
3. Sandys (ch. XIII): the 1583 first edition "placed him at the head of all the living representatives of ancient learning"; at Leyden he reconstructed Greek Eusebius (Syncellus MS from Paris 1602; Olympic list via Casaubon 1605) published in Thesaurus temporum 1606 — conjecture confirmed by the Armenian version discovered 1818.
4. Immediate reception: Petavius' Doctrina temporum (1627) devoted large space to criticizing it (Sandys ch. XVIII). Modern study: Nothaft 2016 on Josephus and NT chronology in Scaliger, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12138-016-0403-9 ; Grafton's monograph, DOI: https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780199206018.001.0001 .
