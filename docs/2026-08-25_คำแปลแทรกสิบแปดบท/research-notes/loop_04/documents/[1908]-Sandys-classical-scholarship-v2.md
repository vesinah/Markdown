## Extract 1 — Periodization of scholarship (ch. I, pp. 1-2)

"THE History of Scholarship during the six centuries that have elapsed since the birth of Petrarch falls into four principal periods... (i) the Italian, (2) the French, (3) the English and Dutch; and (4) the German. The first is the age of the Revival of Learning in Italy, including the two centuries between the death of Dante in 1321 and the death of Leo X in 1521, and ending with the Sack of Rome in 1527. It begins with Petrarch (1304-1374)... It is the age of the Humanists, and its principal aim is the imitation and reproduction of classical models of style and of life."

Note for book use: Sandys' four-period scheme (Italian/French/English-Dutch/German) is a classic older periodization; modern historiography (Pfeiffer, Grafton) complicates it.

## Extract 2 — Petrarch's annotated books (ch. I)

"In his 'Familiar Letters' he quotes Virgil about 120 times; his carefully annotated copy is preserved in the Ambrosian Library..." (footnote cites De Nolhac 118-135).

"...His favourite Roman historian is Livy; he bitterly regrets the loss of the books of the second decade, and, writing to the historian himself, exclaims: O si mihi totus contingeres... He has only an imperfect copy of Quintilian... His copy [of Pliny the elder] is now in the Paris Library, and, in the margin of the passage describing the fountain of the Sorgue, Petrarch has drawn from memory a dainty little sketch of the valley of Vaucluse."

## Extract 3 — Boccaccio at Monte Cassino (ch. I, p. 14)

"Being eager to see the library, which he had heard was very noble, he humbly besought one of the monks to do him the favour of opening it. Pointing to a lofty staircase, the monk answered stiffly: 'Go up; it is already open'. Boccaccio stepped up the staircase with delight, only to find the treasure-house of learning destitute of door or any kind of fastening, while the grass was growing on the window-sills and the dust reposing on the books and bookshelves. Turning over the manuscripts, he found many rare and ancient works, with whole sheets torn out, or with the margins ruthlessly clipped..." (Benvenuto da Imola on Paradiso xxii; Sandys notes the story "meets with protest" from the historian of Monte Cassino.)

Also: "Boccaccio was the first humanist to quote Varro, and he may have obtained from Monte Cassino the extant archetype of all our MSS of that writer." Laurentian Livy MS with his fly-leaf introductory notes.

## Extract 4 — Salutati the collector (ch. II, pp. 17-18)

"Salutati (1330-1406)... held the high office of chancellor, or Latin secretary, of Florence from 1375 to his death. Like Petrarch, he was a great collector of Latin MSS. He eagerly sought for the lost books of Livy, for Pompeius Trogus, and for a complete copy of Curtius and of Quintilian. He obtained a transcript (1375) of the Verona MS of Catullus... On learning in 1389 that the two MSS of Cicero's Letters, from Verona and Vercelli, were at Milan, he caused a copy to be made from the Vercelli MS... In 1392 he received from Milan a copy of the Verona MS of the Letters Ad Atticum..."

## Extract 5 — Poggio's expeditions and St Gall (ch. III, pp. 26-27)

"The quest for classical manuscripts, begun by Petrarch and continued by Boccaccio and Salutati, was extended beyond the borders of Italy during the Council of Constance (1414-1418)... During the vacancy in the 'Apostolic See', from 24 May 1415 to 11 November 1417, the papal secretary had no official duties to perform, and it was during this interval that his principal discoveries were made. These discoveries are connected with four distinct expeditions: (i) to Cluni in the summer of 1415, (2) to St Gallen in the summer of 1416, (3) to St Gallen and other monasteries early in 1417, and (4) to Langres and other places in France and in Germany in the summer of the same year." (Footnote: discriminated by Sabbadini, Le Scoperte dei Codici Latini e Greci ne' secoli XIV e XV, Firenze 1905.)

St Gall conditions: "In that ancient home of learning they found the abbot and the monks absolutely uninterested in literature, and many a precious MS lying amid the dust and damp and darkness of one of the towers of the abbey-church, a noisome prison (says Poggio)..."

Quintilian: "Among Poggio's first discoveries was a complete copy of the Institutio Oratoria of Quintilian, a work which Petrarch had never known except in an imperfect and mutilated form, and which Salutati had vainly hoped to obtain from France, while Gasparino da Barzizza had audaciously undertaken to supply the missing portions by means of compositions of his own... carried off the MS to Constance, and copied it himself in 53 days."

Poggio's letter (Epp. i 5, to Guarino, 15 Dec 1416), quoted: "Quintilianum comperimus adhuc salvum et incolumem, plenum tamen situ et pulvere squalentem."

## Extract 6 — The January 1417 haul: Lucretius, Ammianus, etc. (ch. III, pp. 29-30)

"The rest of the new finds were Lucretius, Manilius, Silius Italicus, Ammianus Marcellinus, and the grammarians Caper, Eutyches, and Probus. The Lucretius was discovered in a 'distant' monastery where a copy was made on Poggio's behalf... Fulda was the unnamed source of the MS of books xiv to xxxi of Ammianus Marcellinus... Poggio afterwards essayed in vain to obtain another MS of the same historian from Hersfeld."

Poggio's retrospective claim, quoted from Epp.: "Ammianum Marcellinum ego latinis musis restitui cum illum eruissem e bibliothecis ne dicam ergastulis Germanorum."

Also noted same expedition context: "a copy of Vitruvius" (with further copies at Reichenau and Avignon); and summer 1417 Cicero speech finds at/around Langres (pro Caecina, three de lege agraria, two pro Rabirio, pro Roscio Comoedo, in Pisonem).

## Extract 7 — Lodi chest find 1421; failed complete-Livy hunts (ch. III, pp. 31-33)

"In the cathedral church of Lodi, the bishop, Gerardo Landriani, was engaged in searching for some ancient charters in a chest that had long remained unopened, when he lighted on a MS of Cicero... including a complete copy of the De Oratore, the Brutus, and the Orator. The Brutus was absolutely new..."

Rumours of a complete Livy (Cismar near Luebeck; Chartres c. 1413; Soroe in Denmark, "three vast volumes") all dissolved: "But no such MS was found either at this, or at another monastery in Denmark, and a still later rumour was dismissed by Poggio as a mere romance."

## Extract 8 — Politian as investigator of MSS; later Italian finds (ch. III, pp. 35-36)

"Politian was a keen investigator of all the ancient MSS that came within his reach in Florence or elsewhere." Bobbio finds 1493 under Merula; Fra Giocondo found Pliny-Trajan correspondence in Paris about 1500; Corvey Tacitus Annals i-vi to Medicean Library 1508; Beatus Rhenanus found Velleius Paterculus at Murbach 1515; Grynaeus brought Livy V 41-45 from Lorsch 1527.

## Extract 9 — Valla: Donation and Elegantiae (ch. IV/V, pp. 67-69)

"Valla's denunciation of the 'Donation of Constantine' in 1440 served the interests of Alfonso by discrediting the papal claim to temporal power... He attacks this decree on legal, linguistic, political, and historical grounds, showing inter alia that its style and contents are inconsistent with the date to which it purports to belong, and that the ancient MSS of the legend of St Sylvester, on which the decree professes to rely, say nothing of the alleged 'Donation'. Thus it was that, 'in the revival of letters and liberty, this fictitious deed was transpierced by the pen of Laurentius Valla, the pen of an eloquent critic and a Roman patriot' (Gibbon). ... it was first printed by Ulrich von Hutten in 1517."

"On the Elegancies of the Latin language... He here attacks the barbarous Latin of the Middle Ages and of his own times... He tries its modern use by the standard of Cicero and Quintilian... It was printed at Venice in 1471, passed through 59 editions between that year and 1536."

"As a textual critic Valla is represented partly by certain passages of his Elegantiae, and still more by the emendations that arose out of the readings in Livy at the court of Alfonso... Many of his emendations on the first six books of Livy's Second Punic War now form part of the current text. He also criticises the Vulgate version of the New Testament in relation to the original Greek (1444), and his criticisms were first published by Erasmus in 1505."

## Extract 10 — Poliziano's Miscellanea (ch. VII, pp. 85)

"The most learned of his extant productions is his Miscellanea (1489). Among the many topics discussed in its pages are the use of the aspirate in Latin and Greek, the chronology of Cicero's 'Familiar Letters', the evidence in favour of the spelling Vergilius in preference to Virgilius, the details of the discovery of purple dye, and the differences between the aorist and the imperfect in the signatures of Greek sculptors."

Anti-Ciceronian polemic: "'To myself (he adds) the face of a bull or a lion appears far more beautiful than that of an ape... But someone will say: You do not express Cicero. I answer: I am not Cicero; what I really express is myself.'"

## Extract 11 — Erasmus: Adagia and the 1516 Greek Testament (ch. X, pp. 128-131)

"In 1500 he produced his Adagia... Venice, where (as a guest of Aldus) he prepared a second edition of his Adagia..."

"His varied learning is best seen in his Adagia, where his erudite illustrations of the meaning of ancient proverbial phrases are often curiously diversified by pungent criticisms on modern priests and princes..."

"Early in 1514 he left Cambridge with a view to the publication of these works at Basel in 1516. His edition of the Greek Testament, the first that was actually published, was accompanied with a Latin version and with notes suggested by those of Valla, which Erasmus had discovered in 1505."

Preface quotation given by Sandys: "aliorum litterae sunt eiusmodi ut non parum multos paenituerit insumptae in illis operae... at felix ille quem in hisce litteris meditantem mors occupat."

## Extract 12 — J. J. Scaliger and Casaubon (ch. XIII, pp. 200-207)

Scaliger's emendation method: "He regarded the Italian type of Scholarship, with its fancy for the imitation of the ancients, as a frivolous pursuit, and he had no sympathy with Italian scholars in their hap-hazard alterations of classical texts. He was the first to point the way to a sounder method of emendation founded on the genuine tradition of the MSS..."

Chronology: "his Manilius thus serves as an introduction to the comprehensive system of chronology set forth in his folio volume De Emendatione Temporum (1583). The publication of this work placed him at the head of all the living representatives of ancient learning."

Reconstruction of Greek Eusebius (boxed note): Syncellus MS obtained from Paris 1602; Olympic-victor list via Casaubon 1605; published in Thesaurus Temporum 1606; conjecture confirmed by Armenian version found 1818.

On Miscellanea fashion (RQ8): "He had no sympathy with the fashion of publishing Miscellanea or Adversaria, which had been set by Politian and Victorius, by Turnebus and Muretus; he preferred to deal with the exposition and criticism of each author as an undivided whole." And: "he is also the founder of historical criticism."

Casaubon: born Geneva 1559 of Huguenot parents; professor at Geneva 1582; married daughter of Henri Estienne 1586 ("he guards his books as the griffins in India do their gold"); Scaliger on Casaubon: "the greatest man we now have in Greek"; left France for England 1610 (see also ch. XVIII opening).

## Extract 13 — Reception: Petavius against Scaliger (ch. XVIII, p. 283ff)

"Denys Petau, or Petavius... devoted a large part of his chronological work, the Doctrina Temporum (1627), to the criticism of Scaliger's De Emendatione Temporum."
