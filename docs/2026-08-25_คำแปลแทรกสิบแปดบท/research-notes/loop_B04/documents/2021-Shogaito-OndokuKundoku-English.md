## Key content notes (quotable)

### Corpus

- Fifteen fragments of Chinese texts written in Uighur script discovered at the St Petersburg Branch of the Institute of Oriental Studies RAS (published Shoogaito 1995a, 1997), identified as:
  - Arya Manjusrinamasamgiti (Sheng miao jixiang zhen shi ming jing): SI Kr. IV 266, 271, 291, 817, 819, 821; SI 4b Kr. 89
  - Caturvargiya-vinaya bhiksunipratimoksasutra (Si fen lü biqiu jieben): SI Kr. IV 285, 309
  - Worship-and-repentance / praise texts (Lichan wen / Lizan wen): SI O105, SI 4bKr. 175, 176, SI Kr. I125, SI Kr. IV 276, SI 2Kr. 45
- Phonology of the Chinese in Uighur script = northwestern dialects of Tang-Five Dynasties; fragments Yuan-period but phonology reflects inherited Uighur pronunciation of Chinese (IUPC), not colloquial Yuan Mandarin.

### Interlinear insertion (core claim)

- "it is occasionally observed that Chinese characters are sporadically inserted into Uighur lines in the above texts. These inserted Chinese characters must have been read in Uighur. These Chinese characters appear not only as words, but also as phrases and sentences."
- In bilingual texts such as the Thousand Character Essay "the Uighur inherited reading of Chinese is followed by its corresponding Uighur translation."
- Worked example (from Shoogaito 2000, p. 169): yuŋ(雲) tïŋ(騰) ču(致) yu(雨) ... bulït sekridi yaγmur yaγdï : lu(露) ker(結) vi(爲) šo(霜)... "The clouds rose. The rain poured down." - i.e., every Chinese character read aloud in Uighurized Chinese, then the Uighur rendering; explicitly compared to the Japanese Monzen (文選) way of reading Senjimon.
- Tattvartha practice: the Uighur edition of the Abhidharmakosabhasya-tika-Tattvartha omits the quoted lemmata and assumes the reader reads the CHINESE original directly; Uighur word order is modeled on Chinese; "We can suppose that the Uighur text records the Uighur reading of the text."
- Comparative typology sentence: "We can also suppose that, because Uighurs did not develop kunten, the guiding marks for rendering Chinese into Japanese, the use of Chinese-like word order was a convenient way of reading in Uighur at a constant rate."
- Conclusion: Yuan-period Uighur monks recited Chinese sutras ondoku-style while understanding them kundoku-style, exactly like Japanese monks.
