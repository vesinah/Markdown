## Key facts harvested (verbatim-anchored)

Extent of volume: "Pp. clxxxii, 472" — i.e., a 181-page introduction precedes the edition. The review calls it her "181-page introduction" titled "the oldest gloss tradition on Martianus Capella's De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii".

Reception context: "Shortly after the turn of the ninth century, Martianus Capella's complex fifth-century allegory ... entered the reading programs of Carolingian schools, never to leave." About "Some fifty ninth-century manuscripts survive (out of around 250 all told) along with at least three commentaries attributed to a mysterious Duncaht of Reims, John Scottus (Eriugena), and Remigius of Auxerre."

Motivation (quoting Teeuwen, CELAMA 12 introduction, p. 12): Carolingian fascination with Martianus's "strange language with undreamed-of grammatical constructions, unusual words and neologisms, Graecisms and Greek words, its variety in the use of prose and metre". Plus elite belief that obscurity masked hidden truths unlockable by skill and insight.

Dating and geography of the oldest gloss layer: "The oldest glosses date to the first quarter of the ninth century and survive in twenty ninth- and tenth-century manuscripts, which point in the directions of the Loire valley, north and northeastern France, and Cologne and Lorsch as early centers of intense study of Martianus Capella."

No archetype: "What they do not point to is a single author, place of origin, or common archetype (xxviii)." Yet the oldest glosses form "a relatively stable and coherent body of glossing" (xxvii); families emerged through copying, additions, omissions; local sources plumbed, e.g., Festus's De verborum significatu. Glosses are a living text showing "enthusiastic" (cxliv) Carolingian intellectual and pedagogical creativity.

Editorial method: O'Sullivan "decided wisely against trying to produce a definitive edition of an Ur-text, a text that never existed in fact." Instead she presents "all the glosses in all their variety from the fourteen principal manuscripts she selected for the edition." Editorial principles occupy eleven pages (cxxxi-cxlii). Page layout: De nuptiis lemma in larger font, then glosses in smaller type, e.g., Book I,1 "conubium diuum componens Calliopea": conubium (copulam); diuum (deorum); componens receives three glosses (construens; texens; i. exornans cantu suo); Calliopea = bona uox with variants including Greek characters; mother-of-Orpheus additions.

Apparatuses: "A source apparatus indicates that the gloss on Calliopea owed much to Fulgentius and to the Vatican Mythographers. A second apparatus alerts readers to textual variants among the manuscripts."

Content depth: beyond lexical glosses, allegorical and Neoplatonizing interpretation; fables reveal poetic truth; quotation from O'Sullivan's chapter: "Paradoxically falsehood is the ornament of truth."

Closing praise (also printed on the Brepols page): "By uncovering these glosses from between the lines and in the margins of manuscripts and presenting them in a meticulous and eminently useful edition, O'Sullivan has opened exciting, new perspectives on Carolingian scholarship and intellectual curiosity."
