# Harvest: Formigatti 2015 dissertation - Sanskrit annotated manuscripts from Northern India and Nepal

Full PDF downloaded and text-extracted this loop (272 pages). This is THE dedicated study of how
marginal and interlinear annotation is laid out on South Asian manuscripts, including a Nepalese
palm-leaf pothi witness (Daṇḍin's Kāvyādarśa, Dimitrov's siglum B) and seven Nepalese Raghuvaṃśa
manuscripts examined directly in the National Archives Kathmandu.

## 2. Terminology for annotation layout (ch. 2.1.1, PDF pp. 17, 25-28)

- Gloss vs scholia distinguished; "annotation" used as the generic term covering both.
- Running-commentary material can be integrated into one manuscript regardless of position:
  "(1) above and below the basic text, as in the so called tripāṭha, 'threefold reading' commentaries;
  (2) on all four margins, as in the pañcapāṭha, 'fivefold reading' commentaries."
- Format terminology: pustaka/pustikā -> pothī; discussion of Gāndhārī postaga/postaka (Salomon),
  Khotan Dharmapada verse; Balbir's British Library Jain catalogue definition of pothī vs guṭakā;
  Al-Bīrūnī's passage showing pothī originally denoted the wrapped book, tied to writing material
  (palm-leaf south vs birch-bark north) rather than format.
- Only eight Sanskrit codicological terms out of 166 lemmata in a recent handbook vocabulary are
  Sanskrit (apapāṭha, pāṭha, pāṭhāntara, pothī, prakṣepa, prakṣipta, praśasti, śodhapatra) - evidence
  of how understudied South Asian book-codicology vocabulary was.

## 3. Corpus of manuscripts described

- Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī (ĪPV, Kashmir): manuscripts J, P, L, Ś7.
- Raghuvaṃśa (RaghuV): Nepalese manuscripts N1, N2, N3, N5, N6, N7, N8 (examined directly at the
  National Archives, Kathmandu); Be (Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, microfilm only); O (Jaina pothī,
  Bodleian Library Oxford).
- Daṇḍin's Kāvyādarśa (KĀ): an annotated palm-leaf NEPALESE manuscript, siglum B in
  Dimitrov 2002a/2002b ("Tables of the old bengali script (on the basis of a nepalese manuscript of
  daṇḍin's Kāvyādarśa)", in Dimitrov 2002c, pp. 27-78; Mārgavibhāga vol., Marburg: Indica et Tibetica
  Verlag). Not all examined items were fully described; KĀ-B and an ĪPK paper codex appear in the
  concluding appraisal.

## 4. Nepalese witnesses in detail

### 4.1 Raghuvaṃśa manuscript N1 (PDF pp. 43-44)
- NGMPP data: Place of deposit National Archives Kathmandu; Inventory no. 43783; Reel No. A 391/15;
  Accession No. 1/1272; filming-data line also prints "Reel No.: A 39/15"; Date of Filming 14-07-1972;
  161 exposures; positive film; used copy Kathmandu. (Both reel strings reproduced as printed.)
- Material/format: paper pothī with simple wooden slabs as cover; HYPOTACTIC COMPOSITE of four
  codicological units; all versos yellow except 6v.
- Units: (1) fols 1-2 Devanāgarī smaller format 30.5 x 9.2 cm; (2) fols 3-80 in NEWARI script,
  ca. 33 x 8.5 cm, the kernel (RaghuV 1.18d-9.67d), last three fols misplaced at front as prakīrṇa;
  (3) fols 77r(=76v continuation)-119 Devanāgarī cut to match; (4) fols 120-153 another hand.
- Layout: 153 folios, 5 lines per folio, average 44 akṣaras per line in ALL four units; no running
  marginal title; foliation figures middle-right margin of versos (repeated both margins in unit 4).
- Annotations: an anonymous commentary in the KATHAṂBHŪTINĪ style written ENTIRELY IN THE MARGINS
  AND BETWEEN THE LINES; water damage to the right margin of the Newari unit cancelled/damaged many
  annotations (some opposite-page notes visible in transparency through the leaf).
- Undated; recension of Jinasamudra (numbering per Mallinātha's for convenience).

### 4.2 Raghuvaṃśa N5, N6, N7 - one reel, several objects (PDF p. 38)
"N5, N6 and N7 have been photographed by the Nepal-German Manuscript Preservation Project (NGMPP)
as one single manuscript. Accordingly, they were catalogued by the Nepalese-German Manuscript
Cataloguing Project (NGMCP) under one single reel number (A 397/18). In the National Archives in
Kathmandu, they are kept as a single manuscript wrapped up in a cloth." Codicologically they are
distinct units - a caution for anyone citing NGMPP reels as if they equal physical manuscripts.
(PDF p. 51 adds: three fragmentary folios grouped together, sizes 20.x cm x 9.8 cm / 23.4 x 10 /
23.7 x 9.8; third folio thicker "seemingly Nepalese" paper; NGMCP entry describes the material as
Indian paper.)

### 4.3 Content groups among the Nepalese RaghuV manuscripts (PDF p. 84)
- Group 1: N1 - anonymous kathaṃbhūtinī-style commentary (see above).
- Group 2: N2, N3, N5, N6 - short glosses consisting mainly of quotations (from lexica, usually the
  Amarakośa, or from Mallinātha's commentary).
- Group 3: (Kashmirian Be and others) - quotations from well-known commentaries.

### 4.4 Kāvyādarśa manuscript B (Nepalese palm-leaf) - the pothi layout case study (PDF pp. 78, 88-90)
- Its annotations are "actually a whole anonymous commentary written in the margins of the
  manuscript", in the style of a running commentary INCLUDING THE USAGE OF PRATĪKAS.
- Linkage strategy between main text and annotation: "in the main text a reference sign is provided
  above the word to be commented on (or a representative one for a longer passage), and a digit at
  the end of the annotation marks the line in which the word occurs."
- Word division (padaccheda) is fulfilled VISUALLY by sandhi- and word-divider signs (small circle
  stroke used regularly as word divider); same system in N1, N8 and the Jaina ms O (O uses different
  signs for different types of vocal sandhi; N8 sometimes writes digits above words to give the
  ANVAYA of the stanza).
- Layout economics: the scribe "chose to keep to a minimum the unit of ruling and not to exploit all
  the surface of the page for the textual mass", i.e. he could have written at least two more lines
  but deliberately left space for marginal notes. Terms "textual mass" / "exegetical mass" borrowed
  from Maniaci (2006, 214 ff.).

## 5. Typological classification of annotations (§3.2.1.1, PDF p. 78)

Based on the five lakṣaṇas of running commentaries from traditional stanzas; subdivisions after
Tubb & Boose 2007:

- I. padaccheda
- II. anvayokti
- III. samāsādivivecana: III.a laukikavigraha (ordinary-language paraphrase); III.b śāstrīya-
  alaukikavigraha (technical grammatical analysis)
- IV. padārthabodha: IV.a authorial gloss; IV.b quotation
- V. tātparya: V.a authorial explanation; V.b quotation

Cross-check: Muroya's classification of the Nyāyamañjarī-manuscript annotations (referent of pronoun;
grammatical dissolution/vigraha; doctrinal positions assigned to pūrvapakṣa/uttarakṣa; word meaning;
philosophical exposition) maps onto four of the five lakṣaṇas (padaccheda lacking because those
scholia quote the Nyāyamañjarīgranthibhaṅga).

## 6. General findings of the appraisal (§3.3, PDF pp. 86-90)

- GEOGRAPHY: "annotations in Kashmirian manuscripts are all similar, both from the graphical viewpoint
  as well as from that of the content", regardless of literary genre; genre is the second decisive factor.
- MATERIAL DETERMINES LAYOUT: palm-leaf dimensions are limited by nature (Hoernle 1900: largest usable
  Borassus strip ca. 16 x 1.5 inches); therefore pothi layout must balance readability of main text
  against space for exegesis.
- FORMAT CONSERVATISM: although paper can be cut to any size, most Indian paper manuscripts remain
  oblong pothī-shaped, so the paper Nepalese N1 shows a layout akin to the palm-leaf KĀ ms; annotations
  had to be adapted to the limited space. The Kashmirian codices (paper/birch bark, e.g. ĪPV mss J, P,
  Be) are the exception allowing numerous INTERLINEAR notes.
- FUNCTION: ĪPV annotations quote long passages of independent commentaries (ĪPVV, Raghupañcikā),
  visually disorganized, plausibly TEACHERS' mnemonic supports for lessons (or self-study notes);
  KĀ/N1-type marginal commentaries serve STUDENTS reading classical school texts.
- Transmission: the ĪPV annotations behave "as an independent exegetical apparatus" and were copied
  together with the root text from manuscript to manuscript (German abstract, PDF p. 3).

## Interpretation for the Thai book project

This dissertation supplies exactly what Loop 21 lacked: (a) a first-hand typology of interlinear and
marginal annotation in Sanskrit manuscripts; (b) proof that on Nepalese pothi-format witnesses the
dominant strategy is MARGINAL annotation with sign-and-digit linkage, while true interlinear glossing
is constrained by the pothi ruling and flourishes in Kashmirian codex formats; (c) named Nepalese
witnesses with shelf marks and NGMPP reel numbers (N1 = NAK 1/1272, reel A 391/15; N5-N7 = reel
A 397/18; KĀ ms B per Dimitrov); (d) the indigenous terms tripāṭha/pañcapāṭha for multi-band layouts.
