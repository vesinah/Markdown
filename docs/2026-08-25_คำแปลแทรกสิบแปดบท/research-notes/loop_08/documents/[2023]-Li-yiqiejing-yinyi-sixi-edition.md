## Verbatim key passages (extracted from the open-access PDF)

### Introduction
"The Yiqiejingyinyi 一切経音義 which was compiled in the Tang dynasty is a Buddhist dictionary. The author was the Tang dynasty monk Xuanying 玄応. The Yiqiejingyinyi is the oldest extant Buddhist dictionary. At the time, author Xuanying extracted difficult characters and words from approximately 500 copies of Buddhist scriptures and added his interpretation of their pronunciation, variants and meanings. The Yiqiejingyinyi was introduced to Japan along with Tripitaka. It was then frequently copied in temples throughout Japan. In contrast to old manuscripts, many Brock print editions have survived. In this context, in 2019, the Song dynasty Sixi 思溪 Edition of the Buddhist Canon, jointly edited by the International College for Postgraduate Buddhist Studies and the Department of Antiquities of the National Library of China, made available to the public."

### Manuscripts and editions compared
Manuscripts: "Kongōji 金剛寺 manuscripts (KGJ), Hōryū-ji 法隆寺 manuscripts (HRJ), Nanatsudera 七寺 manuscripts (NNTD), Saihōji 西方寺 manuscripts (SHJ), Hiroshima University manuscripts (HRS), Kōshōji 興聖寺 manuscripts (KSJ)".
Printed editions: "the First and Second Korean 高麗 printed canons (KC1&2), Fuzhou 福州 printed canon and Touzenji 東禅寺 printed canon by Kunaichō Shoryoubu 宮内庁書陵部 of Japan (KNIC), and Qisha 磧砂 printed canon (QS)", plus the newly published Sixi 思渓 Edition (SX).

### Transcription format typology
"Ishizuka and Ikeda (1991) studied Dunhuang manuscripts and Japanese ancient manuscripts and divided them into several types of the Yiqiejingyinyi calligraphic forms."
"The two main types can be grouped together. In the first type, the word and the annotation are written in the same size. In contrast, in the second type, the words are written in large letters and the annotations are written in small letters."

The paper then tabulates format types (A, B, C, D, E and combinations such as C+D, A+B) volume by volume (juan 1-25) across KSJ, NNTD, KGJ, HRJ, SHJ, HRS, KC1&2, SX, KNIC. Findings stated verbatim:
"1) Even within a single type of ancient manuscript, different volumes have different copying styles.
2) Each ancient manuscript has its own unique copying style.
3) The difference between printed editions and manuscripts is obvious.
4) The SX and KNIC are in perfect agreement.
According to the above, there are two main types of the Yiqiejingyinyi lineages: manuscript lineages and printed lineages."

## Notes for the book chapter
- Direct answer to the layout question (RQ1/RQ3): within the Yinyi tradition itself, copyists alternated between equal-size glossing and the classic "large lemma + small annotation" convention (the East Asian counterpart of interlinear/small-note layout); the choice varies volume by volume even inside one codex.
- Establishes a two-lineage stemma: manuscript lineage (Japanese temple copies) vs printed lineage (Song/Korean canons), with the 2019-published Sixi canon agreeing perfectly with the Kunaichō Tōzenji copy.
- Confirms Japanese reception (RQ5): "introduced to Japan along with Tripitaka ... frequently copied in temples throughout Japan".
- Names the reference study for Dunhuang + Japanese calligraphic formats of the Yinyi: Ishizuka and Ikeda (1991) (full bibliographic data not yet verified — see gaps).
