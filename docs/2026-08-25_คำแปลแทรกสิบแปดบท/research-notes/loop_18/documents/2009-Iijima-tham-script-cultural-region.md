## Key content (verified excerpts)

1. Script family and spread:
   "The script referred to as the Tham (Pali: dhamma) script ... is one of the Southeast Asian scripts of Indian origin ... It presumably evolved from a preceding script of the Mon, the earlier inhabitants of present-day northern Thailand, and was adapted by the Tai speaking peoples ... for their vernaculars since 1465 at the latest, in addition to using it for Pali religious matters (Penth 1992: 52-53, 60). It subsequently spread to present-day Laos, northeast Thailand, the Shan State of Burma and to the Sipsong Panna area in southwest China, and evolved into local variants."
   Derivative variants (Lue, Khun, tham Lao etc.) "are characterized by an identical system of writing"; because all are primarily used for religious texts they can collectively be called the Tham script (citing Bizot 1993: 13).

2. Not an ethnic script: Tham script "has in no way been an exclusive feature of any particular ethnic group." Non-Tai groups such as Mon-Khmer-speaking Palaung reportedly used it extensively (Sai Kham Mong 2004: 256-257).

3. Against equating script region with Lan Na polity: naming it the "Lan Na script ... as if it were the official script of a modern state is quite misleading"; Lan Na kings erected stone inscriptions in a different script (Fak Kham). Yet "the most extended influence of the Lan Na polity closely corresponded to the geographical area where the Tham script was used" (Grabowsky 2007: 124 on the "Greater Lan Na cultural area" incl. Lu and Khun east of the Salween).

4. Mechanism of diffusion: under King Tilokarat (1441–1487) Lan Na expanded toward Nan, Phrae, Shan areas and Sipsong Panna while supporting the newly introduced Sinhalese order (Sihala Nikaya); itinerant monks of Wat Pa Daeng carried Buddha images and scriptures into the Shan area (Chronicle of Wat Pa Daeng; Sommai and Swearer 1977). "Lan Na Tham script accompanied the spread of Buddhism" (Sarassawadee Ongsakul 2005: 6).

5. Core concept defined: following Veidlinger's observation that manuscript find-spots let us "virtually delineate the borders of Lan Na," Iijima tentatively names the area "where manuscripts employing the Tham script have been found as 'the cultural region of Tham script manuscripts'" — grounded in "the enduring materiality of the manuscripts" rather than outsider mapping or polity boundaries.

6. Shan evidence: Yuan Buddhism and the Tham script were prevalent in Hsenwi (northern Shan State) until the early twentieth century (Iijima 2007: 473–477); hence "the Salween River never constituted a decisive dividing line ... at least in religious and cultural spheres."

7. Methodological stance: emphasizes on-site inquiry and tracing "the history of manuscripts' journeys in conjunction with human movement"; digital copies ("texts coded on floppy disks and CDs") are not equivalent to palm-leaf manuscripts; calls for returning "thickness" to texts (Saito 9 2009) now that microfilmed texts are partly available and digitization "is at hand."

8. Rural monasteries: endorses McDaniel's focus on rural monasteries outside Chiang Mai, which produced abundant manuscripts replete with local legends; McDaniel concluded "there was great intellectual activity in rural Northern Thai monastic institutions before 1902" (McDaniel 2008: 76–77).

## Relevance to project chapters

- Supplies the "Tham script cultural region" frame for the Lanna chapter: glossing traditions (nissaya etc.) circulated across a transnational script domain, not within one kingdom.
- Dates vernacular adaptation of tham to no later than 1465 CE; supports Pali-first, vernacular-gloss bilingual structure of Lanna manuscripts.
