2. Book: Gathering Leaves and Lifting Words: Histories of Buddhist Monastic Education in Laos and Thailand, University of Washington Press, 2008; won the Harry Benda Prize; first comprehensive study of monastic education in Thailand and Laos (publisher pages: https://uwapress.uw.edu/book/9780295988498/gathering-leaves-and-lifting-words/ ; Project MUSE https://muse.jhu.edu/book/20792).

3. Typology summary quoted by Herngseng 2023 (Manusya 26.1) from McDaniel 2008 p. 131: nissaya and namasadda = close word-for-word glosses; vohara = cites 12-25-word Pali passages with extensive creative vernacular translation/commentary; nissaya intermediate at 4-10 cited words before glosses.

4. Genre definitions per Craig Reynolds' review (New Mandala, 7 Feb 2010): namasadda literally "word-book or glossary", filled with marginalia, corrections, emendations; nissaya = "supports", notes for telling stories, giving sermons, explaining instructions; vohara lift words/phrases from ancient-seeming texts and contextualise them in vernacular sermons. Monk-teachers create curricula by gathering palm-leaf manuscripts and lifting words (yok sap); Alton Becker's term "languaging" names the practice (https://www.newmandala.org/book-review/review-of-mcdaniel).

5. Current project listing (Pali Text Society): "Dr Justin McDaniel is analyzing and translating various Nissaya, Vohara, and Namasadda Manuscripts from Laos and Northern Thailand" (https://palitextsociety.org/current-projects-in-pali-studies).

## Relevance to project chapters

- Core citation for defining nissaya/namasadda/vohara and for the claim that these genres dominate surviving Lanna-Lao manuscript production relative to canonical texts.
