## Content notes

### Scope and argument (from publisher description, verified on multiple pages)

- Explores Chinese manuscripts from ninth-tenth century Dunhuang, discovered in 1900 in the sealed side-chamber of a Buddhist cave temple.
- Discovery comprised tens of thousands of texts in over twenty different languages and scripts: Chinese, Tibetan, Old Uighur, Khotanese, Sogdian, Sanskrit.
- Centres on four groups of manuscripts from the mid-ninth to late tenth centuries (the Guiyijun period of local rule).
- Central thesis: the manuscripts attest a unique cultural makeup with heavy Central Asian influence alongside Chinese elements; ninth-tenth century Dunhuang was much less "Chinese" than commonly imagined in modern scholarship.

### Table of contents items relevant to annotation culture (Google Books TOC)

- Chapters on manuscripts written by students; writing direction (writing from left to right); circulars and names; colophons appear among indexed terms.
- Indexed terms include: multiple-text manuscripts, concertinas, folio numbers, donor inscriptions, association circulars, colophon, Fujieda, Dregè, Guiyijun, Xiaojing, Lunyu - i.e., book-format diversity and scribal paratexts are core concerns.
- Companion monograph by same author pair: van Schaik and Galambos 2012, Manuscripts and Travellers: The Sino-Tibetan Documents of a Tenth-Century Buddhist Pilgrim (De Gruyter) - the Sino-Tibetan documents of Zhai Fengda's circle.
