## Content notes

### The Tibeto-Chinese merchant phrasebook: Or.8210/S.1000 and S.2736

- Two scrolls (British Library Stein shelfmarks Or.8210/S.1000 and S.2736; noted as not yet digitized at the time of the IDP post) containing a Tibetan-Chinese word-and-phrase book.
- Layout principle: the Tibetan word first, then the Chinese equivalent - all written in TIBETAN script, i.e. Chinese rendered phonetically.
- Implied user: a traveller who knows Tibetan but little or no Chinese.
- Lexical field dominated by commerce: goods (food, clothes, tools, weapons, armour), haggling phrases including "last price" and "thanks for letting me look", food-and-lodging phrases, illness/robbery/theft accusation phrases, invoking the Tibetan emperor or a local official, consulting a ritual specialist (bon po, given with its Chinese translation), closing with the humorous phrase "shut up!".
- Foundational study: Thomas, F.W. and Lionel Giles, "A Tibeto-Chinese Word-and-Phrase Book", BSOAS 12.2-3 (1948): 753-769.

### IOL Khot 140: Khotanese monastery goods list glossed in Tibetan

- 10th-century list of goods for a Khotanese monastery (silk and wool coats, trousers, undergarments, shoes, blankets, camel-skin pouch, silver cup, incense).
- Signed by the receiver (nasaka) Ratnavrksa plus witnesses - a receipt rather than a shopping list.
- Key point for this loop: a few items on this Khotanese list are GLOSSED IN TIBETAN, suggesting Tibetan had become the lingua franca of the Dunhuang marketplace by the 10th century.

### Pelliot chinois 5538: Sanskrit-Khotanese pilgrimage colloquy

- Scroll with Sanskrit and Khotanese phrases on pilgrimage themes, late 10th century; the recto carries an official letter from the king of Khotan (Visa Sura) to the ruler of Dunhuang.
- Sample dialogue: pilgrim bound for China to see the bodhisattva Manjusri; provisions and horses talk; gossip about a Tibetan teacher ("He is dear to many women...").
- Function debate: phrasebook vs classroom exercise. Van Schaik's later study (chapter in Medieval Multilingual Manuscripts, De Gruyter OA volume DOI 10.1515/9783110776492, chapter 10.1515/9783110776492-004, 2022) concludes it is a pedagogical tool comparable to medieval Latin colloquies, teaching a vernacular monastic Sanskrit used as lingua franca to a Khotanese-speaking learner.

### Pelliot chinois 2782: Tibetan language written in Khotanese script

- Letter (or copy of a letter) addressed to a Tibetan lama, written in the Khotanese Brahmi-derived script but in the TIBETAN language: writer knew spoken Tibetan but could only write Khotanese script.
- The Tibetan was reconstructed by Ryotai Kaneko and published by H.W. Bailey; van Schaik offers a revised English translation. Content: polite conventions, then a request by travellers returning from Wutaishan (Manjusri pilgrimage) to visit and make an offering.
- Van Schaik's framing: these accidentally preserved phrasebooks, glossed lists and multilingual letters are scraps of evidence for unsung linguistic adventurers; merchants' linguistic efforts paved the way for the transmission of Buddhism before the royal translation projects.
