## Content notes

### Corpus overview

- Cave 17 at Dunhuang yielded tens of thousands of manuscripts of the 4th to early 11th centuries; great majority Chinese and Tibetan, plus a number of Khotanese and Sogdian texts of the 8th-10th centuries.
- Dispersal: mostly Pelliot collection (Bibliotheque Nationale de France) and Stein collection (British Library); small number in St Petersburg (Oldenburg collection), China, Japan.
- Khotanese Dunhuang texts are all in Late Khotanese and many were produced IN Dunhuang, reflecting activities of Khotanese monks and diplomats (monks often functioned as envoys) during the strong 10th-century Khotan-Dunhuang relationship. Handy survey/list: Kumamoto 1996 "The Khotanese in Dunhuang".

### Classification of Dunhuang Khotanese manuscripts (after Kumamoto 1996: 90-98)

1. Group 1 (ca. 90 mss): texts written on the BACKSIDE (verso) of Chinese Buddhist scrolls - manuscript reuse as the material basis of cross-language annotation.
2. Group 2 (ca. 30 mss): "fair copies" on pothi-style folios - Buddhist sutras (e.g. Vajracchedika) and medical texts such as the Siddhasara - written in formal or careful cursive.

### Annotation-culture evidence

- Entertainment and schoolroom texts: Ramayana and Sudhanavadana narratives, lyric poems, alphabet exercises.
- Bilingual teaching tools attested: several copies of a Chinese-Khotanese conversation manual (ed. Takata pp. 195-227); a Buddhist Chinese text phonetically transcribed in Brahmi script (Emmerick and Pulleyblank).
- The Sanskrit-Khotanese conversation manual was copied on the blank side of a royal letter of King Visa' Sura (r. 967-78) - i.e., glossary practice layered directly onto diplomatic correspondence.
- A teacher's model alphabet fair copy carries the introductory sentence "Learn, pupil, so that you do not get my stick!" (Emmerick and Vorob'eva-Desjatovskaja, pp. 165-70) - classroom culture made explicit.
- Drafts of letters to the Khotanese royal court found among the corpus (Kumamoto 1982).

### Sogdian texts

- Sogdians were leading international traders under Sui-Tang; communities settled at Dunhuang.
- Nearly all Dunhuang Sogdian manuscripts published; late 9th-10th century texts show strong Turkic influence ("Turco-Sogdian"), mainly commercial documents by scribes bilingual in Sogdian and Uighur; at least three written by Christians.
- Zoroastrian Ashem Vohu fragment (Avestan phonetically transcribed in Sogdian script) - earliest surviving written Avestan; Rostam fragment (Sogdian Shah-nama) shows diaspora national culture.

### Reference infrastructure named

- Bailey, Khotanese Texts series and Khotanese Buddhist Texts (1951) - virtually all Dunhuang Khotanese transcribed/published there.
- Skjaervo 2002, The Khotanese Manuscripts from Chinese Turkestan in the British Library: complete catalogue with texts and translations (BL holdings).
- Zhang Guangda and Rong Xinjiang (pp. 118-48) list the Paris Khotanese texts.
- Signature prefixes marking Dunhuang provenance: Ch. (Qianfodong), Or., S (Stein), P (Pelliot), SI O (Serindia Oldenburg).
- Staël-Holstein roll dated 9 March 925 CE: best-known Khotanese document (place names, Turkish tribal names), present whereabouts unknown.
