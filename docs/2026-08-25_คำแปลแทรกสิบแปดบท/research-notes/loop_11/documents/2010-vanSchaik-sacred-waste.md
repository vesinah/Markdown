## Content notes

### Why the cave was sealed - state of the question

- No agreement exists on why the manuscripts were placed in the cave or why it was sealed; van Schaik calls this failure deeply problematic for all scholarship based on the corpus.
- Aurel Stein in Serindia (1921, quote at p. 820) proposed the manuscripts were discarded writing that could not be destroyed because of its sacred Buddhist content: "sacred waste".
- Akira Fujieda accepted and developed the waste idea; in China it is named feiqi shuo (the waste theory). Fujieda's specific version: manuscripts were disposed of once printing was introduced to Dunhuang. Fang Guangchang's alternative: texts removed from local monasteries after a large-scale inventory project (Fang 1988).
- Stein himself also noted some bundles looked as if deposited as a religious ("superstitious") act.

### The merit-economy reading

- The cave contains hundreds of complete copies of the same sutra, many copied for patrons: e.g. a single Lotus Sutra copy made for a nun mourning her mother; hundreds of copies of the Aparimitayus sutra to ensure the long life of the Tibetan emperor.
- John Kieschnick (The Impact of Buddhism on Chinese Material Culture, 2003, p. 170): the prodigious store of near-identical Diamond Sutra copies from Dunhuang are mostly "receipts" for merit-giving transactions rather than scriptures that were read.
- Depositing such copies in the cave = final act of a ritual process of merit creation and dedication; donor portraits on paintings show the same commission-paint-deposit cycle.

### The relic-dedication hypothesis

- Van Schaik applies Richard Salomon's analysis of the Gandhari Kharosthi scrolls (Salomon 2009, p. 30): manuscripts as dharma-relics, functionally equivalent to bodily relics; interring them is relic dedication.
- Implication: even if the waste theory stands partly, every deposition was a ritual act within the merit system - not pragmatic landfill.
- Comments thread records the concealment-under-threat hypothesis (associated with Pelliot and others); van Schaik notes there is not a shred of direct evidence for it.
