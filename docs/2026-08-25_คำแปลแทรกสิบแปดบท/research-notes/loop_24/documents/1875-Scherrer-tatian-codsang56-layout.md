## Content (harvested)

- Support/extent: parchment; 342 pages; format 2° (34 x 26 cm).
- Page layout: 32 lines to the page, two columns ("Zu 32 Zeilen, zweispaltig von Mehrern"), strong grey parchment.
- Binding: original binding with spine title "Plenarium".
- Main language: Althochdeutsch (Old High German).
- Contents: (1) ff. 1–2 incipit praefatio Victoris episcopi Capuae; (2) ff. 3–18 Canones X concordantiae evangeliorum; (3) ff. 19–24 elenchus of 181 chapters (Codex Fuldensis has 182, the Cassel codex 184); (4) ff. 25–342 the Latin and Old High German text of the gospel harmony in 181 chapters — "das Lateinische in der Spalte links, das Deutsche gegenüber" (the Latin in the left column, the German opposite).

Key point for layout studies: this is a full parallel-column bilingual translation, not word-by-word interlinear glossing.

Filiation: unique manuscript of this old German translation except an Oxford copy made by Junius of a now-lost codex formerly owned by B. Vulcanius (that copy lacks chapters 76–153). Editions: Palthenius (Greifswald 1706), Schilter Thesaurus II p. 59; from the St Gall manuscript by J. A. Schmeller (Matthew only, Stuttgart 1827; complete Vienna 1841) and Eduard Sievers with glossary (Bibliothek der ältesten deutschen Litteratur-Denkmäler V, Paderborn 1872). Sievers distinguished seven scribes of the codex and suspected several successive reworkers of the German translation, whose linguistic form is High Franconian (Fulda dialect).

Attribution background: the Latin text is attributed by Victor of Capua (6th c.) in his preface in the oldest manuscript to Tatian (2nd c.), known as author of a lost Greek Diatessaron; newer opinion derives it from Ammonios (3rd c.); probably a mere compilation from the Vulgate.

Provenance note: on the inside front cover (13th c.): "Hunc librum cum diligentia quaesitum diu et vix inventum resumite sancte pater Erneste in nom. dom." — Pater Ernestus appears as St Gall dean still in 1271. Codex 56 is mentioned in no old St Gall catalogue, neither that of the 9th century nor that of 1461.
