## Substance of the 2011 article, as documented in Ekman 2019 footnotes

(Each item cites the specific pages of Andrée 2011 given in Ekman's notes.)

1. Summary of modern assessments of Anselm of Laon's originality is at pp. 219-220 (Ekman n.28).
2. The Glosae super Iohannem must have been produced at Laon and must predate the Gloss on John; Andrée builds on Stirnemann's dating and on his own 2008 Revue Bénédictine survey of the manuscripts (Ekman n.85, citing Andrée 2011 at 234-245 and CCCM 267 preface xvi-xxii).
3. Authorship: there are two manuscript attributions of the Glosae to Anselm; since only two candidates exist (Anselm of Laon vs Anselm of Canterbury, the latter credited with no John-commentary), the attributions carry evidential weight (Ekman n.86, citing 232-233; repeated CCCM 267 xvi-xviii).
4. Negative conclusion: because no glossed-book manuscripts survive from before the late 1120s, Andrée considers it unlikely that Anselm was directly responsible for the Gloss on John or any part of it — the opposite conclusion of Smalley and Dove (Ekman n.87). Key quoted formulation: "that an entire hypothetical first generation of glossed books should have been completely annihilated without leaving any trace seems rather unlikely" (cited at 227-28).
5. Positive reconstruction: Anselm supplied material for parts of the Gloss unknowingly, through continuous commentaries (the Glosae) produced in his lectures; pupils mined, abridged and rearranged these after his death in double-gloss format to preserve his celebrated teaching — explaining the posthumous credit for parts or all of the enterprise (Ekman n.88). Possible successors named: Ralph, Gilbert the Universal, Gilbert of Poitiers (228 n.47).
6. Peter Comestor's testimony (Psalt. 118,115; Paris BnF lat. 12011, fol. 173r) that Anselm "was not allowed to completely gloss the whole Bible as he had begun" is judged by Andrée NOT to refer to the Gloss (cited at p. 234).

## Significance for the book project

The article resolves into a two-stage model: (a) Anselm's oral-classroom commentaries (continuous glosae), then (b) student compilation into marginal+interlinear glossed books after ca. 1117 — i.e., authorship transferred from master to school process. This matches Andree's later Traditio 71 (2016) formulation that "the essential function of the Glossa has been overlooked: it was a taught text."
