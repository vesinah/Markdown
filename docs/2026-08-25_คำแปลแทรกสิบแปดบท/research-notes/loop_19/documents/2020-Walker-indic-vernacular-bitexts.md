## Framework (abstract and introduction)

- Mainland Southeast Asians developed techniques "to accurately and efficiently render Pali into local vernaculars, including Burmese, Khmer, Khün, Lanna, Lao, Lü, Mon, and Siamese"; these produced bitexts "arranged in both interlinear and interphrasal formats" that "sprout[ed] a vernacular literature from the established branches of Pali genres."
- Definition: "bitexts are texts that are presented bilingually, with portions in one language mixed together with portions in another, typically in an interphrasal or interlinear arrangement."
- Composition model, three required steps: (1) selection; (2) analysis via five techniques — parsing/amplification/syntactic rearrangement/grammatical annotation/gloss (2a–2e); (3) presentation in four modes — philological (3a), exegetical (3b), homiletic (3c), poetic (3d). In Siamese and Lanna contexts all five analysis techniques were commonly applied per passage.
- Audience mapping: philological = linguistic training; exegetical = scholastic readers; homiletic = public preaching; poetic = court recitation/belles-lettres.
- Thesis against McDaniel: bitext conventions were strict and shared across mainland SEA from the 11th century onward, not idiosyncratic per teacher (McDaniel, Gathering Leaves and Lifting Words, 2008, pp. 119–160, is the explicit counterpoint).

## Local terminology inventory (fn 1)

nissaya (Burmese); naṃ/trā-ai (Mon); nisrai/nissăy (Lanna, Lao, Siamese); nāmaśăbd/săpº (Lanna, Lao, Siamese); cuṇṇiyapad (Siamese); ṕlè yak śăbd (Siamese); prè lot prayog (Khmer). Transliteration follows Walker, "Unfolding Buddhism" (PhD diss., UC Berkeley, 2018), xiii–xvii.

## Chronology of diffusion

- First millennium: bilingual inscriptions mostly divergent in form/content; exceptions: an interphrasal Sanskrit-Pyu inscription tentatively dated 6th c.; second-millennium Sanskrit-Khmer inscriptions with parallel content (citing Griffiths et al., BEFEO 103 [2017]: 43–205 on Pyu; Chhom Kunthea, EPHE PhD 2016, 319–340, on Sanskrit in Khmer).
- Reliably dated first-millennium Pali-Sinhala bitexts exist; Sinhala omits grammatical annotation since morphology signals it.
- Earliest mainland SEA example: Pali-Mon inscription late 11th/early 12th c.; Pali-Burmese from late 13th c.; several Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts listed in a 1442 inscription.
- Tai groups by mid-second millennium: "The earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext is thought to date from 1482 in Ayutthaya. The earliest securely dated Pali-Lanna bitext surviving in manuscript form dates from 1552." Sanskrit-Lanna survives from 1578. Oldest extant Pali-Lao bitexts from a similar period.

## Philological mode: Siamese interlinear annotations (khaam hvăt / tŭa khian)

- Seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Siam (and Cambodia): palm-leaf Pali mss carry a third hand's ink interlinear annotations in a special script, "known in Thai as khaam hvăt or t́vă kṣien," found almost exclusively on monastic-curriculum texts: Kaccāyanavyākaraṇa, Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā, Maṅgalatthadīpanī, Sāratthasaṅgaha, Samantapāsādikā, Visuddhimagga, Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha. Most common in late 18th–19th c. mss but attested earlier (Kongkaew Veeraprajak publications 2001/2002; likely unidentified 17th-c. examples at the National Library of Thailand).
- Case study: Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā ms, Swift Family Collection, Bancroft Library UC Berkeley, ff 4MS PL4251 no. 14 (folio image: http://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/fx/hb7f59p4fx/files/hb7f59p4fx-FID452.jpg ; manuscript: http://www.oac.cdlib.org/ark:/13030/hb7f59p4fx/?&brand=oac4 ).
- Decoding demonstrated on Dhammapada 320 stanza: annotator supplies five Pali amplifications, fourteen numerals for syntactic rearrangement, two vernacular grammatical particles, three Pali glosses. Vernacular conditional particle mè~ị̄ö (modern Thai mè2 mị̄ö1) = "even though"; Pali-Pali gloss marker maḥ (short of maḥ vā1 / moḥ1) "roughly means 'that is to say'," equivalent to moḥ in Middle Khmer, muh in Old Khmer, măk vā1 in Lanna.
- Key comparative claim (fn 27): many such Pali-Siamese bitexts traveled to Cambodia in the 18th–20th c.; because Khmer and Thai have very similar syntax, the same rearrangement works for Khmer (citing Huffman, JAOS 93.4 [1973]: 488–509). The annotation symbol's "likely Khmer origins" are discussed in Walker, "Unfolding Buddhism," p. 335.
- Full-out inscribed (not inked) interlinear annotations occur on Kaccāyanavyākaraṇa copies — "the primary treatise used to teach Pali grammar in Siam, Laos, and Cambodia prior to the twentieth century."

## Homiletic mode (Pali-Lanna contrast set)

Nisrai mahosathapaṇḍit, earliest copy 1563 CE; a 1666 homily drawing on the Sāratthadīpanī retains interphrasal presentation or drops to long vernacular passages with sparse Pali insertions.

## Poetic mode: the Mahājāti Gāṃ Hlvaṅ of 1482

- "Believed to have been composed in 1482 under the aegis of King Borommatrailokkanat of Ayutthaya (Paramat́railokanāth, r. 1431–1488)," it is "the earliest extant Tai-language version of the Mahāvessantara-jātaka and one of the earliest known pieces of Siamese literature." Only seven of thirteen chapters survive; six reconstituted in early Rattanakosin (history per Niyaḥtā Hlo1sundar in the Royal Institute 2549/2006 edition, pp. 721–724; Walker cites only that printed edition, unable to access manuscripts).
- Format: Pali aṭṭhakathā phrases followed by parsed, amplified, rearranged, grammatically annotated, glossed Siamese; glosses chained in the rāy1 yāv rhyme scheme — among its earliest attestations in Siamese literature. First in the line of royal gāṃ hlvaṅ compositions (Pali-Siamese and Sanskrit-Siamese bitexts).
- Amplifications partly parallel the Vessantaradīpanī of Sirimaṅgala of Chiang Mai (1517) (Yamanaka 2009), showing commentarial-network awareness plus original interpretation.

## Conclusion points usable verbatim in the book draft

- "From the fifteenth to the nineteenth century, the analytic techniques and presentation styles of Indic-vernacular bitexts occupied a critical place in the intellectual culture of mainland Southeast Asia, including Siam and Lanna."
- "The sheer volume of compositions inscribed in a bitextual format on palm-leaf and folded-paper manuscripts across the region is staggering; very few have been subject to contemporary methods of critical reading, editing, and translation."
