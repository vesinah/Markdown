## Key findings (with URLs)

1. Walker's JAOS framework confirmed and harvested in full. Title: "Indic-Vernacular Bitexts from Thailand: Bilingual Modes of Philology, Exegetics, Homiletics, and Poetry, 1450–1850," JAOS 140.3 (2020): 675–700, Stanford affiliation, Khyentse Foundation postdoc at Chulalongkorn (with Peter Skilling, Arthid Sheravanichkul acknowledged). Definition: bitexts are texts presented bilingually, Pali/Sanskrit portions mixed with vernacular portions, "typically in an interphrasal or interlinear arrangement." Composition follows three steps — selection, analysis (parsing, amplification, syntactic rearrangement, grammatical annotation, gloss), presentation in four modes (philological, exegetical, homiletic, poetic), each mode matched to an audience. He argues strict conventions were shared across mainland Southeast Asia from the eleventh century onward, against McDaniel's idiosyncrasy thesis. https://doi.org/10.7817/jameroriesoci.140.3.0675 ; free published-version PDF: https://lockwoodonlinejournals.com/index.php/jaos/article/download/679/532

2. Local terminology inventory (Walker fn 1) — direct answer to the "khmer nissaya?" naming question: nissaya (Burmese), naṃ/trā-ai (Mon), nisrai/nissăy (Lanna, Lao, Siamese), nāmaśăbd/săpº (Lanna, Lao, Siamese), cuṇṇiyapad (Siamese), ṕlè yak śăbd (Siamese), prè lot prayog (Khmer). There is no single "khmer nissaya" label; the Khmer tradition names its format prè lot prayog. Same URL as finding 1.

3. The 1482 Pali-Siamese literary work claim verified precisely: the Mahājāti Gāṃ Hlvaṅ (Mahachat kham luang), believed composed in 1482 in Ayutthaya under King Borommatrailokkanat (r. 1431–1488), is "the earliest extant Tai-language version of the Mahāvessantara-jātaka and one of the earliest known pieces of Siamese literature"; only 7 of 13 original chapters survive, six were reconstituted in early Rattanakosin; it is a poetic Pali-Siamese bitext whose Siamese glosses are linked in the rāy1 yāv rhyme scheme, and it founded the royal gāṃ hlvaṅ genre of Pali-Siamese and Sanskrit-Siamese bitexts. Standard edition: Royal Institute (Rājapăṇḍit́yasthān) 2549/2006. Same URL as finding 1.

4. Siamese interlinear philology: the hitherto undeciphered annotation system khaam hvăt / t́vă kṣien (ink annotations above/below inscribed lines on palm leaves) appears on seventeenth- to nineteenth-century Siamese copies of the monastic curriculum (Kaccāyanavyākaraṇa, Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā, Maṅgalatthadīpanī, Sāratthasaṅgaha, Samantapāsādikā, Visuddhimagga, Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha). Walker gives the first proposed decoding using a mid-nineteenth-century Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā manuscript in the Swift Family Collection, Bancroft Library, UC Berkeley (ff 4MS PL4251 no. 14; folio image http://cdn.calisphere.org/data/13030/fx/hb7f59p4fx/files/hb7f59p4fx-FID452.jpg ; whole manuscript http://www.oac.cdlib.org/ark:/13030/hb7f59p4fx/?&brand=oac4 ). The annotations combine numbered syntactic-rearrangement markers, case/tense-mood particles (e.g., mè~ị̄ö "even though"), amplifications, and a Pali-Pali gloss marker maḥ (= moḥ), cognate with Middle Khmer moḥ, Old Khmer muh, Lanna măk vā1 — hard evidence that Siamese, Khmer, and Lanna glossing systems share one underlying technology. Same URL as finding 1.

5. Distribution chronology across traditions (Walker, section I): first-millennium bilingual inscriptions mostly divergent in form/content, exceptions include a 6th-c. interphrasal Sanskrit-Pyu inscription and second-millennium Sanskrit-Khmer epigraphy (Chhom Kunthea EPHE 2016); reliably dated first-millennium Pali-Sinhala bitexts exist; earliest mainland SEA bitext is a Pali-Mon inscription late 11th/early 12th c. (Blagden, Epigraphia Birmanica); Pali-Burmese from late 13th c.; Sanskrit-Burmese listed in a 1442 inscription; earliest complete Pali-Siamese bitext thought to be 1482 (Ayutthaya); earliest securely dated Pali-Lanna manuscript 1552 (DLNTM 011903027_00, http://lannamanuscripts.net/en/manuscripts/3700 ); Sanskrit-Lanna 1578 (DLNTM 011318001_01); exegetical Pali-Lanna bitexts 1585 and 1638; homiletic Nisrai mahosathapaṇḍit dated 1563 and a 1666 Sāratthadīpanī-based sermon; oldest surviving Pali-Lao bitext is a bilingual Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha of CS 948 = 1586/87 CE (PLMP code 08040102007_04). Note: laomanuscripts.net has migrated — the Digital Library of Lao Manuscripts (12,000+ texts, National Library of Laos/Passau/SBB) is now hosted at CrossAsia: https://digital.crossasia.org/digital-library-of-lao-manuscripts/ . Same JAOS URL for all citations.

6. Khmer-Thai transferability: Walker notes many Pali-Siamese bitexts traveled to Cambodia in the eighteenth-twentieth centuries and that because "Khmer and Thai have a very similar syntactic structure," the same syntactic-rearrangement output works for Khmer (fn 27, citing Franklin E. Huffman, "Thai and Cambodian: A Case of Syntactic Borrowing?", JAOS 93.4 [1973]: 488–509). This grounds the book-chapter claim that Siamese glossing technology functioned as a regional substrate beyond Lanna. Same URL as finding 1.

7. Laulertvorakul identified — the brief's "Laulertvorakul 2003" is Anant Laulertvorakul (อนันต์ เหล่าเลิศวรกุล): (a) ปฐมสมโพธิสำนวนล้านช้าง (The Lan Xang Version of the Pathamasambodhi), Journal of Letters 31.1 (2002): 54–82, abstract: the Lan Xang recension is the oldest regional-dialect Pathamasambodhi and "เป็นฉบับแปลแบบนิสสัยเพียงฉบับเดียว" (the only nissaya-style translation recension), usable as evidence linking to the older Lanna Pali Pathamasambodhi and for the size of the original story before later expansion — https://digital.car.chula.ac.th/jletters/vol31/iss1/4 (full scanned PDF downloadable, 42.5 MB); (b) Paṭhamasambodhi in Nine Languages: Their Relation and Evolution, Manusya 6.1 (2003): 11–34 — https://doi.org/10.1163/26659077-00601002 . Together these are the central Thai-language studies connecting Lao/Lanna/Siamese Paṭhamasambodhi glossing.

8. Royal/noble lineage continuity into the modern period: Prapod Assavavirulhakarn and Peter Skilling, "Tripiṭaka in Practice in the Fourth and Fifth Reigns: Relics and Images According to Somdet Phra Saṅgharāja Pussadeva's Paṭhamasambodhi Sermon" (Manusya, 2002), https://doi.org/10.1163/26659077-00504001 — documents Bangkok-court engagement with Paṭhamasambodhi preaching in the Fourth/Fifth Reigns; and Phra Akbordin Rattana, "On the Devotion to the Buddha in Paramanuchit's Mārabandha Episode of the Paṭhamasambodhi" (2016, Semantic Scholar record, no OA copy located) touches Prince-Patriarch Paramanuchit Chinorot's Paṭhamasambodhi, the classic noble-authored Thai Buddha-biography. Specific claims that contemporary Mahachulalongkornrajavidyalaya translation practice derives methodologically from nissaya: (ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน) — see gaps.

9. Pre-modern Siam's literary geography (RQ2 background): Skilling, "Geographies of Intertextuality: Buddhist Literature in Pre-modern Siam," Aséanie 19 (2007): 91–112 — opens with Dharmakīrti of Suvarṇadvīpa's early-11th-century Durbodhāloka (Abhisamayālaṅkāra commentary, oldest datable SEA Buddhist commentary, preserved only in Tibetan translation by Atiśa's circle), arguing Siam participated in pan-Buddhist textual circulation long before the Sukhothai-Ayutthaya Pali vernacularization visible in bitexts. https://www.persee.fr/doc/asean_0859-9009_2007_num_19_1_2027

10. Khmer manuscript heritage (RQ3): Filliozat, "Pour mémoire d'un patrimoine sacré: Les manuscrits pāli du Cambodge à l'École française d'Extrême-Orient," BEFEO 87.2 (2000): 445–471 — recounts the EFEO's century-old Cambodian Pali collections, opening with Coedès's 1912 report that Cambodian monks preserved their Pali manuscripts well and his policy of acquiring sattras for philologists; also documents earlier EFEO funds of Pali manuscripts in Burmese and Mon scripts (Duroiselle, Huber missions). https://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_2000_num_87_2_3487 . Mon tradition technical vocabulary (naṃ/trā-ai bitext labels; Old Khmer muh / Middle Khmer moḥ gloss marker) appears in Walker fn 1 and fn on the maḥ particle (finding 4).

11. Newest development for the chapter: Tossaphon Sripum and Trent Walker, "Indic-Siamese Bitexts and Ayutthaya Scribal Culture: Exposition and Exegesis in Three Kham Luang Manuscripts," Manuscript Studies 11.1 (2026): 1–35, https://doi.org/10.1353/mns.2026.a990231 — a Thai-based co-author extending the bitextual framework into Ayutthaya scribal practice; confirms this is a live research front the book can cite as current scholarship.

Burmese/Mon comparative baseline (for contrast sections): Okell, "Nissaya Burmese," Lingua 15 (1965): 186–227, https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-3841(65)90013-6 ; Pruitt, Étude linguistique de nissaya birmans (EFEO 1994); Tin Lwin, SOAS PhD 1961 on a Mahāparinibbāna-sutta nissaya; McCormick on Mon Rājāvaṁsa Katnā history-telling, https://doi.org/10.1353/jbs.2011.0010 .
