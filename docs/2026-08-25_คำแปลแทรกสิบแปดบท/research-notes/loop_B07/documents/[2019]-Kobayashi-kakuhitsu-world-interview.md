## Content notes

- Kakuhitsu defined: an ancient writing implement with a sharpened point (先端をとがらせた古筆記具); writing done as depressions (くぼみ) in the paper surface, without pigment. Kobayashi was the first to discover ancient documents written this way; honored as Person of Cultural Merit 2019 for opening three fields: history of kanbun kundoku, Kamakura-period language, and kakuhitsu literature.
- Discovery chronology: 1961 at a Koyasan temple exhibition he noticed claw-mark-like characters/signs beside kanji on a kanbun item; about a year later a second example in a Heian sutra at the Dotoku Memorial Library (大東急記念文庫), Tokyo; a third found even in a photo-reprint of a Kanseki book under favorable lighting - because there is no ink, researchers had overlooked them entirely.
- First monograph after 26 years: 100 items located by then -> 角筆文献の国語学的研究 (A linguistic study of kakuhitsu literature, Kyuko Shoin 1987); date range Nara through Edo period; majority are kunten materials showing how to read kanbun (kana and symbols); also letters, old documents, even Nara-period mokkan carry stylus insertions. Users presumably worked by candlelight. Won the Imperial Award/Japan Academy Prize (恩賜賞・日本学士院賞 1991).
- Register finding: because invisible, stylus writing is private, informal writing - if brush-and-ink writing is "hare" (public), stylus writing is "ke" (everyday); colloquialisms and vulgar speech are recorded in it.
- Nationwide survey: after retiring from Hiroshima University he spent ten years surveying Hokkaido to Okinawa and found kakuhitsu documents in ALL 47 prefectures, proving archipelago-wide use; total items discovered exceed 3,250 (3250点余); some record local dialects. Tooling: a special light called the Kakuhitsu Scope (角筆スコープ) developed with a science faculty member enabled the survey (development publication: Kobayashi Yoshinori & Yoshizawa Yasukazu 1991, "Development of the kakuhitsu scope", Shinika 2(8): 64-71).
- East Asian spread: China - during a 1985 visiting professorship (Beijing center for Japanese studies) he visited Lanzhou and confirmed depressions resembling stylus marks on the Han-dynasty Wuwei wooden slips (武威漢簡, ca. 2000 years old); later found stylus insertions in Dunhuang documents, in Song-dynasty woodblock-printed issaikyo sutra sets, and in Ming/Qing texts. Korea - 2000 survey of major Seoul university libraries and museums: on copies of the FIRST-CARVED Goryeo Tripitaka (初雕高麗版, 11th c.) he found, written in stylus, both 点吐 jeomto (the analogue of Japanese wokototen) and 字吐 jato (analogue of kana phonograms); also attested in post-13th-c. materials; hence stylus glossing was practiced in Korea too, within the shared sinographic reading culture, with mutual exchange.
- Europe/Middle East: surveying the British Museum he confirmed former use of similar implements: 11th-12th-c. hand-copied Bibles bear insertions of archaic letters/symbols/pictures made like stylus work; Quran copies also show such insertions; research barely begun.
- Ongoing decipherment: a sutra brought from Silla in the 8th c., held at Todaiji, glossed IN SILLA LANGUAGE by stylus; joint decoding with Korean researchers every year since 2009; by the planned 15th joint meeting (Jan 2020, Todaiji) 757 of 1,141 lines had been decoded.
- Closing claim: shining light from the kakuhitsu perspective will open a new cultural history.
- Bio sidebar: b. 1929 Yamanashi; Tokyo Bunrika Daigaku; professor at Hiroshima U. 1972, emeritus 1992; principal works: 角筆文献の国語学的研究, 角筆のみちびく世界, 漢文訓読史の研究, 角筆文献研究導論, 角筆のひらく文化史―見えない文字を読み解く.
