## The corner-to-tone mapping (all dictionaries agree)

- Dots are attached at the FOUR CORNERS of a character square (or at midpoints between corners) to show the Middle Chinese four tones:
  - LOWER-LEFT = 平声 level tone
  - UPPER-LEFT = 上声 rising tone
  - UPPER-RIGHT = 去声 departing tone
  - LOWER-RIGHT = 入声 entering tone
- Origin: China; said to have been common practice under the TANG dynasty (Nihon kokugo daijiten). Japan transmitted the system and kept the same corner rule.
- Later extension: dots also attached to KANA to mark JAPANESE accent (pitch accent); called 声符 shōfu.

## Origin story inside China (Nipponica, Ishizuka Harumichi)

- Rooted in the 破音 poyin method: marking that a character carries a DERIVED reading/meaning rather than its original one.
- First stage: vermilion 点発 tenpatsu marks placed at the CENTER or SIDE of the character to flag poyin readings.
- Second stage: using the four-tone frame, the dot moved to the CORNERS so as to indicate which tone-class the derived reading belonged to; initially an upper-right = level-tone convention existed, later fixed to lower-left = level.
- Third stage (late Tang): marks detached from the poyin function and became pure TONE indicators - this is shōten proper.

## Forms and the dakuten connection

- Shapes: white-circle 圏点 marks vs black star-dot 星点 marks; kanji usually receive circles, while siddham (Sanskrit bonji) characters carrying interlinear kanji notes, and wakun glosses, more often receive star points (Nihon kokugo daijiten).
- Two-stroke circles and single-stroke dots attested (Britannica).
- Once VOICING came to be distinguished: ONE point for voiceless 清音, TWO stacked points for voiced 濁音 - this double-dot practice is identified as the ORIGIN OF THE DAKUTEN 濁点 (Nihon kokugo daijiten; Britannica; MyPedia).

## Six-tone elaboration

- A six-value system adds 平声軽 "light level" (marked on the left side, midpoint, slightly low position; inferred falling contour) and 入声軽 "light entering" (right side, midpoint, slightly low; inferred high-level like 上声) (Britannica; also Nipponica mentions 平軽・入軽).

## Earliest Japanese examples and subsequent history (Nipponica)

- Poyin and shōten both transmitted to Japan; FIRST JAPANESE EXAMPLES visible from around the VERY END OF THE 9TH CENTURY ("9世紀極末期ごろから実例がみられる").
- From the EARLY 11TH CENTURY shōten were attached to kana as well, making them prime data for the history of Japanese pitch accent.
- Heian-period tonal values distinguished: 平 = low-level pitch; 上 = high-level; 去 = final-rising (shiriagari); 平軽 = final-falling (shirisagari). Later 平軽 and 去 merged unmarked; from the Kamakura period generally only 平 vs 上 remained distinguished - continuing to today.
- Inferred Japanese tonal values of the four Chinese tones when used on kanji: 平 low-level, 上 high-level, 去 rising, 入 checked/abrupt like level (Britannica).

## Corroborating pedagogical source (kambun.jp, Matsumoto Jun 2004)

- Confirms the same corner mapping as the historical vermilion-corner practice: "昔は、漢字の四隅に朱で点を打って、声調をしめしていました" - in former times vermilion dots at the four corners indicated tone; modern kanwa dictionaries preserve the system in their tone/rhyme boxes (dot at lower-left = level tone etc., with the rhyme group name inside the box).
- Also documents Edo-period workarounds: since Japanese kanji-on lost tone contrasts, Edo poets memorized level-vs-oblique (平仄) word tables marked with open circles for 平 and filled dots for 仄 (e.g., Sakumon Kanmon, Meiji Shoin ed., p. 306), recited as HYO-HYO/SOKU-SOKU patterns; and notes entering-tone characters remain identifiable from historical kana endings -ku/-tsu/-ki/-chi/-fu.
