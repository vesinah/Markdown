## Content notes

### A Yuan-period Old Uyghur manuscript with Chinese characters read aloud in Uyghur

- The article discusses a text first published by Peter Zieme in 2017, republished in his collected articles (Zieme 2020) and now numbered text 27 in Berliner Turfantexte vol. 47: an Old Uighur text on the significance of the four Buddhist agamas.
- Provenance: collected by Paul Pelliot at Dunhuang cave no. 181 (today's numbering B 464).
- Script practice: "The quite numerous interspersed Chinese characters are supposed to be pronounced as Old Uyghur words because suffixes can be attached to them according to the demands of Old Uyghur syntax and phonology."
- The system "is similar to the manuscript from Dunhuang edited by Sinasi Tekin in 1980."
- Dating evidence: confusion of dentals and sibilants points to origin during the Yuan dynasty.

### Sample of the mixed-script text (as quoted by Wilkens after Zieme's edition, lines 014-025)

Chinese characters function inside running Uyghur sentences, each supplied or understood with an Old Uyghur equivalent:

- 或 huo = azu "or"
- 次 ci = basa / anta basa "next" (Wilkens emends reading anta ci basa; equivalents attested in Wilkens 2021b: 113)
- 復 fu = avir "again"
- 三 san = uch "three" (equivalent missing in Zieme's edition, later added in Zieme 2022)
- (read: ming) 四十 sishi = qirq toerlug "forty kinds"
- 法 fa = nom "dharma/law"
- 如是 rushi = muni munchulayu "thus"
- 經 jing = sudur "sutra"
- 佛 fo = burhan "Buddha"
- 地 di = yer "earth"

Passage content: a metaphorical description (verso of folio 15 - recto of folio 16) of how a painter creates a painting step by step; painting references also occur in the Altun Yaruk Sudur itself (Radlov & Malov edition 1913-1917, folio 544.2-7).

### Contextual notes harvested

- Art-historical scholarship referenced: Lilla Russell-Smith 2005 (Dunhuang), Ines Konczak 2014 (Turfan region); Uyghurs adopted from China e.g. iconography of the Ten Kings of Hell, plus tools/materials/terminology.
- Editorial signs used: square brackets for damaged parts, parentheses for unwritten vowels, braces for superfluous letters - useful for transcription conventions in the book's methodological chapters.

## Relevance to project

A second independent witness (besides Shoogaito's St Petersburg corpus) for the practice of embedding Chinese characters into Old Uyghur text where the characters are read as Uyghur morphemes taking Uyghur suffixes - i.e., script-mixed glossing/reading culture rather than pure translation.
