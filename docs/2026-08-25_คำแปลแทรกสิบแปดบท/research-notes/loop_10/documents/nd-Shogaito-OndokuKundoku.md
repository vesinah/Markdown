## Content notes (from the fetched abstract)

### The St Petersburg corpus of Chinese texts written in Uighur script

- The author found fragments of Chinese texts written in Uighur script at the St Petersburg Branch of the Institute of Oriental Studies, Russian Academy of Sciences (= IOM RAS), and identified their corresponding Chinese originals. (His monograph on this material: Uighur Manuscripts in St. Petersburg - Chinese Texts in Uighur Script and Buddhist Texts, Kyoto 2003, cited in the Umemura-Zieme article.)
- Phonological finding: the Chinese written in Uighur script has a phonological system basically identical with the northwestern dialects of Tang and Five Dynasties times; although the fragments were composed later, in the Yuan dynasty, their phonology is quite different from colloquial Yuan Chinese - it reflects an inherited reading tradition of Chinese characters comparable to Japanese ondoku ("Chinese-style reading").

### Interlinear insertion of Chinese characters read as Uighur

- "It is occasionally observed that Chinese characters are sporadically inserted between Uighur lines in the above texts. These inserted Chinese characters must have been read in Uighur."
- These inserted characters appear not only as words but also as phrases and sentences.
- In bilingual texts such as the Thousand Character Essay (Qianziwen), "the Uighur inherited reading of Chinese is followed by its corresponding Uighur translation."
- In other texts represented by the Abhidharmakoshabhashya-Tika Tattvartha, the fragments record how Uighur speakers read Chinese texts in Uighur pronunciation while translating the contents into Uighur.

### Typological conclusion

- Conclusion: the Uighurs had their own way of reading Chinese texts typologically comparable to the Japanese kundoku system (reading Chinese characters as native Japanese). Uighur monks of the Yuan period are argued to have recited Chinese sutras by ondoku-style inherited pronunciation while understanding them through kundoku-style Uighur rendering - exactly parallel to how Japanese Buddhist monks handled Chinese liturgy.

## Relevance to project

This is the single most direct scholarly statement found this loop that Old Uighur manuscript culture possessed a genuine interlinear-gloss practice: Chinese characters inserted between Uighur lines, pronounced in Uyghur, functioning word-for-word before a running translation - the Central Eurasian counterpart of East Asian kanbun-kundoku glossing.
