## Key findings (with URLs)

1. Old Uighur had a true interlinear-gloss practice with Chinese characters read aloud as Uyghur words. Shoogaito's St Petersburg corpus shows Chinese characters "sporadically inserted between Uighur lines", read in Uighur, appearing as words, phrases, even sentences; in the bilingual Qianziwen the inherited Chinese reading is followed by its Uighur translation, and the Abhidharmakoshabhashya-Tika Tattvartha records how Uighurs read Chinese texts in Uighur pronunciation while translating them - a kundoku system typologically parallel to Japanese (https://ls-japan.org/en/kenkyu/view/abstract/127-2/). Independently, Wilkens documents a Pelliot manuscript from Dunhuang cave 181 (B 464, BT 47 text 27) where interspersed Chinese characters take Old Uyghur suffixes per Uyghur syntax/phonology (Yuan-dating from dental/sibilant confusion), similar to the Dunhuang manuscript edited by Tekin 1980 (https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/2118491).

2. The Qianziwen (Thousand Character Classic) functioned as THE bilingual primer-glossary object of Old Uighur scholarship: five distinct Old Uighur versions are known (Berlin Ch/U 8152v; SI 4000/SI 4006/SI 4084; SI 4083; SI 5778+SI 5836+SI 1787+SI 1850), covering about 440 of the 1000 characters; layout pairs a 17-character-per-line Chinese recto with an Old Uighur verso translation organized by four-character units; scribes quote the Chinese inconsistently - sometimes as characters, sometimes in Uighur-script transcription, often mixed within one line - with "no fixed arrangement" (Umemura-Zieme 2015, https://journals.eco-vector.com/2410-0145/article/view/25776). A Berlin colophon to the Qianziwen (Ch 3716, T II Y 62) written by the Uyghur Shingson Tutong of Yarkhoto in Tianxi 13th year (xinwei) was composed in Chinese but follows Old Uyghur syntax - evidence of Uyghur-shaped Chinese scribal culture (Yakup 2023, https://www.akjournals.com/abstract/journals/062/76/4/article-p529.xml; conference abstract: https://www.altaist.org/home/confessions/yakup-abdurishid/crossroads-of-languages-old-uyghur-interpretation-of-a-chinese-colophon-in-the-berlin-turfan-collection/).

3. Altun Yaruk Sudur (SuvarnabhAsottamasUtra, from Yijing's Chinese translation) is quantifiably the most-copied work of Uyghur Buddhism (Brepols description: "With regard to the number of extant copies the Altun Yaruk Sudur is the most important work in Uygur Buddhism", https://www.brepols.net/products/IS-9782503513140-1). Its research history runs from Mueller 1908 and Radlov-Malov 1913-1917 to Kaya 1994 and beyond, with "Saint Petersburg" and "Berlin" versions distinguished (https://tsj.enu.kz/index.php/new/article/view/606). Glossing-relevant phenomena inside the tradition: Kitsudō's study of "Teachings of the Consciousness Only Inserted in Chapter 6, Book 4" documents doctrinal interpolations (doi:10.14220/9783737009324.187); Zieme 2020 shows native-language appositions glossing transcribed Sanskrit names inside the buyan avirmak section (Suv. 682/22-23, https://www.orientalstudies.ru/eng/images/pdf/WMO_11_1_2020_03_zieme.pdf); a new fragment from Tuyuk is held by Academia Turfanica (https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/90016392.pdf); and an exceptionally late Suzhou manuscript is dated 1687 (Kasai, https://repository.crossasia.org/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/crossasia_derivate_00000640/Crossasia_Transactions%20Xinjiang_Yukio%20Kasai.pdf).

4. Trilingual print culture of Tangut Khara-Khoto: the only surviving tripartite parallel edition - Avalokiteshvara and Ushnishavijaya dharani-sutras with Emperor Renzong's own postscript - was printed in Tangut, Chinese and Tibetan in 1149; identified items: Chinese block prints TK-164/TK-165, identical Tangut book Tang. 109 (inv. 6796/6821, colophon date "first year of Heavenly Prosperity... 1149"), Tibetan butterfly-bound prints Kh. Tib. 63/64/67/68, plus Stein fragment Or. 8212/1914 (Hamanaka-Sizova 2020, https://journals.eco-vector.com/2410-0145/article/view/48894). Modern scholarship on these texts uses interlinear presentation: Lin Ying-chin 2011 made "their collation and interlinear translation into Chinese" of the Tangut and Chinese postscripts.

5. Khara-Khoto corpus basics verified: Kozlov found the site in 1908-1909, shipping to St Petersburg more than 2,000 documents and about 300 Buddhist paintings; the finds included the Tangut-Chinese bilingual glossary Fan-Han Heshi Zhangzhong Zhu ("Pearl in the Palm") and homophone dictionaries that unlocked the script decreed official in 1036 under Li Yuanhao; translated classics found there include the Analects, Classic of Filial Piety and Sunzi (https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/rarebook/08/index.html.en; Russian side catalogues: Menshikov 1984 for the Chinese part of the Kozlov fund; Kychanov 1999 Kyoto catalogue, both cited in https://journals.eco-vector.com/2410-0145/article/view/48894). Tangut translations of Chinese classics were studied early by Kolokolov & Kychanov 1966 (Lun yu, Men dzy, Xiao tzyn; referenced in https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-the-royal-asiatic-society/article/abs/chinese-primers-along-the-northwestern-frontier/F9E317D738FEB6808564AF2F96E37B61), and Galambos 2015 devotes a monograph (open access) to Tangut translations of Chinese texts including a chapter on Chinese primers in Tangut education (https://doi.org/10.1515/9783110453959). A further Tangut pedagogical item, Nie Hongyin's "Unique Tangut Primary Reader Brief Collection by Taizong", is kept at IOM RAS (WMO 6.1 contents, 2020).

6. Colophon/donor culture of Uighur scribes is institutionalized as a research field: the CERES project "Uyghur Donors and Old Uyghur Texts" (Yukiyo Kasai, within ERC BuddhistRoad) states that donations "are sometimes recorded as a part of the Old Uighur Buddhist texts", i.e., in colophons appended at the end of texts carrying donor information (https://ceres.rub.de/en/research/projects/uyghur_donors/). Adjacent inscriptional evidence: Moriyasu 2000 on Uighur Buddhist stake inscriptions from Turfan (doi:10.1484/m.srs-eb.4.00100) and Ma 2021 on paired Old Uighur-Chinese wall inscriptions in newly excavated Tuyuq Cave 26 (doi:10.1556/062.2021.00011). Zieme's WMO articles also document colophons/provenance notes such as the old signature T I alpha tying SI 1748 to Kocho ruin alpha.

7. Mongolian adjacent evidence ('Phags-pa): the square script, created 1269 by 'Phags-pa for Kublai Khan, became the Yuan official script for edicts, seals, passes, currency and stelae, and after the dynasty "it was mainly used as a phonetic gloss for Mongolians learning Chinese characters"; key textual witnesses are the rhyme dictionary Menggu ziyun and the primer Baijiaxing in Phags-pa transcription (http://www.chinaknowledge.de/Literature/Script/phagspascript.html; https://www.babelstone.co.uk/Phags-pa). The Juyongguan Cloud Platform (1345) bears the same imperial inscription in six scripts - among them Old Uighur and Western Xia/Tangut - showing the scripts remained legible together a century after the Tangut state fell (https://dsr.nii.ac.jp/rarebook/08/index.html.en). For Khara-Khoto itself, the 1983-84 Inner Mongolian excavations yielded about 3,000 Yuan-period fragments chiefly in Chinese and Tangut, now also studied for their Mongolian pieces (Galambos 2024, BSOAS, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/redeeming-the-orphaned-souls-fragments-of-a-mongolian-translation-from-kharakhoto/564C2583FA201989B1243CBA7E5D05F9).

8. Modern digital infrastructure for this material: IDP (founded 1994, website 1998, 450,000+ images by 2015) federates the British Library, IOM RAS, NLC and others (https://iranicaonline.org/articles/international-dunhuang-project/; https://idp.bl.uk); IDP Berlin has fed the Berlin Turfan collection into the IDP database (2008-2012: ~395 Syriac, ~14,000 Sanskrit, ~6,800 Tocharian fragments; Digital Turfan Archive at BBAW hosts Middle Persian/Old Turkic/Mongolian parts) (https://turfan.bbaw.de/idp-berlin-en.html); the Goettingen/Trier Dictionary of Old Uighur retro-digitized Roehrborn's Uigurisches Woerterbuch (from 2010) in the Trier Dictionary Network (https://tcdh.uni-trier.de/en/projekt/dictionary-old-uighur); the British Library Endangered Archives Programme hosts digitized Khara-Khoto Tangut materials (EAP140, https://eap.bl.uk/collection/EAP140-1?page=1); and Galambos 2015 is openly available via University Press Library Open (https://uplopen.com/search?page=2&q=Chinese).

## Remaining gaps
