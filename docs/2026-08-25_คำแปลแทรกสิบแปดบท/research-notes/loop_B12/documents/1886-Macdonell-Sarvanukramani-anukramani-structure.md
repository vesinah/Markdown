### Structure of the Sarvānukramaṇī (Macdonell's preface)

Contents of the main part, per hymn:

> "The main part of the work ... contains, for every hymn of the Rgveda in their order, according to the recension of the Sakala Sakha, the first word or words (pratika), the number of verses, the name of the poet and of the deity, as well as the metres of the whole hymn or of its parts. Occasionally, in order to make the meaning of the hymn clearer, an itihasa is briefly sketched..."

i.e., the rishi-devata-chandas triple indexing plus pratikā and verse count.

Introduction in twelve paragraphs:

> "the main part of the work is preceded by an introduction in twelve paragraphs... S1 shortly describes the scope of the work and the good resulting from a knowledge of its contents. SS 2 and 12 contain the paribhashas necessary as a key to the sutras of the main portion of the work. SS 3-11 contain a treatise on the metres of the Rigveda, which coincides almost word for word with the corresponding portion of the Vajasaneyi Anukramani."

### The five Śaunaka anukramaṇīs and dating

> "1. The Arshanukramani; 2. the Khandanukramani; 3. the Devatanukramani; 4. the Anuvakanukramani; 5. the Suktanukramani."

Dating argument (preface):

> "That anukramanis existed as a branch of literature several centuries before the beginning of our era, is shown by an unmistakeable reference to the Anuvakanukramani in the Dharma Satra of Apastamba (I, III, 11, 6). Prof. Buhler in his introduction to his translation of this sutra has furnished a tolerably convincing proof that the composition of this sutra cannot be placed later than the third century B.C., and even makes it probable that its date should be placed 150 or 200 years earlier. Thus the composition of the Anuvakanukramani would reach back to the fifth century."
>
> "...that most of them at least were anterior to the Sarvanukramani, is tolerably clear, for the latter, on Shadgurusishya's showing from the quotations which he adduces, is based on them, and, as embodying the substance of all of them, is for the sake of brevity composed in sutras. ... that this task should have been undertaken by Katyayana, himself the author of a Pratisakhya and of a Srauta Stra, is highly probable. The date of the Sarvanukramani would thus be about the middle of the fourth century B.C."

Bonus codicological detail (preface):

> "Of this anukramani only one MS. exists in Europe. It belongs to the India Office Library, and is written on palm leaves in the Grantha character. Though not dated, it is apparently of the seventeenth century. It is described in Burnell's small Sanskrit Catalogue..." [concerning the Pada-anukramaṇī / Padavidhāna attributed to Śaunaka]

## Part 2 — A. A. Macdonell, A History of Sanskrit Literature (1900), "The Pada Text" pp. 51-53

- Scan: https://archive.org/details/historyofsanskri00macdrich (djvu.txt 994 KB, downloaded this loop).

Classic statement of the pada-pāṭha's nature and date-relation to the Saṃhitā:

> "The first step taken in this direction [fixing the text] was the constitution of the Pada, or 'word' text, which being an analysis of the Samhita, gives each separate word in its independent form, and thus to a considerable extent restores the Samhita text to an older stage. That the Pada text was not quite contemporaneous in origin with the other is shown by its containing some undoubted misinterpretations and misunderstandings. Its composition can, however, only be separated by a short interval from that of the Samhita, for it appears to have been known to the writer of the Aitareya Aranyaka, while its author, Sakalya, is older than both Yaska, who quotes him, and Shaunaka, composer of the Rigveda Praticakhya, which is based on the Pada text."

Canonical function of the six unanalysed verses:

> "There are six verses in the Rigveda not analysed in the Pada text, but only given there over again in the Samhita form. This shows that Cakalya did not acknowledge them as truly Rigvedic, a view justified by internal evidence. This group of six, which is doubtless exhaustive, stands midway between old additions which Cakalya recognised as canonical, and the new appendages called Khilas, which never gained admission into the Pada text in any form."

Recitation layers after pada:

> "A further measure ... was soon taken in the form of the Krama-patha, or 'step-text.' This is old, for it, like the Pada-patha, is already known to the author of the Aitareya Aranyaka. ... The Jata-patha, or 'woven-text,' in its turn based on the Krama-patha ... The climax of complication is reached in the Ghana-patha..."
>
> "The Praticakhyas may also be regarded as safeguards of the text, having been composed for the purpose of exhibiting exactly all the changes necessary for turning the Pada into the Samhita text."
>
> "Finally, the class of supplementary works called Anukramanis, or 'Indices,' aimed at preserving the Rigveda intact by registering its contents from various points of view, besides furnishing calculations of the number of hymns, verses, words, and even syllables, contained in the sacred book."

Schools note (same chapter): five RV śākhās in the Charana-vyūha (Śākala, Vāṣkala, Āśvalāyana, Śāṅkhāyana, Māṇḍūkeya); only Śākalas and Vāṣkalas represent true recensional differences (Vālakhilya hymns).
