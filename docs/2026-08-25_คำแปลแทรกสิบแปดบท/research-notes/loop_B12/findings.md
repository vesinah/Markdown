## Key findings (with URLs)

### Gap 1 — Padapāṭha fine-dating debate (CLOSED)

Positions now documented from primary accessible texts:

1. **Traditional attribution chain (Müller 1869 preface):** Śākalya is "the recognized founder of the discipline for the Śākalas"; Yāska already knows him as padakāra of the RV and criticizes his word-splitting of *va yaḥ* (RV X.29) in Nirukta 6.28; Nirukta 1.17 (*saṃhitā padaprakṛtikā*) quotes RVPr sūtra 405 verbatim; at RVPr sūtra 485 Śākalya is called *sthavira* ("the Elder"); his accent/phonetic rulings were transmitted teacher-to-pupil until compiled by Śaunaka. Müller concludes the Pratiśākhya's agreement with the best manuscripts proves we possess the RV text as it lay before its author "more than 2000 years ago". https://archive.org/details/india.history.resource.71009
2. **Classic philological position (Macdonell 1900):** the pada-pāṭha is an analysis of the Saṃhitā that "to a considerable extent restores the Saṃhitā text to an older stage", yet contains "undoubted misinterpretations" proving it is not contemporaneous; its composition can be "separated by a short interval" from the Saṃhitā because the Aitareya-Āraṇyaka already knows both pada and krama; its author Śākalya is older than Yāska (who quotes him) and than Śaunaka, whose RV-Prātiśākhya is based on the pada text; the six verses given unanalysed show Śākalya did not acknowledge them as truly Rigvedic (vs khila appendages). https://archive.org/details/historyofsanskri00macdrich
3. **Oldenberg's opposite ordering (reported by Bronkhorst):** Oldenberg (Rigveda-Prolegomena, 1888, p. 386) thought the final redactors PRECEDED the padapāṭha — "Since he gives no real arguments, we can ignore his opinion," says Bronkhorst (GM, App. IV, p. 341 n. 11). Oldenberg later changed part of his view (1907: 834-35), which Bronkhorst calls a weak argument.
4. **Bronkhorst's radical thesis (GM ch. III.2, pp. 193-194 + Appendix IV, pp. 337-342):** Pāṇini's rules P. 1.1.16-18 refer to Śākalya's Padapāṭha; the padapāṭha was added to the hymn collection as a whole (excepting six verses, Kashikar 1951: 44) and presupposes it, so Pāṇini knew the COLLECTED Ṛgveda; further, "It is not impossible that the creation of a Padapāṭha differentiated the Taittirīya Saṃhitā from the Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa and Āraṇyaka, just as the Ṛgveda may conceivably have been collected by the author of its Padapāṭha (Bronkhorst, 1982a: 187)" — i.e., pada-analysis possibly CONSTITUTIVE of the canon, not merely a late analysis. Against Mahābhāṣya (on P. 1.4.84: "śakalena sukṛtaṃ saṃhitaṃ anuniśamya devaḥ pravavarṣa") he argues Śākalya was NOT the final redactor: maker of padapāṭha ≠ final redactors, who did not treat the padapāṭha as authoritative; Aitareya-Āraṇyaka 3.2.6 sandhi-doubt rules show uncertainty alive after Śākalya; six verses without padapāṭha (RV 7.59.12; 10.20.1; 121.10; 190.1-2-3) show redactors deviating from him. Also: Patañjali "has no respect" for padakāras ("na lakṣaṇena padakārā anuvartanīyāḥ...", MBh vol. II p. 85); Yāska likewise disagrees with Śākalya (Nir. 6.28). Criticism registered: Cardona 1999: 238 against Bronkhorst's Tripādī argument; Deshpande 1997: 81 doubts Pāṇini knew exactly our Śākala recension (intervocalic l / śakalization, cf. Bronkhorst GM p. 340). https://archive.org/details/bronkhorst-johannes-2013-2007.-greater-magadha-studies-in-the-culture-of-early-india
5. **Witzel's anchoring (canon.pdf, loop 20, re-used):** Vidagdha Śākalya authorship tradition; pada-reciters (padaka Brahmans) already in the Pāli canon (DN 1.88 etc.) before c. 250 BC; fixed canon recognized by Pāṇini c. 400 BC. http://web.archive.org/web/20220128055355/https://www.people.fas.harvard.edu/~witzel/canon.pdf
6. **Oral-layer position (Farmer-Sproat-Witzel 2004):** the sophisticated pada-style segmentation is cited as evidence that analytic textual control did NOT require writing — explicit parallel drawn to "the resistance of Vedic priests to the literate encoding of their ritual traditions". http://web.archive.org/web/20070611142813/http://www.ejvs.laurasianacademy.com/ejvs1102/ejvs1102article.pdf
7. **Modern synthesis (Colas 2024, OA):** editor-compilers codified and phonically adapted the collection in the eighth or sixth century BCE; from the eighth century BCE onwards they elaborated a succession of pāṭhas; eleven modes total; three fundamental prakṛtis — saṃhitā-pāṭha, pada-pāṭha "(said to be due to Śākalya, perhaps 6th century BCE)", krama-pāṭha (attributed to Bābhravya) — plus eight vikṛti derivatives (jaṭā etc.); function = consolidating oral invariance "better than writing would have done". OAPEN 20.500.12657/94302.

Net picture for the book: tradition + Müller + Macdonell place Śākalya's padapāṭha shortly after the Saṃhitā fixation (late-brāhmaṇa period, pre-Yāska); Witzel anchors it between the recognized canon (c. 400 BC, Pāṇini) and Pali padakas (pre-250 BC); Bronkhorst pushes Śākalya BEFORE the final redaction and makes pada-analysis potentially constitutive; Oldenberg ordered redactors before pada; Colas synthesizes with an 8th-6th-c. BCE window.

### Gap 3 — Ṭīkā/pothi commentary layout conventions (PARTIALLY CLOSED)

1. **Separate-volume convention is well attested in the catalogues harvested:** commentaries circulate as independent palm-leaf volumes — e.g., Burnell item XXIII Tribhāṣyaratna (= commentary on the Taittirīya-prātiśākhya) on 153 talipat leaves in 2 prapāṭhakas; GOML lists Sāyaṇa's commentaries (RV, Aitareya-Brāhmaṇa, Taittirīya) as separate volumes; Whish's RV pada volumes are text-only units bound per-aṣṭaka group.
2. **Pothi visual-organization conventions (Bhattarai 2020, OA monograph):** standard North Indian/Nepalese pothi anatomy 800-1300 CE — one/two string-holes per folio, foliation in margins verso, divider symbols and blank spaces marking sections; West Indian (Jaina) pothis divide EACH folio into two vertically-ruled text-sections; corpus explicitly includes root-text AND commentary manuscripts (Tattvasaṃgrahapañjikāvṛtti MS dated 1143 CE; Jātarūpa's Amarakośa commentary studied via Pant 2000); multi-text manuscripts bundle related works. OAPEN 20.500.12657/23234.
3. **South Indian maintenance practice (Colas 2023, OA):** palm-leaf fragility required a standing copying/restoration workshop; copies produced by reading aloud and re-checked; scholars consulted bundles with library staff unfastening and retying the palm-leaf bunches (Sarasvatī Library, Chidambaram, 12th-13th c.). OAPEN 20.500.12657/91071.
4. NOT verified this loop: an explicit published statement of the interleaved-blank-folio convention (commentary copied onto alternating blank versos/interleaved folios of the SAME pothi) — "(ยังไม่พบข้อมูลยืนยัน)". Wilden 2025 "Writing a Commentary on Palm-Leaf" (DOI 10.1515/9783111591025-022) is paywalled and likely treats exactly this; flagged for a future loop.

### Gap 5 — Sarvānukramaṇī structure (CLOSED)

From Macdonell's own 1886 edition (preface, pp. vii-xi of scan):
- Main part: for EVERY hymn of the RV "according to the recension of the Śākala Śākhā": the pratikā (first word/words), number of verses, name of the poet (rishi) and of the deity (devatā), and the metres (chandas) of the whole hymn or of its parts; occasional itihāsa sketches; paribhāṣās inserted.
- Preceded by an introduction in twelve paragraphs: §1 scope and merit; §§2 and 12 paribhāṣās; §§3-11 a treatise on RV metres coinciding almost word-for-word with the Vājasaneyi-anukramaṇī.
- Tradition (via Śādguruśiṣya's Vedārthadīpikā): Sarvānukramaṇī by Kātyāyana, condensing five earlier metrical anukramaṇīs of Śaunaka (Arṣa-, Khaṇḍa-, Devatā-, Anuvāka-, Sūkta-anukramaṇī); date argued ~mid-4th century BCE; Āpastamba-Dharmasūtra already cites the Anuvākānukramaṇī (Bühler: composition <=3rd c. BCE, probably 150-200 years earlier) -> anukramaṇī literature reaches back to the 5th century BCE.
- Bonus codicology: the sole European MS of the Padanukramaṇī is "written on palm leaves in the Grantha character... apparently of the seventeenth century" (India Office; described in Burnell's catalogue). https://archive.org/details/katyayanassarvan00katy
