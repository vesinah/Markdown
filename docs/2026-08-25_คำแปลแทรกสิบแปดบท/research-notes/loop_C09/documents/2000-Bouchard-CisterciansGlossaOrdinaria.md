## Verbatim quantitative layout statements (the reason this article was harvested)

1. Page anatomy, twelfth century (p. 185):
"By the early decades of the twelfth century, more systematic scribes were creating organized, even tidy series of commentaries - in effect, a kind of twelfth-century hypertext - carefully arranged around the Bible text itself, which was situated in the middle of the page and written in large letters. The gloss was written in letters only about half the height of the Bible text, arranged in columns (sometimes multiple columns) on either side of the text, and often between the lines as well. A quote of a few words of the Bible text made it clear to what specific sentence a particular marginal gloss referred, while the interlinear glosses, generally no longer than a phrase, were written directly over the words to which they referred."

2. Commentary-to-text bulk ratio (p. 185):
"Because an entire glossed Bible, in which the commentary might easily be twice as long as the text of the Bible itself, would have been far too bulky for a single volume, most glossa manuscripts contain only one or a few books of the Bible."

3. Parchment economics (Cistercian sheep flocks, p. 188):
"The answer, of course, is their need for parchment: a good-sized Bible requires some 300 sheep-skins, and a full collection of glossed books of the Bible, being at least twice as large, would require at least twice as many."

4. Survival dating and the English tradition:
- Oldest extant glossa manuscripts "date from around 1140"; earliest surviving copies were preserved at a Cistercian house (article's argument section).
- The glossed Gospels that Thomas Becket brought back in 1170 from exile at Pontigny became "the basis of glossed Gospel production in England"; produced at Pontigny, large, carefully ruled, very neatly written, gold-decorated, with an even more extensive marginal commentary than the c.-1140 exemplars.

## Other usable points

- Thesis: glossed books originated in the schools (Laon, St-Victor) but much of their development and dissemination was due to Cistercian monks; monastic vs scholastic strands less distinct than the older paradigm assumed.
- Example manuscript cited for early gloss copying outside Laon: Orleans, Mediatheque municipale MS 82 (scribe Rahingus, monk of Flavigny, colophon naming the house's three saints).
- Compiling printer of the Biblia cum Glossa (late fifteenth century) drew on different twelfth-century glossed biblical books rather than a single archetype.
