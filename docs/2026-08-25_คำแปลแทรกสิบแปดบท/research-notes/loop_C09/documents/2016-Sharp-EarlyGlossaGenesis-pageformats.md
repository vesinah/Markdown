## Key content for the layout/format question

1. Terminology anchored to Lesley Smith's Layout chapter - verbatim from Sharp's note 12:
"I have chosen Lesley Smith's system (simple, transitional, advanced) for describing these formats over the terms offered by Christopher de Hamel, who first described the development of the Gloss format" - with citations to Lesley Smith, The Glossa Ordinaria: The Making of a Medieval Bible Commentary (Leiden, 2009), 94-114, and C. F. R. de Hamel, Glossed Books of the Bible.
   => This pins Smith's three-format typology to pp. 94-114, i.e. inside the Layout chapter (91-139), giving the book a precise page anchor into the gated chapter.

2. Why the "simple format" failed for Genesis:
Ruling fixed outer columns restricted gloss space while leaving blank parchment beside lightly-glossed passages; "The inefficiency of this method is likely why the so-called simple format is never seen for a complete manuscript of the Glossa on Genesis, either primitiva or reformata".

3. Transitional format mechanics (per de Hamel, Glossed Books, 16-17):
Multiple prickings along top and bottom margins; scribes chose which pricking holes to use as column boundaries based on required space - i.e., column widths were decided page-by-page. Sharp adds that a crucial conceptual step was seeing each page as an individual unit of text+gloss rather than two texts copied side by side (citing also Smith 115-116 and 114).

4. Paris, BnF lat. 14398 (primitiva witness):
- First folios copied in a version of the transitional format.
- Unusual feature: biblical text interrupted three times by ALL-GLOSS two-column folios carrying only commentary (part of original quires, planned in advance).
- First section actually pricked for TWO columns with a single central division; three-column pages made by letting the text occupy one outer column space; scribes altered proportions of writing space more by varying HEIGHT than width ("Altering the proportions of the three columns ... made it possible to include far more commentary than would have been possible in the simple format").
- At fol. 22v / Genesis 3:20 the format changes to fixed three columns with non-varying widths, matching the change point in lat. 64.

5. Paris, BnF lat. 64 (110 folios, 250 x 165 mm):
- Opens in textus intercisus format (text and gloss in one, then two columns); biblical text in a larger hand on every other ruled line - which "had the added benefit of leaving some room for interlinear glosses"; commentary in smaller hand on every line.
- Fol. 23r mostly blank (gloss ran out mid-page); fol. 23v scribe re-divides the page in ink from two to THREE columns over visible dry-point predecessors; manuscript continues in the simple three-column gloss format thereafter.
- Sharp argues the format switches reflect the history of the TEXT (creation/fall commentary bulk), not sloppy planning.

## Usable synthesis line for the book

Twelfth-century scribes moved from fixed-width simple layouts to per-page variable allocation of parchment between text and gloss, driven by the sheer bulk of Genesis-commentary glossing; the technical vocabulary for this (simple / transitional / advanced) is Smith's, first systematized in her Layout chapter pp. 94-114, building on de Hamel 1984 pp. 16-17.

## Caveats

- Article studies Genesis witnesses only; do not generalize its all-gloss-folio observations to whole-Bible glossed books without qualification.
- Sharp follows Smalley-attribution debates via her own earlier work ("Gilbertus..." note 7); not relevant to layout data.
