## Full abstract (verbatim from Crossref)

"This article studies paratextual scribal features from the oldest leporello manuscripts of three Thai Buddhist poems composed for and by the royal court of Ayutthaya: the Pali-Siamese Nanthopananthasut kham luang and Mahachat kham luang along with the Sanskrit-Siamese Supritithammarachachadok kham luang. The manuscripts date from the early eighteenth to early nineteenth centuries and are each composed in a special bilingual format known as the Indic-vernacular bitext. Two kinds of compositional modes govern these works: exposition, where phrases in Indic are followed by their rendering in Siamese, and exegesis, where a distinct unit of Indic, often a stanza of verse, is cited in full before being subject to a complex form of analysis. The scribal features we focus on - color, script, spacing, and punctuation, along with markers for verse citation, section division, and musical notation - form a unique constellation among all known bitextual manuscripts in Southeast Asia. We argue that these scribal features are crafted to accentuate the differences not only between Indic and vernacular passages but also those between exposition and exegesis, thereby revealing the inner workings of each composition."

## Notes for the book chapter

1. The two-mode vocabulary (exposition vs exegesis) refines Walker's JAOS 2020 four presentation modes (philological/exegetical/homiletic/poetic): here "exposition" corresponds to interphrasal glossing; "exegesis" to citation-then-analysis blocks. Cite both articles together.
2. The three named manuscripts anchor concrete Ayutthayan witnesses:
   - Nanthopananthasut kham luang (Pali-Siamese) — same text family as the Nandopanandasutta kham luang studied by Assanee Poolrak's 2012 MA thesis (see companion document) and by Tossaphon Sripum's own SSRN 2023 paper on the Nandopananda story.
   - Mahachat kham luang (Pali-Siamese) — the 1482 Mahajati gam hlvang lineage documented in JAOS 2020.
   - Supritithammarachachadok kham luang (Sanskrit-Siamese) — royal chronicle-verse bitext; new witness name for our manuscript inventory.
3. Dating claim usable in draft: extant leporello copies range early 18th c. to early 19th c.
4. Scribal-feature inventory (color, script, spacing, punctuation, verse-citation markers, section-division markers, musical notation) is claimed as a constellation unique among known SEA bitextual manuscripts — supports the book's argument about Siamese glossing technology as a developed local system.
