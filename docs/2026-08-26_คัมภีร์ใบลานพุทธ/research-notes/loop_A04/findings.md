## S03 - bl_kammavaca_burmese.html
Source URL (og:url in file): https://blogs.bl.uk/asian-and-african/2017/02/kammavaca-burmese-buddhist-ordination-manuscripts.html ; dated 17 February 2017.

- Support materials for ornamental Burmese pothi: "Kammavaca manuscripts are written on a variety of materials, primarily on palm leaf but also on stiffened cloth, or gold, silver, metal or ivory sheets in the shape of palm leaf."
- Distinct display script: text "written in black lacquer in ornate Burmese characters known as 'tamarind-seed' script, also refered to as 'square' script".
- Leaf dimensions (Burmese kammavaca range): leaves "range in size from 50 to 60 cm in length, and from 10 to 15 cm in width."
- Perforation and binding: "The leaves have two holes in them in which small bamboo sticks are usually inserted in order to hold the leaves together, and the leaves are bound between decorated binding boards."
- Boards: text leaves of Or.12010A stored "between a pair of bevelled binding boards, red on the inside, and lacquered and gilt on the outside"; a donor inscription is painted on the inside of the top board (gift of U Tha Hsan and wife Ma Lun).
- Outer-leaf decoration: "The outer sides of the first and final leaves of the Kammavaca are usually decorated with panels of birds, lotus, flower and leaf designs, devas, figures of the Buddha and geometric patterns."
- Post-binding enclosure: "Kammavaca were usually wrapped in woven or silk wrappers, and secured with a woven ribbon and placed in a gilded box."

## S04 - bl_palmleaf_part1_central_thailand.html
Source URL (canonical in file): https://blogs.bl.uk/asian-and-african/2014/11/the-beauty-of-palm-leaf-manuscripts-1-central-thailand.html ; dated 20 November 2014.

- Leaf preparation and durability (central Thailand): leaves "first boiled and then dried and sometimes smoked or baked in a kiln"; they "can last for as long as 600 years even in the humid, tropical climate".
- Line count per leaf: "Each leaf contains between 3 to 5 lines of writing" (central Thailand; occasional miniatures or gilt ornaments).
- Bundle unit and cord practice: "A bundle of leaves, called ph?k [phuk] in Thai, was usually fastened together with two cotton cords, which were threaded through two holes pierced through the leaves of the entire manuscript."
- Title indicators: "Title indicators made from wood, bamboo or ivory, with the title or a very brief description of the contents of the manuscript, were sometimes tied to a palm-leaf manuscript in order to identify the text(s) contained within."
- Covers: "Valuable manuscripts or important Buddhist works were protected from physical damage with wooden boards, which could be beautifully carved, gilded, lacquered, or decorated with mother-of-pearl inlay."
- Enclosure options: wrapped "in cotton or silk cloth", or kept "in custom-made gilt and lacquered wooden cases to protect them from damage by rodents, insects or water."
- Worked example BL Or 5107 (royal edition of Samantapasadicatthakathayojana): fifteen bundles; covers "lacquered black, with patterns of leaves and flowers in mother-of-pearl inlay"; stored in a "red silk and cotton wrapper of Indian origin with a gold thread pattern, made to order for the Thai royal court."

## S05 - bl_palmleaf_part2_northern_lao_shan.html
Source URL (canonical in file): https://blogs.bl.uk/asian-and-african/2015/01/the-beauty-of-palm-leaf-manuscripts-2-northern-thai-lao-and-shan-traditions.html ; January 2015.

- Raw material (Tai traditions): large fan-shaped leaves of the lan palm (Corypha), "cut into a long rectangular shape, soaked in a herbal mixture, then dried or baked in a kiln, and finally pressed."
- Inscription technique: inscribed with a sharp wooden or metal stylus, then wiped with a mixture of resin and/or oil and carbon soot.
- Dating profile: "Most of the extant palm leaf manuscripts from the Tai traditions were produced during the 18th and 19th centuries, but some date back to the early 16th century." In Shan tradition palm leaf was largely replaced by bound or folded paper books (citing Terwiel 2003).
- Cover structure: "Precious manuscripts or palm leaves containing important texts were covered with two wooden or bamboo boards"; boards left plain or "beautifully carved or decorated"; covers "could be lacquered in red or black, and decorated with gold leaf, mirror glass, mother-of-pearl inlay or even with crystals or precious stones."
- Regional cover styles: Shan raised gilt-lacquer covers with mirror glass (Or.16114); "Bamboo strips cut to match the size of the palm leaves were popular covers for manuscripts in Lanna, Laos and among the Shan" (Or.16790); incised black-and-red lacquer boards - "This technique may have been imported into Lanna and Laos from the Burmese and Shan traditions" (Or.13157, dated 1918).
- Mother-of-pearl inlay geography: "very popular in central Thailand, but it was also adopted in Lanna and Laos"; in Thailand used exclusively for ecclesiastical objects under royal patronage until end of the 19th century.
- Wrappers: manuscripts "often wrapped in a piece of cloth", including an unused lady's skirt, hand-woven shawl, or imported printed Indian cotton; custom wrappers of local or imported silk, "occasionally ... interwoven with bamboo strips to provide extra stability for palm leaf manuscripts which had no covers"; also long cotton or silk bags sewn to match.

## S06 - bl_palmleaf_part3_storage_preservation.html
Source URL (canonical in file): https://blogs.bl.uk/asian-and-african/2015/02/the-beauty-of-palm-leaf-manuscripts-3-storage-and-preservation.html ; dated 06 February 2015.

- Retrieval problem: "the title or contents were rarely mentioned on the front leaf or front cover of a manuscript, quick retrieval of a particular manuscript was only possible if certain finding aids and methods were in place." Manuscripts arranged in systematic order within one cabinet; cabinets ordered within the library building.
- Title indicator mechanics: "A title indicator ... was attached to a rope, and the rope was wound around the manuscript"; indicator length "could range from 100 to 400 mm"; wood/bamboo strips with title and contents list incised or written; some carved ivory examples.
- Furniture hierarchy: most mss had no own case and were "stored in a larger casket, either a chest with a lid or a cabinet with lockable doors. Thick layers of lacquer helped to prevent damage to the chests and cabinets by the humid climate and insects. Long legs on the cabinets, and pedestals to stand the chests on, served to protect the manuscripts from flooding." (Lanna/Shan chest F1060.)
- Custom cases: "Wooden manuscript cases that were custom-made for a particular palm leaf manuscript were often decorated in the same technique and design as the manuscript covers" (case + Kammavaca, Or.16893).
- Racks: "Manuscript racks were used to hold manuscripts that had been selected to be worked with, for example to be read or discussed during Buddhist ceremonies" (rack photo from Pakse, Champassak Province, Laos; PLMP / National Library of Laos).
- Ritual maintenance cycle: at merit ceremonies (annually or every several years) manuscripts are removed from storage furniture and "cleaned from dust, dead insects and dry mould by wiping them with a clean, soft piece of cotton or a brush made from soft animal hair on a bamboo stick", done by lay volunteers and novice monks.
- Repair economy: "Damaged manuscripts can be repaired or copied onto new palm leaves by trained monks and novices, and broken covers, title indicators or torn wrappers can be replaced with new ones," followed by blessing of manuscripts and community.

## S07 - buhler_indian_paleography_1904_djvu.txt
Source: Buehler, Indian Paleography, Indian Antiquary Vol. XXXII-XXXIII appendix, 1904 (full djvu text held locally). Section numbers per text.

- Canonical pothi perforation rule (sec. 37 D/E): "All palm-leaf MSS. are pierced either with one hole, usually in the middle, more rarely, in specimens from Kashgar, on the left, or with two holes on the left and the right, through which strings (su?ra [sutra] or garayantraka) are passed in order to keep the leaves together."
- Format transfer to other supports: Bower MS birch-bark leaves "cut according to the size of palm-leaves, and, like these, are pierced in the middle in order to pass a string through, intended to hold them together"; copper plates copied from palm-leaf models were made "narrow and long", while birch-bark models gave broader, almost square plates; plate rings "correspond to the threads which keep the palm-leaves together"; single ring usually in Southern India sasanas with the hole on the left side.
- Blank protective pages (copper-plate analogue): "the first side of the first plate, as well as the second side of the last, is left blank."
- Plants: tada/Borassus flabelliformis and tali/Corypha umbraculifera or C. taliera; "The earliest witness for the general use of [palm leaf] throughout the whole of India is Hiuen Tsiang (7th century)"; oldest certainly dated palm-leaf MS "goes back to the 6th century"; Godfrey Collection fragments from Kashgar at least 4th century.
- Preparation (after Rajendralal Mitra): leaves "next boiled or soaked in water, then again dried, and finally polished with stones or [shells] and cut to the proper size."
- Incision vs ink: in Dravidian districts and Orissa letters written "with a stilus and afterwards blackened with soot or charcoal"; oldest MS found in Southern India dates (per Burnell) from A.D. 1428; raw palm-leaves used for letters, documents and schools in Southern India and Bengal.
- Line ruling (sec. on writing): lines of MSS "always very regular, even in the oldest specimens ... probably have been made with the help of a ruler"; "In the ancient palm-leaf MSS. and in many later ones on paper, the ends of the lines are marked by vertical double strokes, running across the whole breadth of the leaves."
- Pagination/foliation numerals (sec. 34): smaller number-symbols placed "vertically below, the higher ones" in "the pagination of all manuscripts"; letter-numerals "usually only found in Jaina palm-leaf MSS. up to about A.D. 1450."
- Burmese varnished-board MSS noted as common in Burma; British Museum holds two Burmese ivory-sheet specimens and "MSS. on gilt and silver plated palm-leaves."

## S09 - npj_heritage_science_2025_engraved_palm_leaf_restoration.html
Source URL (canonical in file): https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01943-0 ; JSON-LD: npj Heritage Science vol 13, issue 1, published 2025-07-30; authors Yan Li et al. (14 names).

- Transmission datum: "Around the 7th century AD, PLMs were introduced to the Yunnan region of China via Myanmar and Thailand following the southern transmission of Theravada Buddhism from Sri Lanka."
- Material basis: Xishuangbanna's tropical climate suits talipot palm cultivation; region preserves the creation/use skills; Dai PLM corpus known as the "Encyclopedia of Dai Culture."
- Damage taxonomy: "Common types of damage include insect damage, stains, missing fragments, fraying fibers, tearing, and hole wear."
- Restoration findings: "Wiping with warm water was conducive to the removal of surface contamination, while the use of inlay techniques and a composite restoration paste effectively repaired the damaged areas and enhanced the structural stability of the manuscript."

## S10 - shway_yoe_the_burman_djvu.txt
Source: Shway Yoe, The Burman: His Life and Notions, Macmillan and Co., London (archive.org cu31924023243938, full text).

- Terminology: "pe in its simple form may mean (i) the palmyra palm or leaf, whence pe-sa, a palm-leaf book."
- Sadaik definition and placement in monastery: "The sadaik, the carved and gilded box containing the palm-leaf books, stands in the central room near the images, and often shaded by a sacred umbrella." Also listed among objects "Close by" the central image: "the manuscript chests, sadaiks".
- Professional copyists: monks never write themselves; "professional writers who are copying out manuscripts, carefully scratching on the palm-leaves with sharp steel style... The writers are all professionals, laymen, and almost without exception from Upper Burma."
- Writing speed and finishing: "the most practised manipulator cannot, with his agate style and light strip of palmyra-leaf, exceed the speed of an English boy who has just got over the preliminary difficulties"; "The letters scratched on the leaf are made more distinct by the application of crude earth-oil. This serves to darken the writing, as well as to preserve the book."
- Thiho Taik library, Mandalay (model monastic collection): sadaiks "arranged in three rows, one over the other"; order - sermons and birth-stories below, "Cinghalese commentaries and exegeses" above, "on the top shelf Burmese translations"; inside each chest "every separate collection of leaves being enclosed between wooden boards, some plain, some carved in high relief, with figures of dewas and demons."
- Wrapper and ribbon system: over the boards "is wrapped a kabalwe, a square piece of cloth with inwoven scenes from the sacred books... Most of these are of silk, very often with narrow slips of bamboo worked in so as to give stiffness"; alternatively/in addition the "sa-si-gyo, a riband, about two fingers' breadth, and upwards of a yard in length", crocheted, bearing "the name of the owner, his titles and distinctions."
- Security: "All the boxes are kept carefully locked, and the door is barred at night."
- Sewn-leaf variant: horoscope specimen on "two palm-leaves ... tightly sewn together, and the writings and figures, done with a metal style on the dry leaf."
- School practice contrast (paper folding books): pupils "write out maxims and prayers on his little parabaik with a clumsy pointed steatite pencil."
