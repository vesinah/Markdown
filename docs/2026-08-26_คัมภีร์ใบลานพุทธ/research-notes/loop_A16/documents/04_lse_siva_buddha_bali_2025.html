Hierarchies of Buddhism and Hinduism? The Śiva-Buddha Cult in Contemporary Bali

0 comments | 7 shares

Estimated reading time: 10 minutes

7 Shares

Bikash K Bhattacharya explores the Śiva-Buddha cult in contemporary Bali, which is often cited as evidence of “syncretic amalgamation” of Hinduism and Buddhism in Java and Bali – but is it that simple?

A narrow, cobbled path, flanked by moss-covered walls, led us toward Pura Pegulingan. Leaving the main road, we passed a row of traditional Balinese houses. The air was heavy with the fragrance of frangipani blossoms mingling with incense from the daily Canang sari offerings.

After several minutes of walking, the path opened onto rice fields. Unsure whether we had reached a dead end, Pak Bagus—the Balinese guide accompanying me—laughed, “I’ve never heard of this temple,” before asking a farmer at work in the field. The man reassured us we were on the right track.

Soon the silhouette of a split gateway (Candi bentar) came into view. The courtyard lay silent and deserted. At its centre stood the remains of a circular stupa—an unmistakable marker of Buddhist origins—its outlines softened by moss and lichen. Alongside the stupa rose distinctly Hindu structures: meru shrines, a padmasana (lotus-throne shrine), and open-sided bale pavilions.

Figure 1. The Buddhist stupa at the centre of Pura Pegulingan, a Balinese Hindu-Buddhist temple, overlooking the famed Tirta Empul, one of Bali’s most popular tourist destinations. Photo: author.

Pura Pegulingan is largely absent from tourist maps, overshadowed by the famed Tirta Empul just below. While Tirta Empul is animated by pilgrims and tourists drawn to its sacred springs, Pegulingan feels hidden and hushed. The two temples share the same sacred landscape, yet one is vibrant and crowded, the other secluded and little known.

Figure 2. The Buddhist stupa at Kalibukbuk, a quiet Balinese temple in North Bali. Photo: author.

Pak Bagus visits Tirta Empul several times a week, yet had never heard of Pura Pegulingan. Similarly, in North Bali, Ibu Luh, a tour planner with over a decade of experience, knew almost every attraction but had little knowledge of Kalibukbuk in Buleleng—a ninth-century Buddhist stupa incorporated into a Balinese Hindu temple complex and often cited as evidence of what is called the Śiva-Buddha cult (Agama Siwa-Buda or Agama Hindu-Buda). When we visited Kalibukbuk on two occasions, the caretaker (juru kunci) was absent. His wife explained that few people visit, so he usually spends his time in Singaraja tending another temple. Even at more popular sites such as Goa Gajah and Pura Ulun Danu Bratan—Hindu temple complexes that also contain, respectively, a Buddhist shrine and a stupa—the Buddhist elements appear neglected and unattended, no longer functioning as active places of worship.

Figure 3. The Buddhist stupa outside the Ulun Danu Bratan temple complex, a popular tourist site in Bali. Photo: author.

Although often celebrated as symbols of Hindu-Buddhist “syncretism,” these structures reveal a clear hierarchy between the two faiths, with Śaivism holding ritual and doctrinal primacy, and Buddhism functioning as its complementary and subordinate counterpart. For instance, in Pura Ulun Danu Bratan the Buddhist stupa is situated in a corner outside the main temple complex, reflecting this hierarchy.

An Established Hierarchy

The conventional view holds that the Śiva-Buddha cult in Java and Bali represents a fusion of Hinduism and Buddhism. Yet recent scholarship suggests that “syncretism” is too simplistic a lens. Scholar Andrea Acri argues that, from the Majapahit period onward, the connection between Buddhism and Śaivism was less about merging beliefs and more about sharing rituals and cooperating in practice. This partnership, Acri notes drawing on Thomas M Hunter’s research on syncretism, began with the Singhasari king Kṛtanagara (r. 1268-1292), who declared himself both a form of Śiva and of the Buddha, earning the title Bhatara Siwa Buddha.

In the early nineteenth century, John Crawfurd observed:

“The Buddhists are few in number. […] The followers of Śiva spoke of those of Buddha more with contempt than with hatred or rancor—feelings, indeed, not likely to be entertained by any people toward a fallen sect, in which light the Buddhists were evidently regarded. The Brahmans in their conversation often let fall expressions showing that they entertained no respect whatever for the followers of the opposite worship. The sect of Śiva may indeed be denominated as the national religion. It is the religion of nine-tenths of the people, of every sovereign on the island, and of every man in power.”

Crawfurd’s account makes clear that Buddhism was seen as subordinate, while Śaivism dominated. This hierarchy is also reflected in Bali’s long-standing Śiva-Buddha dual clergy system, in which both priestly lineages are recognized, but the Śiva Pedanda holds higher ritual authority.

Within this system, hierarchy is embodied in the figures of Dang Hyang Nirartha and Dang Hyang Astapaka. Nirartha, the Javanese Brahmin who established the Śaiva priestly lineage in Bali in the sixteenth century, is given primacy: his descendants, the Pedanda Śiva, preside over the most prestigious ceremonies. Astapaka, by contrast, is remembered as the founder of the Buddhist priestly line, Pedanda Buddha. Although the Pedanda Buddha remain indispensable collaborators, their authority is clearly subordinate to that of the Śaiva clergy in rituals—highlighting the asymmetrical yet complementary nature of the Śiva–Buddha relationship in Balinese tradition. Buddhist priests are required participants in rituals like Ekādaśa Rudra, but their role is supportive and secondary to that of Śaiva priests. While Śaiva priests lead the main ceremonies, Buddhist priests perform chants and invoke protective deities, reflecting the historical Śiva-Buddha synthesis within a predominantly Śaiva framework.

Contemporary Uses of the Śiva-Buddha Category

Contemporary religious pluralism in Indonesia is characterized by the coexistence of diverse faiths within a national framework that recognizes six religions under the state ideology of Pancasila, which upholds belief in one supreme God (Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa). The Śiva-Buddha tradition occupies a distinctive historical and cultural position within Indonesia’s landscape of religious pluralism. Although no longer a living or organized faith, it remains a powerful symbol of pre-Islamic religious harmony, evoking a period when Hindu and Buddhist traditions coexisted and intertwined in Java and Bali. In contemporary Indonesia, the Śiva-Buddha synthesis is frequently invoked in cultural heritage discourse, art, and temple ritual—particularly in Bali—as an emblem of the nation’s enduring capacity for religious accommodation and synthesis.

I first learned about Pura Pegulingan and Kalibukbuk from Eko Widyo Prasetyo, a religious pluralist and lecturer in religion and tourism at Ciputra University in Surabaya. One morning this past August, I sat with him in the courtyard of his home, where a shrine displayed an eclectic array of figures—Buddhas, Guanyin, Ganesha, Śiva, and even Brayut.

Figure 4. A small corner of Eko Widyo Prasetyo’s home shrine in Mojokerto, Java. Photo: author.

Our conversation turned to religious pluralism in Indonesia. A committed advocate of pluralism, Prasetyo shared a revealing family history: his grandfather, among the first converts to Roman Catholicism in Yogyakarta, once stopped sponsoring traditional wayang performances, which he came to view as incompatible with his new faith. From this story, Prasetyo explained, he had drawn a different lesson—one of openness to practices across traditions, inspired by examples of syncretism in precolonial Java.

For pluralists like Prasetyo, sites such as Pura Pegulingan and Kalibukbuk serve as reminders of this ethos. They are seen as models of coexistence, valuable for contemporary Indonesia, even if historical realities—as scholars like Acri have shown—point to more complex, asymmetrical relationships. In this way, Śiva-Buddha sites are repurposed to embody modern ideals of pluralism.

Similar narratives appear in tourism. English-speaking guides, such as Pak Bagus, often present the Buddhist stupa at Pura Ulun Danu Bratan as a symbol of Śiva-Buddha syncretism, framing it as a hallmark of Balinese heritage. Yet such interpretations often smooth over the historical dominance of Śaivism, obscuring the unequal dynamics that underpinned this so-called syncretism.

The Śiva-Buddha cult in Bali thus emerges as a complex category: historically marked by hierarchy and asymmetry, yet today reimagined as a symbol of harmony and coexistence. The quiet neglect of Buddhist structures at sites such as Pegulingan or Kalibukbuk contrasts sharply with the celebratory narratives offered by tour guides and pluralist thinkers, underscoring the gap between historical reality and contemporary memory. In this way, the Śiva-Buddha category continues to function less as a record of balanced syncretism than as a flexible idiom—one that accommodates shifting needs, from legitimizing political authority in the past to promoting religious pluralism and heritage tourism in the present.
