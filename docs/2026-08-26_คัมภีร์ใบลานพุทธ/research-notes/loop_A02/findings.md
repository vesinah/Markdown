## S01. Yu et al. 2024 - npj Heritage Science 12:280 (processing chemistry / hygroscopicity)
- Status: FULL TEXT FETCHED AND READ; archived at documents/yu2024_nature_heritscience_hygroscopicity_fulltext.txt
- URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-024-01402-2
- Authors as printed: Dehao Yu, Xuanhua Li, Si'ao Sun, Hong Guo, Hao Luo, Jinmeng Zhu, Liusan Li, Shan Wang & Liuyang Han. Published: 05 August 2024.
- Title as printed: "The effect of traditional processing craft on the hygroscopicity of palm leaf manuscripts"

Verified facts with exact quotes:
1. Processing steps canon: "researchers have reviewed the processing steps for palm leaf manuscripts, which include cutting, steaming and boiling, polishing, flattening, drying, and perforating [4]."
2. Yunnan traditional craft sequence (Methods): "Talipot palm leaves (Corypha umbraculifera L.) were collected from various regions in Yunnan Province, China." Steps: "Selecting robust palm leaves and shaping them into the requisite dimensions"; "a controlled boiling process, utilizing a solution comprising fermented rice water and the extract derived from the fruit of tamarind (Tamarindus indica L.). This procedure lasts approximately 10 h."; "brush them to achieve a smooth surface texture"; "Air-drying these palm leaves until they attain a pale green hue."
3. Rationale for acidic boil: "Fermented rice water and tamarind extract were selected to provide a weakly acidic environment, aims to remove components of the leaves that are prone to decay, thus increasing the flexibility and longevity of the leaves."
4. Quantified effect of processing: "traditional processing slightly increases the equilibrium moisture content (EMC) in environments with 0 to 60% relative humidity (RH), but significantly lowers EMC in high humidity environments, with reductions up to 19.01%. Additionally, hysteresis doubled post-processing".
5. Chemistry shift: "FT-IR ... indicates that the relative contents of cellulose and hemicellulose were reduced by 39.90% and 3.97%, respectively" after processing; "the relative contents of cellulose and hemicellulose were decreased by 57.52% and 19.83% after nature aging, respectively."
6. Storage guidance: "long-term preservation of palm leaf manuscripts should avoid relative humidity above 70%."
7. Color: "traditional processing craft remove chlorophyll from the palm leaves, changing the color from the deep green of FPL to the pale green hue of SPL1."

## S02. Zhang et al. 2025 - npj Heritage Science 13:218 (RH aging, optimal storage)
- Status: FULL TEXT FETCHED AND READ; archived at documents/zhang2025_nature_heritscience_RH_aging_fulltext.txt
- URL: https://www.nature.com/articles/s40494-025-01794-9
- Authors as printed: Wenjie Zhang, Shan Wang, Liuyang Han & Hong Guo. Published: 27 May 2025.

Verified facts:
1. Species framing: "Palm Leaf Manuscripts are a unique medium for writing, made from the leaves of palm species such as Talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera L.) and palmyra palm (Borassus flabellifer L.)."
2. Composition: "Similar to paper and wood, Palm Leaf Manuscripts are cellulose-based organic materials primarily composed of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin."
3. Sample prep heritage process: samples from Yunnan; "Healthy talipot palm leaves (Corypha umbraculifera L.) underwent a series of treatments, including boiling, washing, air-drying, trimming, and flattening"; production process "recognized as a National Intangible Cultural Heritage in China."
4. Experimental design: aging chamber at constant 25 C with RH set at 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%; analyzed after 200 days using SEM, DVS, TMA, FT-IR, XRD.
5. Key result: "In dry conditions, they experience bending, cracking, reduced mechanical strength, lower hygroscopicity, and chemical degradation. In humid conditions, fungal growth compromises the manuscripts' structure." And: "Manuscripts stored at 50% RH showed no significant damage, suggesting that this level is optimal for their preservation."

## S03. Wiland et al. 2022 - Journal of the Institute of Conservation 45(3):236-259 (Part 1 review)
- Status: PDF ARCHIVED + EXTRACTED; key sections read (species, seasoning by country, media). Not every page read line-by-line.
- URL used for PDF: https://list.indology.info/pipermail/indology/attachments/20230619/a867d80b/attachment.pdf (journal DOI printed in article: https://doi.org/10.1080/19455224.2022.2115093)
- Authors as printed: Wiland, Brown, Fuller, Havelok, Johnson, Kenn, Kralka, Muzart, Pollard and Snowdon.

Verified facts:
1. Vernacular names exactly as printed: "variants include Ola in Sri Lanka, Larn in Thailand, Lontar (meaning palmyra) in Myanmar and Indonesia, and Tala, Sritala or Karalika in different parts of India."
2. Three most-used species: "three have been most widely used as writing supports: Borassus flabellifer linn (palmyra palm), Corypha umbraculifera linn (talipot palm, fan palm) and Corypha taliera Roxb."
3. Borassus profile: "The palmyra palm grows in many parts of India, Myanmar and Sri Lanka. It is native to Africa and needs a drier climate. The trees grow up to 15-20m and the leaf stalks grow to around 1m... The leaves from the palmyra palm are thicker and coarser compared to those of the talipot. The leaves also appear to be more prone to insect attack than those of the talipot variety." (Sanskrit name: Tal; Hindu: Tar.)
4. Corypha umbraculifera profile: "The talipot palm grows in the forests of Sri Lanka, Myanmar-where it is known as gebang-Thailand, Malaysia and South India. It needs a wet climate, and the trees can grow up to 20-25m... The leaves remain flexible and supple for a long period of time and are smoother than those of the palmyra palm." (Sanskrit names: Karalika, Sritalam, Tali.)
5. Corypha taliera profile: "This palm grows in Bengal and some coastal areas of Tamilnadu in India. The trees grow up to 10m tall. The leaves are thick and not very flexible, and more prone to insect attack."
6. Longevity claim: "Palm leaves treated in such ways are relatively robust and have been known to last as long as 500-600 years even in the humid, tropical climate of Southeast Asia."
7. Harvest timing: "Once harvested-usually during the dry season-palm leaves require additional processing or 'seasoning' in order to make them fit for writing".
8. Regional seasoning variants quoted:
   - South India: "Fresh palm leaves are dried in the shade and rubbed with gingili oil to make them smooth."
   - South/eastern India and Odisha: "Tender palm leaves are hung and smoked for several days (typically in a kitchen) after which any surface deposits are wiped off"; variants "(b) the palm leaves are dried in the sun and buried for 10-15 days under pond mud or silt..."; "(c) the mature palm leaves are boiled in water, making the leaves thin and soft...".
   - Sri Lanka: "Fresh palm leaves are boiled in water or limewater for a few hours and dried in the shade... They are also sometimes boiled interspersed with pineapple leaves, then washed, fumigated and burnished."
   - Myanmar: "palm leaves are boiled and once the black secretions have been removed, the leaves are weighed down to dry and burnished."
   - Bali: "Bundles of leaves are dried in the sun, then soaked in water for 3-4 days, rubbed clean in the water, dried again in the sun, cut to size and boiled for 8 hours in a herbal solution, then gently cleaned in water with a cloth, and laid out to dry in the sun... they are pressed repeatedly and finally polished with a cloth."
   - Thailand: "it is the golden leaf from the Lopburi region that is preferred. After being cut the leaves are dried in the shade, the midrib removed with a sharp blade and the leaves then cut to uniform size. Bundles of leaves are then placed in a kiln for 24 hours, causing a black oil to exude from the edges. The oil is wiped off, the leaf held over a fire for a few minutes before finally being polished with hot sand."
9. Media: "The most common media used was soot (carbon black) combined with a binder of either plant juice, resin or oil... Regional examples of media variations include soot combined with Dummala and Kakuna oils in Sri Lanka, turmeric powder, soot and citronella or gingili oil in India, and the use of red ink, indigo and vermillion in Thailand."
10. Stylus dimension: beginners start "with a blunt stylus and then progress to using a decorated and well-balanced sharp stylus usually 25-35cm long."
11. Chronology anchors: "almost no surviving manuscripts from before the tenth century, with the exception of one example preserved at Horyuji temple in Japan, dated to the sixth century"; "A fragment of text discovered in Central Asia from 2 CE is currently the oldest known and dated fragment of palm leaf in existence."; "when paper was introduced in South Asia around the tenth century, it very slowly began to replace palm leaf".

## S04. Nishanthi et al. 2025 - Proc. ICFSS 2(1):37-51 (Sri Lankan species and technique)
- Status: PDF ARCHIVED + FULLY EXTRACTED AND READ
- URL: https://www.dpublication.com/conference-proceedings/index.php/icfss/article/download/1108/845/10248 (DOI as printed: https://doi.org/10.33422/icfss.v2i1.1108)
- Authors as printed: Nishanthi, H. D. M., Wijayasundara, N. D., Abhayasundera, P. (Library, University of Sri Jayewardenepura; Dept of Anthropology).

Verified facts:
1. Three species: "Three varieties of palm leaves were used for writing in ancient Sri Lanka, including the talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera), the lonta palm (Corypha utan) and the palmyra palm (Borassus flabellifer). However, this research revealed that the talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera) and the palmyra palm (Borassus flabellifer) were most commonly used."
2. Measurements: "The Talipot palm's frond is more than 10 feet long and has 80-100 sections."; "a well-grown Talipot palm bud has a length of about 10-20 inches."
3. Local name: "the palm tree Corypha umbraculifera, which is used for manuscripts, has 205 varieties and is referred to as 'pus-kola' in Sri Lanka (as cited by De Silva, 1938)".
4. Harvest timing/ritual: "They should be cut one or two days before the full dark or Amavaka Poya day. This procedure has a strong scientific basis, and it has been shown to help extend the longevity of manuscripts and reduce insect activity." (attributed within the paper to Indika 2011).
5. Sequence as listed by the paper: "to mark the palm tree ... select the palm bud (Thala Gobaya), cutting the palm bud ..., removing the ekels, and making rolls (Vattu), the rolls boiled in a pot with medicinal leaves, boiled leaves are dried in a cool place, seasoning the palm leaves (the leaf blade pulls up and down over the smooth surface of a cylinder of wood), the manuscript pages (folios) are cut..., the edges of the pages were burnt with a hot iron..., write in palm leaf using a steel point and blackened".
6. Boiling additives: "the leaves are boiled using a mixture of natural ingredients, including unripe pineapple leaves, papaya fruit and its leaves ..., beli leaves and fruits, kappetiya, pinna, bombu leaves, paddy, pure water and firewood, usually in a copper pot." (Alahakoon 2007, 2012 as cited).
7. Drying/polishing: "they are hung out as single strips on a thread and dried under gentle sunshine for three or more days. Later, they are kept for three days exposed to the dew..."; polishing on "poles six feet above the ground", "Stones weighing about two pounds are tied to both sides of the leaf strip"; "one end of the palm leaf is weighed down using a stone called a 'padi-gala' or 'polishing stone' (Piyadasa, 1985). 'Hema-kanda' is the name of the tree used to polish the palm leaves."
8. Strip dimensions: "Leaf strips, which range from eight to thirty-two inches long and 2 to 2 3/4 inches wide, are cut to standard sizes appropriate to the subject to be written on, says Kulasuriya (1996)."
9. Styli and blackening: styli "are the sharp stylus (panhinda) and the blunt stylus (ulkatuwa)"; blackening called "kalumadeema": "Finely ground charcoal from the Gaduma tree is combined with resin or kekunu oil, and the engraved surface is cleaned with finely ground millet flour or rice bran."
10. Dating anchor inside corpus: "The Chulla Vagga, one of the oldest palm leaf manuscripts in Sri Lanka, was written in the 13th century AD (Alahakoon, 2006)." Also cites Ferguson (1888) reporting "400-500-year-old palmyra leaf manuscripts in Sri Lanka".

## S05. Meher 2009 - Orissa Review (January 2009) pp.43-46
- Status: PDF ARCHIVED VIA WAYBACK (original host cert expired) + FULLY EXTRACTED AND READ
- URL (original): https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/2009/Jan/engpdf/43-46.pdf ; archive copy retrieved via web.archive.org (id_ mode, 2024 snapshot)
- Author as printed: Ramesh Meher, "Conservator in the Manuscript Conservation Centre, P.G. Department of History, Sambalpur University, Jyoti Vihar, Burla, Sambalpur."

Verified facts:
1. Mechanical comparison: "When compared with good quality hand made paper, it is found three times stronger then the hand made paper. Palm leaf having very good tensile strength, is even better than that of good quality ledger paper. Its folding endurance is almost nil in the case of very old and dry leaves."
2. Anatomy: "The palm leaf consists of epidermis consisting of thick walled cells."
3. Leaf choice: "people have chosen the palm leaves to prepare the manuscript which are just about to open."
4. Seasoning methods (Western Orissa), exact quotes: "people hang their palm leaves in their kitchen, take them out and apply turmeric paste to them."; "leaves are dried completely under the sun and are then kept under the mud or silt of a pond for 10-15 days."; "the palm leaves are allowed to boil with paddy husk and then they are cleaned with soft cloth and kept alternately under dew and sun for a few days. They are polished and cut into the required size."
5. Pigment recipes: "Black: Lamp (carbon) black mixed with wood apple gum. Red pigment: Ground Cinnabar (iron oxide) it is locally known as Hingula. White: Burnt conch shell powder mixed with plant gum. Yellow: Prepared out of the adhesive of wood apple mixed with Turmeric powder. Blue: Ground juice of Indigo plant leaves mixed with some plant gum. Green: Ground bean leaves, mixed with plant gum."
6. Storage protection materials kept beside manuscripts: "Ghodabacha powder, Emblic myrobalan powder, Belleric myrobalan Powder, vitex negundo leaves Powder, Neem Leaves powder, Cinamon oil powder, black pepper powder, pipali powder etc".
7. Historical witnesses: "In 1442 C.E. the Persian ambassador Abdur Razzak wrote about a dafterkhana(wing or annex of documents) where a number of writers were engaged to write down accounts on palm leaf." Also Portuguese traveler Duarte Barbosa on scribal speed.

## S06. LIS Academy - "How Palm Leaves Were Prepared for Manuscripts: Traditional Methods"
- Status: FULL TEXT FETCHED AND READ; HTML archived
- URL: https://lis.academy/preservation-conservation-of-library-material/how-palm-leaves-prepared-manuscripts/
- Page states "Last updated on: January 12, 2026". Educational website (no named author on page); treat as tertiary synthesis but its cited chain includes Odisha Review 2009 and dsource.in.

Verified facts:
1. Leaf age selection: "A leaf that was too tender or too mature was rejected... Very young leaves shrank and warped on drying, while old, dried-out leaves had almost no folding strength and tore easily."
2. Burying durations: "kept under the mud or silt of a pond for ten to fifteen days... Some accounts describe leaves being buried in marshy water and kept wet for up to three months before use."
3. Kerala oil tradition: "In the Kerala tradition, prepared leaves were smeared with lemongrass oil to guard against deterioration and termites."
4. Strip sizing: "around 20 to 50 centimetres in length and only a few centimetres wide"; talipot "sometimes close to a metre in length".
5. Repellent list: "neem leaves, sweet flag, camphor, black cumin, and various plant juices."

## S07. Penn Cultures of the Book - "Palm Leaf"
- Status: FULL TEXT FETCHED AND READ; HTML archived
- URL: https://www.digitalbookhistory.com/culturesofthebook/Palm_Leaf (page last edited 3 December 2018 per page footer)

Verified facts:
1. Species contrast: "The Talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera) has broad, lengthy leaves with a thin, smooth surface... The Palmyra palm (Borassus flabellifer)... has thick and lengthy leaves that grow brittle over time despite being initially strong and flexible."
2. South Indian sequence with ages/durations: "5-6 month old palm leaves are first collected, edge ribs removed, and then left to dry in the shade... boiled in water or milk to soften the fiber... left to dry again, buried in wet sand for up to 3 months, applied with gingelly oil to improve softness, then burnished a final time."
3. Writing method: "a metallic stylus is used to etch letters into the upper cuticle of the leaf"; visibility paste: "lampblack... or turmeric mixed with aromatic oils"; oils list: "camphor, citronella, castor, lemongrass, etc."
4. Script-shape adaptation: "Most script written on palm leaf is shaped in a circular form, so as to prevent the leaf from splitting while writing."
5. Case study dating: "The Astasahasrika Prajnaparamita... produced in March 21, 1015 by a scribe named Sujatabhadra"; "The 222 palm leaf folios contain a total of 85 paintings".
6. Shelf life: "The shelf life of a manuscript is normally around three to four centuries, but is shortened by mold, insect activity, and the hot, humid climates of South and Southeast Asia."

## S08. Cornell University Library Preservation Tutorial - "Palm Leaf Manuscripts"
- Status: FETCHED (HTML archived); content verified from archived copy
- URL: https://chinapreservationtutorial.library.cornell.edu/content/palm-leaf-manuscripts (mirror also exists at preservationtutorial.library.cornell.edu)

Verified facts:
1. "Palm leaf manuscripts are produced from two main types of palms: palmyra and talipot. The palmyra leaf is rather thick and inflexible and inclined toward brittleness over time. The talipot is thinner and more flexible and has excellent durability, reportedly lasting as long as 600 years."
2. Anatomy: "Palm leaves are plicate (that is, have parallel folds) and segmented, with a central rib."
3. Visibility mixture and oils: "applying a mixture of lampblack, bean plant or berry juice, and aromatic oil. The oils used have included camphor, citronella, castor, lemongrass, cedarwood, mustard, neem, eucalyptus, clove, and sesame. They are chosen for their insect repellent qualities."
4. Dimensions/binding: "they seem to average 48 centimeters in length and 4 centimeters in width"; cords through "two holes pierced through the entire manuscript about 4 centimeters from each end"; heavy wooden covers.

## Cross-source comparison notes (grounded strictly above)
- Durability numbers cluster: Cornell "as long as 600 years"; Wiland et al. "500-600 years"; Penn "three to four centuries" typical shelf life; Nishanthi cites Ferguson 1888 for 400-500-year palmyra mss in Sri Lanka.
- Burial/soaking durations differ by region: pond mud/silt 10-15 days (Meher; Wiland citing Agrawal); wet sand up to 3 months (Penn); marshy water up to 3 months (LIS Academy); Bali soak 3-4 days (Wiland); Sri Lankan vattu dew treatment 3 days (Nishanthi).
- Boil durations differ: ~10 h fermented rice water + tamarind (Yu, Yunnan); 8 h herbal solution (Wiland, Bali); up to 8 h (sawidji, unverified); 3 h spiced boil (lex-localis, unverified); "a few hours" water or limewater (Wiland, Sri Lanka).
- Chemical composition consensus: cellulose + hemicellulose + lignin (Zhang; Yu; PMC snippet). Yu gives processing-induced reductions (cellulose -39.90%, hemicellulose -3.97% relative contents).
- Optimal RH: Zhang et al. 2025 -> 50% RH optimal; Yu et al. 2024 -> avoid >70% RH.
