## Historical Background (excerpts)
Buddhism in present Cambodia is Theravada followed by approximately 95 percent of the total 15 million population... The current Theravada Buddhism became widespread and took firm root in the country in the late 13th century... Cambodian Theravada may have come from three sources: Thailand, Sri Lanka and Mon... Thai invaded Angkor for the second time in 1395 (for five months), captured hundreds of learned scholars and took religious scriptures and valuables to Thailand. After Jayavarman VII, Angkor declined and was finally abandoned in 1432.
[15th-century inscription with Pali words at Kompong Svay per George Coedes; monk Lanka Sriyasa taught Dhamma to royal princes.] Tamalinda, a son of Jayavarman VII, went to Sri Lanka and received ordination and education at the Mahavihara. After having studied Tripitaka in Sri Lanka for about ten years and earning the title Mahathera, he went to help establish and spread Sinhala Buddhism in Pagan (Myanmar) with four other monks (Chapata of Talaing/Mon; Sivali of Tanalithi/Tamluk Bengal; Ananda of Kancipura/Conjeeveram; Rahula of Sri Lanka). Pang Khat (Cambodian scholar): after studying Tripitaka at the Mahavihara Anuradhapura, Tamalinda Mahathera returned to Cambodia and helped consolidate Theravada at end of 12th century.
Jinakalamali (chronicle of Chiang Mai): 1967 years after Mahaparinirvana = 1424 CE(1423), eight monks headed by Mahananasiddhi from Cambodia with twenty-five monks from Nabbisipura (Chiang Mai) went to Sri Lanka to receive upasampada ordination; joined by six Mon monks from Ramanna. Granted ordination 1968 years after the Buddha's passing = 1424 ACE at Yapapattana in Kalyani region, under king Parakramabahu VI of Jayavardhanapura (Kotte). Vanarata Mahasami was Preceptor (upajjhaya); Dhammacariya Assistant Preceptor (kammavacacariya).
Inscription of 1770? [1.77m height] written 1305 ACE discovered at Neun Preah Bo (Prachinpuri province, Thailand, then part of Cambodia) mentions Theravada Buddhism brought there from Sri Lanka - strong proof Sri Lanka was main source of spread of Theravada to Cambodia.
Wat Lanka (Lankakusumaram) monastery built 15th century heart of Phnom Penh; 15th-16th centuries Sri Lankan monks used to come to Cambodia and stayed there.
Pali Tipitaka mainly introduced from Sri Lanka by Cambodian monks who studied there; also brought from Thailand (Nabbisipura/Chiang Mai, Lan Na kingdom capital 1296-1775) from 15th century up to French colonization (1863-1953). During colonial period French administration dissuaded Cambodian monks from travelling to study in Thailand.
Pali Tipitaka texts were copied and transcribed into Khmer on palm-leaves; stylists/preservers used elements like lacquer and coal powder to preserve the texts for long periods. Most texts did not contain names of copiers or transcribers as was the practice of the day.

## Khmer Tripitaka Commission
On 14th December 1929, King Sisowath signed a Royal ordinance for the setting up of the Tipitaka Commission for translating and editing the Pali texts in Khmer. Eminent Khmer scholars both monks and laymen (mostly former monks) were appointed; initially 37 members including a President, two deputies and 34 members of which 11 were laymen.
Idea initiated by: 1. Mr. Fermant Laric, Resident Superieur of French colonial administration; 2. Samdech Chaovea Veang Voraveangchey Chuon, Minister of Royal Palace, Royal Warehouse and Architecture, who drafted royal decree; 3. Samdech Chakrei Konchareathipatei Pon, Minister of Propaganda, Military and Education; 4. HRH Krom Luang Sisowath Sophanuvong, Minister of Interiors and Religious Affairs; 5. Oknha Yomareach Chea, Minister of Justice; 6. HRH Krom Khun Visuddhikhattiyavong Norodom Suramrith, Minister of Navigation, Agriculture, Handicrafts and Commerce; 7. Oknha Issarathipatei Pen; 8. HRH Samdech Krom Preah Vorachak Ranariddh Norodom Sothearos; 9. Miss Suzane Karpeles, Secretary General of Royal Library.
First commission list (positions): President Ven. Preah Sirisammativong Lvi Em; Vice Presidents Ven. Preah Udommuni Oum Sou, Ven. Preah Mahabrahmamuni Tep Ou; Member Ven. Preah Sasanasobhana Chuon Nath (later Sangharaja; Samdech Preah Pothiwong, Rector of the Buddhist University and later President of the Tipitaka Commission); Members incl. Huot Taht, Moeung, Som Chhun, Som Loch, Meung Ses, Ngean Chhin, Chap Pin... through #37 Uch Ros; lay teachers of PMS (Pan High School?) among them Nhok Them.
In 1940, the Tipitaka Commission was re-organized and leadership adjusted. Ven. Huot Tath promoted to post of Vice-head of the Commission following death of Ven. Oum Sou, while number of members increased to 46(?). In the 1940 list 21 new members added (incl. Pang Khat, Sanskrit Professor, prolific writer, Khmer translator of Hitopadesa).
By 1969 only 13 remained in toto including a President (Samdech Preah Bodhivang Huot Tath, Rector of SBU, President; Ven. Preah Munikosala So Hay Vice President; ...; Mr. Phal Tith layperson member).
All members had been either teachers/professors or graduates of monastic educational institutions.

## The corpus and completion
This magnum opus produced 110 volumes. Each volume ranges from 400 to 900 pages. Entitled Preah Trai Pitak, this bilingual corpus comprises: (i) 13 volumes of the Vinaya (Vinay Beydak), (ii) 64 volumes of the Suttanta (Suttant Beydak), and (iii) 33 volumes of the Abhidhamma (Abhidhamm Beydak). The completion of this great literary undertaking was celebrated on 1st and 2nd April 1969 in the capital on a grand scale.

## Translation method
Commission decided to compare editions published in Ceylon, Burma and England. Variants noted in footnotes: 'M' for Burmese (Maramma), 'S' for Sinhalese and 'E' for the Pali Text Society edition. Used Atthakatha, Tikas and Yojanas (commentaries, sub-commentaries, grammatical construction exegeses). Translation not colloquial but rendered in literary style to preserve original Pali meaning.
Six groups: 1. copying Pali from old texts; 2. editing Pali; 3. translating Pali into Khmer; 4. Khmer proofreading; 5. Khmer final proofreading; 6. final editing and publishing.
Arrangement to print Pali on one page and Khmer on the opposite page; both languages in same volume. Sets sent to Burma, Ceylon, China, India, Japan, Laos, Thailand, Vietnam, England, France, Germany and US on basis of donations, exchange or subscriptions.
Expected benefits listed (6 items), incl.: "Possessing the Tipitaka one side in Pali another side in Khmer is like having two lamps or lanterns set up on both sides of a path toward tranquility."

## Abolition under the Khmer Rouge
The Khmer Tripitaka Commission, like other institutions in the country, was abolished when the Khmer Rouge came into power on April 17, 1975, and all the commission members, except two, namely, Oum Sum and Heng Leang Hor, were killed. Since its abolition, the Tipitaka Commission has so far never been revived whereas other Buddhist Institutions have mostly been put back to life. Four reasons discussed: (1) regime targeted educated monks; most prestigious Commission members killed annually; few Pali/Buddhism scholars survived; (2) no strong commitments of post-KR regimes to re-establish it; (3) no facilities after KR defeat; (4) no urgent need since canonical texts already translated though Atthakatha, Tika, Yojana and other Pali compendiums not yet translated. Over past decades individuals translated some Atthakatha such as Samantapasadika (commentary of Vinaya Pitaka), Atthasalini (commentary of Dhammasangani); never officially accepted/authorized for formal Pali schools.

## Conclusion
From the time the Pali Tipitaka had been translated into Khmer and entire sets were published in 1969, Cambodia has proudly enjoyed possession of the whole Pali-Khmer Tipitaka... Had not the Tipitaka Commission been abolished, it would have continued translation work... It is fervently hoped that the Cambodian government will take necessary steps to set up the Khmer Tripitaka Commission or similar again to translate remaining Pali texts into Khmer.
