## ABSTRACT

Aiming to reconstruct local historical and modern production of palm-leaf manuscripts (PLM) preliminarily dated from the 14th to the beginning of the 20th century CE in South (S) and Southeast (SE) Asia, we faced a great volume of publications available in ca 20 languages, which briefly describe the manuscript production process and/or focus on PLM preservation traditions. However, we found a lack of publications with a botanical or ethnobotanical focus, and there are none that thoroughly document the detailed process of PLM production. Consequently, we conducted the first comprehensive analysis of literature and online sources dating from 1753 to 2024, covering approximately 270 years, and compiled a list of about 300 publications concerning both modern and traditional methods of palm-leaf manuscript production and preservation. By performing a geographical analysis and mapping of all retrieved botanical and ethnobotanical data related to this research, we compiled lists of plants used for PLM production and conservation in S and SE Asia. These lists are region-specific (e.g. for Bengal, Gujarat, Odisha, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and South India as a whole), and country-specific (e.g. for Sri Lanka and Indonesia). The lists of plant names mentioned in the analyzed publications range from as few as three in Nepal and Malaysia to as many as 79 in India overall. This work will enhance our understanding of traditional and modern plant usage related to the PLM cultures of South and Southeast Asia.

## Introduction

Palm leaves have traditionally been used in the millions to fashion manuscripts in South (S), Southeast (SE), and, more rarely, Central Asia for at least the last two millennia (e.g. Agrawal [Citation1984](#); Sah [Citation2002](#); Henderson [Citation2009](#); Colas [Citation2017](#); Riberaigua and Messager [Citation2018](#); Wiland [Citation2022](#), [Citation2023](#)). Various plants, specific to regions, cultures, historical periods, or manufacturers, are utilised in the multi-step palm-leaf manuscript (PLM) preparation process. Each step involves close contact between the writing support and other plants – from seasoning and boiling with various fruits and herbs to brushing (e.g. with diverse grasses, sand, shells, and ash), oiling, inking, and applying plant-based solutions for protection against insects. Understanding the methods and materials used in the preparation and conservation of PLMs is thus crucial for several reasons, both culturally and scientifically. Although early specimens are rarely extant, we know that PLMs are among the earliest forms of written records in many Asian societies, including India, Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Indonesia (Ciotti [Citation2021](#)). The content of these manuscripts, ranging from literary, religious, and philosophical texts to the first scientific treatises and legal and administrative documents, offers profound insights into the civilizations that crafted them, revealing their linguistic, artistic, and technological advancements. Preserving these manuscripts means safeguarding the narratives and knowledge of previous generations and maintaining a link to shared human history. Scientifically, the study of PLMs provides critical data for academic research in fields such as history, philology, anthropology, and natural science. In fact, material codicology and manuscript conservation studies develop in close connection with physics, computer science, chemistry, and biology.

Initially, the problems of the deterioration of PLMs and, as a consequence, their conservation stimulated some progress in material codicology (e.g. Bhattacharya [Citation1947](#), Chakravarti [Citation1947](#); Kishore [Citation1961–62](#); Agrawal [Citation1975](#), [Citation1977](#), [Citation1982](#), [Citation1984](#); Florian et al. [Citation1990](#); Wiland [Citation2022](#), [Citation2023](#)). However, as pointed out by Sah ([Citation2002](#)), “authoritative literature” on PLM conservation is scarce. The same can be said about the literature on PLM production; almost every publication that deals with the study of PLMs contains a brief description of the practices of palm-leaf manuscript production. However, they merely refer to the most cited sources, e.g. Agrawal ([Citation1984](#)), Joshi ([Citation1995](#)), Sah ([Citation2002](#)), Sahoo and Mohanty ([Citation2004](#), [Citation2007](#)), Meher ([Citation2009](#)), Sharma ([Citation2018](#)), Wilson and Rice ([Citation2019](#)), Sharma et al. ([Citation2021](#)). As of today, just a handful of publications contain clear and consistent lists of plants used for PLM production and/or preservation (Padhi [Citation1974](#); Agrawal [Citation1984](#); Suryawanshi et al. [Citation1992](#); Sah [Citation2002](#); Perumal [Citation2013](#)). The great majority of the authors describe plants only in terms of their local or regional names; the same names are used for different, often unrelated species or genera, so in many cases it is difficult, ambiguous, or impossible to understand what particular plant was meant.

Reviews on the PLM production and preservation literature are limited to two publications. Nishanthi et al. ([Citation2023](#)) performed a bibliometric study on PLM preservation methods in 101 publications (mainly journal and magazine papers) from 178 authors and author collectives, and covered the timeframe from 2002 to 2021. The study was based on an Internet search (Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, OpenAlex) for literature with the keywords “conservation of palm leaf”, “preservation of palm leaf”, and “conservation and preservation”, as indicated in the original paper. The authors performed citation analysis and described a trend of a growing number of publications on PLM conservation. Wiland ([Citation2022](#), [Citation2023](#)) carried out a comprehensive review of PLM conservation with a historic overview, a brief description of the country-specific palm leaf preparations, materials, and media used for leaf preservation in the past and nowadays. This two-part review used the available conservation literature and surveyed results to address the issues inherent in the practical conservation of palm-leaf artefacts. However, to the best of our knowledge, neither publications with a botanical or ethnobotanical focus, nor lists of plants used for PLM preparation or conservation in specific regions or countries are currently available.

With the aim of understanding what plants were historically used for the production of extant PLMs and which of them are still used nowadays in areas where PLMs are still produced (e.g. Bali), we performed a comprehensive analysis of literature and online publications published from 1753 (i.e. Palmae flabellifoliae [Citation1753](#)) to 2024 (i.e. Sun et al. [Citation2024](#)). The results of the work presented here will help us in the interpretation of the palaeoecological and palaeogenomic data obtained during our material studies of ancient and modern PLMs from S and SE Asia (Poliakova et al. [Citation2025](#)). Furthermore, from the point of view of cultural history, knowing in more detail what plants were involved in the production of PLMs can allow us to identify local production practices, thus providing us with a more nuanced understanding of the history of manuscript production, and investigating matters of continuity and change across different regions and sub-regions where this artisanal tradition has been thriving.

## Material and methods

### Literature search and algorithmizing

A systematic literature review was conducted in order to build up a comprehensive resource on the plants regionally used for production and/or traditional preservation of PLMs. We used four main Internet platforms for searching for academic publications, namely the Web of Science, Google Scholar, Mendeley, and Science Direct. The publication records were collected through the search function across all databases, using the following keywords: “palm-leaf manuscript\*” OR “palm leaf manuscript\*” OR “palm manuscript\*” OR “Pothi palm manuscript\*” OR “tala palm manuscript\*” OR “sritala palm manuscript\*” OR “lontar” OR “writing on palm leaves” OR “writing on leaves of palms” OR “palm-leaf manuscript creation” (\*create) OR “palm-leaf manuscript making” (\*make) OR “palm-leaf manuscript production” (\*produce) OR “palm-leaf manuscript conservation” OR “palm-leaf manuscript preservation”. We did not activate any internal automatic filters in the databases. The search was performed mainly in English, French, German, Polish, and Russian; translation of the keywords into South and Southeast Asian languages was performed using automatic translation through Google Translator.

We included in the analysis any literature and online sources that mentioned botanical (regardless of whether correctly or incorrectly spelled), trivial, or local names of plants used either for PLM production or preservation. The following selection criteria were applied during the final compilation of the publication list: (a) The publication (i.e. literature or Internet source of information) contains study (studies) or a revision on plants of any species, genera, and/or families used in the PLM production process, in the PLM conservation (preservation) process, or for both. (b) The publication contains clear reference(s) to the plant names in any form and language. (c) The publication contains clear indication of its author(s), production year, and issuing details. If the older synonym of the taxon is coherently referred to in the historical literature, the modern plant name is used in this study (i.e. *Ocimum tenuiflorum* L. for *Ocimum sanctum* L.). If the literature referred to different synonyms of the same plants, indicating different use of them, these synonyms were listed and analyzed separately, i.e. as in the case with *Vitex incisa* Lam. vs. *Vitex negundo* L. and *Vetiveria zizanioides* (L.) Nash vs. *Chrysopogon zizanioides* (L.) Roberty. Only primary sources of publications were used in order to avoid double counting of citations. The compilation of the publication list is based on the principles of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses, following recommendations of PRISMA ([Citation2020](#)) and Page et al. ([Citation2021](#)). The flow of identification, screening, and inclusion is summarized in Figure 1.

### Local plant names vs. scientific names

Most of the plants indicated in the analyzed literature were only mentioned through their local names. English common names or botanical names were in many cases confused with other plants, misspelled, referred to by a less used synonym, and/or assigned to a wrong family. To assess the scientific plant names, we used the taxonomic-local name matching as provided in Jain and Jain ([Citation2018](#)), Nayar et al. ([Citation2008](#)), Narasimhan and Irwin ([Citation2021](#)), Hajra and Mudgal ([Citation1997](#)), Verheijen ([Citation1993](#)), as well as electronic resources, i.e. Flora of Peninsular India ([https://indiaflora-ces.iisc.ac.in/FloraPeninsular/index.php](https://indiaflora-ces.iisc.ac.in/FloraPeninsular/index.php)), the Kerala Plants web-site ([http://keralaplants.in/index.html](http://keralaplants.in/index.html)) and the Electronic flora of Kerala ([http://keralaplants.in/trees-in-kerala.aspx](http://keralaplants.in/trees-in-kerala.aspx)), Flora of Sri Lanka ([https://www.floraofsrilanka.com/](https://www.floraofsrilanka.com/)), Digital flora of Indonesia ([https://www.indonesiaplants.org/](https://www.indonesiaplants.org/)), Flora Malesiana ([https://portal.cybertaxonomy.org/flora-malesiana/node/38](https://portal.cybertaxonomy.org/flora-malesiana/node/38)). The International Plant Name Index (IPNI) was followed for the correct format of plant authors. Plants are listed alphabetically (see Table 1) together with their local names, botanical families, and short references of the sources mentioning the plant. If the publication mentioned at least one plant with regard to PLM production and/or preservation, it was included in the list and analysis. The complete list of references can be found in Supplementary Material S1.

### Taxonomical and life form analysis

Plant taxonomy follows the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG [Citation2016](#)). Establishing the theoretical background for our material studies of the ancient and modern PLMs from S and SE Asia (Poliakova et al. [Citation2025](#)), and considering the specificity of palaeoecological and palaeogenomic methods, which merely reveal plants at the levels of families and genera, special attention in this study was paid to the family composition of plants used for PLM preparation and/or preservation. As some methods (i.e. phytolith and plant ash fragment analyses) are restricted by the preservation levels of the material and do not always reveal the exact taxonomy of the plants, but instead indicate large groups, e.g. arboreal vs. non-arboreal plants, grasses, annual vs. perennial plants, we also conducted analysis of the life forms. To assess the life-form diversity, for each plant its growing form was described as one of the following categories: for arboreal – “tree”, “shrub”, “vine”; for non-arboreal perennial plants – “forb” or “grass” for Poaceae; for one-season plants – “annual plant” (e.g. Rowe and Speck [Citation2005](#)). In addition, the category “fern” was used for Polypodiopsida. In case two categories should be used to describe the possible growing forms of a given plant, both were indicated with a backslash: “tree/shrub”, “tree/vine”. All trees were additionally subdivided into four groups: “palms” (Arecaceae), “conifers” (Coniferophyta), “deciduous trees” (those that seasonally shed leaves), and “evergreen trees” (those whose leaves remain functional throughout the year and are changed gradually).

### Geographical analysis

Geographical data included references to the specific region, country, or other geographical area. If the plant is mentioned for India, special attempt was made to find out if the given literature source refers to a certain state, manuscript production area, and/or language that can be attributed to certain territory(ies). If the plant is mentioned for Indonesia, we checked if the cited source refers to specific island(s). In case the specification was not possible, the formulation “as a whole” was used, e.g. “India as a whole”. If the attribution to the country was not possible, the formulation “SE Asia as a whole” was applied. In case two or more regions, countries, or states were mentioned, all these areas were listed. If the plant is referred to as used for the same purpose in India and Sri Lanka, that was indicated in the table and on maps specifically.

### Use of plants in PLM production and conservation

Based on the plant PLM production- and/or preservation-related utilization specified in the literature, the following categories were established: **(1)** insecticide and insect repellent plants, **(2)** oil plants, **(3)** manuscript preservation agents, **(4)** food plants (edible plants used for PLM production), **(5)** medicinal plants, **(6)** ritual plants (those having special meaning for religion or those used for worship and/or temple ceremonies), **(7)** ink producers, **(8)** ink binders and fixation agents, **(9)** plants used for brushing, smoothing, and polishing PLMs, **(10)** palm species whose leaves are used as the manuscript support, **(11)** plants used for making writing instruments (i.e. stylus and pen), **(12)** plants whose parts are used for the manuscript cover production, **(13)** poisonous plants, **(14)** narcotic plants, **(15)** plants used as fibre sources, and **(16)** plants used for ash and/or soot production for inking.

### Multivariate data analysis

Detrended Correspondence Analysis (DCA; Hill and Gauch [Citation1980](#)) was carried out on the frequencies of plant names that are referenced in the analyzed volume of publications, using the multivariate data analysis software CANOCO for Windows 5.10 (Ter Braak and Smilauer [Citation2018](#)). All plants were included in the analysis; no normalization or transformation of the data was performed. Comparison of the regions and sub-regions in terms of plants used for PLM production and/or traditional ways of conservation was performed with the Sørensen’s index of similarity (Sørensen [Citation1948](#); Carass et al. [Citation2020](#)). The Sørensen community similarity coefficient (Hammond and Pokorný [Citation2020](#)) ranges in values, indicating clear difference in two compared areas (S < 10), low similarity (S < 50), clear similarity (50 < S < 80), or that the plant composition of the two compared areas is almost identical (S > 80%).

The most used writing supports are *Borassus flabellifer* (indicated for six regions, referenced in 20 publications; Table 1, Figures 7(B) and 8(A)) and *Corypha umbraculifera* (mentioned for eight regions in 20 publications; Table 1, Figures 7(B) and 8(B)). *C. taliera* (six regions, nine citations; Table 1, Figures 7(B) and 8(C)) is, according to the results of our analysis, used mainly in India, whereas *C. lecomtei* (mentioned for three regions, six citations; Table 1, Figure 7(B)) is used in SE Asia (Vietnam, Burma/Myanmar, Indonesia) and is not mentioned for India. *C. utan* (six regions, six citations; Table 1, Figures 7(B) and 8(D)) is used both in S (Sri Lanka and Indian states of Gujarat, Kerala, Tamil Nadu) and SE Asia (Vietnam, Burma/Myanmar, and Indonesia in particular). *Nypa fruticans* is mentioned for Indonesia (two citations; Table 1, Figure 7(B)) and Malaysia (Hossain and Siddique [Citation1969](#); Shafri et al. [Citation2009](#); Cabra and Rathnabahu [Citation2023](#)), oil and medicinal plant (Geethecharya [Citation1995](#); Harinarayana [Citation1995](#); Joshi [Citation1995](#); Prajapati [Citation1995](#); Vivekanandagopal [Citation1995](#)).

*Vitex* sp., with four species mentioned separately, are all listed as repellents except *Vitex trifolia*. However, the simple fact that we did not find any reference to it being used as a repellent does not necessarily indicate that the plant was not used for that purpose: the absence of evidence cannot be considered as evidence itself. Instead, *V. trifolia* is referred to as a conservation agent and as a medicinal plant (Hinzler [Citation1993](#)). *V. agnus-castus* (three references, see Table 1; Figure 7(C)), in addition, is mentioned as an oil plant (Joshi [Citation1995](#); Mahilrajan et al. [Citation2014](#)). *Vitex negundo* is only indicated as a natural repellent (8 references). *Mentha spicata* (4 references; Table 1) has the highest variety of use: as a repellent, as an oil plant, and as a food and medicinal plant (Sahoo and Mohanty [Citation2004](#), [Citation2007](#); Sah [Citation2006](#)). Wood of *Tectona grandis* is used for making manuscript covers and as a source for making writing instruments (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#); Sheth [Citation1995](#)). Among Poaceae, some 11 plants are mentioned (Figures 7(D)) including at least three species of *Cymbopogon*: *C. nardus* (20 references: 10 from India, one reference from India and Sri Lanka mentioned together, and an additional reference from Nepal; Table 1, Figures 7(D) and 8(E)), *C. citratus* (13 references: eight from India and one from India and Sri Lanka mentioned together; Table 1, Figures 7(D) and 8(F)), and *C. martinii* (only mentioned by Sah [Citation2002](#)). From our own observations in local temples in Tamil Nadu (SE India), both rice seeds (fresh and cooked) and rice green are used for decoration inside. Some of these grasses were also regularly seen at the sanctuaries. Thus, in the literature reviewed so far, we did not find unequivocal evidence for rice to be used in religious practice; therefore, we do not include *Oryza* sp. into the list of religious plants.

Common reed (*Phragmites australis*) is also used for brushing palm leaf surfaces and for making writing instruments (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#); Sheth [Citation1995](#); Van Dyke [Citation2009](#); Wilson and Rice [Citation2019](#)). The strong regional differences revealed by the Sørensen’s index, on the one hand, prove the high cultural diversity of the study area, also in terms of PLM production, traditional preservation, and related plant use. On the other hand, these calculations most likely do not show a high difference in the plants used in various regions, but rather demonstrate a large difference in the amount of information about plants used in different regions available to individual authors. Therefore, these numbers should be treated with caution. Another factor to consider could be the subjectivity of the authors who attempted to describe the processes of PLM production. Most of them worked locally and did not tackle any picture larger than their study area.

## Discussion

### Individual palm-leaf manuscript production areas and related plant use

Most of the studied geographical areas are characterized by an individual set of plants used for PLM production and preservation. Based on the literature survey and DCA assumptions, we are able to describe several area- and culture-specific PLM production practices, mainly for individual Indian states.

#### Manuscript production in Bengal

In Bengal (i.e. the modern state of West Bengal, NE India), 15 plants are mentioned in the literature (Figure 3 and Figures 10–11), among them three species apparently used only there. *Artocarpus heterophyllus* is used as a source of wood for manuscript covers and writing instruments (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#)). *Capsicum annuum* serves as an insecticide and oil plant (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#); Sah [Citation2006](#)). *Careya arborea* is referred to as Kumbhi tree, or wild guava, whose bark is used as a conservation agent and, in Odisha, also as a writing support (Bisoi [Citation1995](#)). Dried (presumably fragmented and/or ground) leaves of *Phoenix dactylifera* are used for brushing and polishing as well as for covering the manuscripts (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#)).

#### Manuscript production in Gujarat

In Gujarat (NW India), 14 plants are mentioned in the literature, with three plants unique to the area. *Diospyros* sp., *Swietenia macrophylla*, and *Dalbergia* sp. were apparently used as sources of wood for making manuscript covers and writing instruments (Sheth [Citation1995](#)); these plants are not mentioned for any other region or geographical area. *Juglans regia/Juglans cinerea* is used for timber, and its fruit is also mentioned as an important oil source in India and Sri Lanka. *Phragmites australis* is also mentioned for Bengal and for South India as a whole (Mahaparata [Citation1995](#); Van Dyke [Citation2009](#); Wilson and Rice [Citation2019](#)). Sheth ([Citation1995](#)) additionally specified that a reed pen was used in Northern India, whilst in the South, *Phragmites* served rather for making powder of charcoal and soot (presumably for inking).

#### Manuscript production in Odisha

A total of 35 plants are mentioned in the analyzed publications for Odisha (NE India; Figure 3 and Figures 10–11), including at least nine specific plants – namely, *Alpinia calcarata/Alpinia galangal* (exact species is unclear), used as insecticide and as a food plant; *Phyllanthus emblica* and *Terminalia bellirica*, applied as insecticides; as well as *Pongamia pinnata*, which is also used as an oil plant. Among the ink binders, there are *Zea mays* (only starch; the use of any other products or parts of maize is not indicated), *Triticum* sp., and *Solanum tuberosum* (all three are also used for food). *Arachis hypogaea* is referred to as an ink producer, oil, and food plant (see Table 1 for references), and conf. *Craspedia* sp. is only mentioned as a conservation agent and only for Odisha. The insecticidal *Panicum dichotomiflorum* is also mentioned for India and SE Asia as a whole (see Table 1 for references). *Crinum asiaticum* (insecticide, oil, and medicinal plant) is simultaneously mentioned for SE Asia as a whole. *Vitex negundo* is mentioned three times specifically for Odisha (Sahoo and Mohanty [Citation2004](#), [Citation2007](#); Sah [Citation2006](#)) but could also be used in other (unspecified) parts of India and in other countries of SE Asia (Joshi [Citation1995](#); Meher [Citation2009](#), Sharma [Citation2018](#); Wilson and Rice [Citation2019](#); [Citation2021](#)). At the same time, Perumal ([Citation2013](#)) refers to this species as one specifically used in Tamil Nadu as a conservation and Insecticide (insect repelling agent).

#### Manuscript production in Kerala

Kerala (SW India) is associated with 15 plants (Figures 3 and 10–11) used in PLM practices, with five plants being specific to this particular area: *Illicium verum* (insecticide and oil plant; see Table 1 for references), *Olea europaea* and *Linum usitatissimum* (both oil and food plants), *Murraya exotica* (conservation agent), and *Citrus aurantiifolia/Citrus × latifolia* (conservation agents, oil and food plants). Other plants used in Kerala are also used in other Indian states, surprisingly not only in the neighbouring Tamil Nadu (8 plants are used in both states, but for different purposes), but also in northern ones, i.e. Bengal (with six plants used in both areas, Kerala and Bengal) and Gujarat (with five shared plants used for the same purposes in both states). Although all mentioned areas of manuscript production are characterized by either low similarity (Kerala vs. Tamil Nadu; S = 30.1) or a clear difference in plant use (Kerala vs. Bengal S = 21.2 and Kerala vs. Gujarat S = 25).

#### Manuscript production in Tamil Nadu

In Tamil Nadu (SE India), around 20 plants (Figure 3 and Figures 10–11) are used according to the analysed publications, with two unique plants recognized for the area so far, namely *Glycyrrhiza glabra* and *Calendula officinalis*. Both are used as insecticide and oil plants, with the latter species also considered a medicinal plant. In addition, *Nicotiana* species (possibly *N.* *tabacum*, *N.* *sylvestris*, *N.* *tomentosiformis*, and/or *N.* *otophora*) can be assigned as specifically used in Tamil Nadu, as well as in Gujarat. *Datura stramonium* is referred to as having been used also in Sri Lanka and unspecified areas of South India, which includes Tamil Nadu but can also be read in a much broader geographical context.

#### Manuscript production in Indonesia

Only a scarce number of publications is available for Indonesia (three publications; Figure 3), with another three for Bali specifically. In terms of plants used for PLM production, Balinese practice appears to be rather similar to those in India. In Bali, conf. *Erythrina abyssinica* and *Dracaena angustifolia* are mentioned as ink producers (Hinzler [Citation1993](#)). *Dracontomelon* sp., *Dioscorea hispida*, *Curcuma xanthorrhiza*, *Vitex trifolia*, *Uncaria gambir*, *Bruguiera parviflora*, and *Alpinia galangal* are used as manuscript conservation agents. *Artocarpus mariannensis* and *Aristolochia grandiflora* are used as “plant fibre” (Hinzler [Citation1993](#)), which could mean either ink binding or manuscript cleaning with these fibres. Other Indonesian islands, i.e. Java, Madura, and Lombok, are mentioned in the literature only in association with *Corypha lecomtei* as a palm species regionally used as a manuscript support.

#### Palm-leaf manuscript production in other countries

For Malaysia, no data on area-specific plants were retrieved from the analyzed publications. The use of *Nypa fruticans* in the country is a matter of discussion, since there are only scant references to it (Hossain and Siddique [Citation1969](#); Shafri et al. [Citation2021](#)) that are difficult to verify. *Cymbopogon nardus*, *Camphora officinarum/Cinnamomum camphora*, and *Curcuma longa* are also used in other countries and regions of the studied area (Table 1, Figure 11). For Thailand (23 publications; Figure 3), only the plants most commonly used for PLM production are mentioned (unspecified palms as the writing support and *Cymbopogon* species as protective agents). For Burma/Myanmar, we managed to find four publications mentioning two species, i.e. *Cymbopogon nardus* and *Corypha lecomtei*. In two publications available for Vietnam, only *Corypha lecomtei* is mentioned. For Laos, there are six publications referring to “preparation of the palm leaves in a specific way”, “oiling”, and “applying various herbs”, but not providing any plant names. No literature is found for Cambodia. Field and archival work along with precise material analysis of the manuscripts from these countries is needed in order to reveal which plants are used there for PLM production.

### Plants not associated with any geographical area

Additionally, there are several plants that demonstrated a weak correlation (r2 ≤ 0.3) to any of the three geographical areas; instead, they were clustered in the middle of the diagram. These plants are used for multiple purposes in several (five or more) countries and regions within countries across the whole study area. These plants are *Acorus calamus* (Figures 7(E) and 9(D)), widely used in S and SE Asia as an insecticide; *Cymbopogon nardus* (Table 1; Figure 7(D) and Figure 8(E)), *C. citratus* (Table 1; Figure 7(D) and Figure 8(F)), *Camphora officinarum/Cinnamomum camphora* (Table 1; Figure 7(E)), and *Cuminum cyminum* – all four species are used as insecticides and oil sources (see Table 1 for references).

Bambusoideae (unspecified, Table 1) is an extremely diverse group of Poaceae, accounting, according to different estimations, for ca 115 genera and about 1400 species (Kelchner and Working Group [Citation2013](#)) to ca 121 genera and 1662 species (Canavan et al. [Citation2017](#)). Bamboos grow and were/are used for various purposes, from brushing and polishing of palm leaves to feeding the fire, since they are much cheaper compared to any wood. These plants are used all across S and SE Asia, but it was extremely difficult to understand from the publications analyzed so far what bamboos exactly are referred to. To conclude on this, precise botanical (possibly including plant DNA studies) and palaeoecological research are needed.

*Oryza sativa* (Table 1; Figure 7(D)) is often mentioned in the literature as a plant used in PLM production (Gunawardana [Citation1997](#); Sah [Citation2002](#); Padmakumar and Sreekumar [Citation2003](#); Panda [Citation2006](#); Meher [Citation2009](#); Alahakoon [Citation2012](#); Wilson and Rice [Citation2019](#)) since the earliest available publication (Chakravarti [Citation1897](#)). Rice is not associated with any country and/or region in terms of its use, although it has a typical application: most of the references (6 of 8) refer to rice as a seasoning and polishing agent. Thus, different parts of the plant can be used, from the fresh, dry, wet, and rotten rice straw to the husks, ground grains, and water left after paddy cooking for boiling palm leaves before turning them into manuscripts. The last plant of this group is *Indigofera tinctoria* (Table 1). This typically used as an ink producer (Joshi [Citation1995](#); Sah [Citation2002](#); Meher [Citation2009](#); Van Dyke [Citation2009](#); Wilson and Rice [Citation2019](#); Sharma et al. [Citation2021](#)) in S and SE Asia, but in Odisha also as a ritual plant.

### Bias and uncertainties of this study

Having studied ca 300 literature sources on PLM production and/or traditional preservation, covering ca 270 years of publications from all across S and SE Asia, we attempted to put together scarce and fragmented knowledge about plants used for seasoning, brushing, oiling, inking, protecting, and preservation of PLMs. Based on DCA analysis (Figure 10), we revealed which plants are associated with particular geographical areas and mapped them (Figure 11). We described some main culture- and area-related patterns in PLM-related plant use, particularly for West Bengal, Odisha (NE India), Gujarat (NW India), Kerala (SW India), Tamil Nadu (SE India), and Indonesia (Bali).

Nevertheless, our knowledge on the subject remains discrete, contradictory, and incomplete. In the absence of any previous botanical or ethnobotanical studies on PLM production and preservation, it is difficult to estimate how close our assumptions are to reality. This is especially true if we take into account that: (1) Assessing botanical names of plants mentioned through their common or local names, often spelled in the local language, can (and most likely did) insert errors into our plant list. (2) It is often difficult to verify what exact plant was meant in the primary publication, as some authors refer to plants without giving their initial names as they learned them, but by changing the initially provided plant name to the English common name, or even by replacing it with the scientific name of an unrelated plant, not rarely from another family. In several cases, initial publications mentioning the plants in question were not available, or the list of references was not provided. As a result, information was lost or corrupted in the chain of inaccurate references. (3) Scientific plant names are not always used in PLM-related publications. Common plant names are redundant; the same plant name can be used in different areas for not necessarily related plants. (4) Introduced and exotic plants can be nicknamed with the same name used for locally known plants. For example, in South India and Sri Lanka the name “sal” is often used both for *Shorea robusta* Roth (Dipterocarpaceae) and for *Couroupita guianensis* Aubl. (Lecythidaceae) (Malaka [Citation2020](#)). (5) We cannot exclude financially motivated frauds involving plants, which can lead to deliberate (i.e. intentional) use of the wrong plant names, as is often the case with *Santalum album* L. (Santalaceae) and its various surrogates (Dev et al. [Citation2014](#)) or saffron (*Crocus sativus* L., Iridaceae) and its cheaper imitations (Ma et al. [Citation2001](#)). (6) Despite PLMs and their production being known for centuries, credible academic articles on the subject are scarce; the publication array is mainly represented by secondary and grey literature. (7) For some countries, even those well-known for their rich and long PLM culture, publications in English are virtually missing. This is the case for almost all mainland SE Asia and Indonesian islands except some areas in Java and Bali. (8) Data on plants and their PLM-related use that could have been historically collected by individual authors is limited. (9) One should also note the discrepancy in the number of references between regions or countries, which introduces another level of dissimilarity and uncertainty into this study. Further studies on the subject would greatly benefit from the analysis of primary sources in local languages, as well as from regional-to-local botanical and ethnobotanical observations conducted with professional rigour and cultural consideration.

## Conclusions

We conducted the first comprehensive review of literature and online sources from 1753 to 2024, covering approximately 270 years and analyzing 311 publications with 931 references to plants used in PLM preparation and traditional conservation across South and Southeast Asia. Our systematic analysis identified 165 plant species mentioned for 16 purposes in PLM production, including repellents/insecticides (75 in total), oil plants (73), conservation agents (45), ritual plants (18), ink producers (18), binders (10), polishing agents (8), plants used as writing support (6), plants used for making manuscript covers (9; mainly woody plants and bamboos), and writing instruments (8). Among these, about half are food plants (34) and one-third are medicinal plants (19). Functional groupings show that repellent or insecticide and oil plants are the most commonly cited uses, and that many plants have multiple roles, such as food and medicinal applications. At least seven poisonous and three narcotic plants are applied for PLM production in S and SE Asia, mainly for conservation and insecticide purposes. These plants are *Daphne bholua*, *Datura stramonium*, various species from the *Nicotiana* genus, *Ricinus communis*, *Ryania* sp., *Piper betle*, and *Withania somnifera*. All these plants are mentioned in the analyzed publications at least twice by independent authors.

Although diverse plant use is highlighted, especially in India and Sri Lanka, the variety and specifics of plants reported vary widely between regions, reflecting both real differences and discrepancies in the number, quantity and detail of available sources. Patterns of plant use show significant cultural and regional diversity, but comparisons are limited by the uneven data availability across areas. The Sørensen’s index demonstrates generally low similarity in plant lists between regions. However, differences revealed in this study should be treated with caution, as the dissimilarities are most likely not (or not only) to be attributed to differences in plants used in various regions, but rather to the large difference in the amount of information about plants used in different regions collected by individual authors. Despite attempts made in this study, future research is highly desired to directly document current and historic PLM craft practices (especially in understudied regions), provide unambiguous botanical identification (through field collection and analysis), and systematically study functions beyond preservation – such as inks, binders, and ritual uses. Laboratory analysis of plant materials from manuscripts (e.g. studies of the plant DNA traces and plant micro-fragments accumulated on the surface of the PLM) as well as in-depth ethnobotanical interviews with knowledge holders, are essential to fill remaining gaps and preserve disappearing traditions or reconstruct them in the regions where these practices have ceased.
