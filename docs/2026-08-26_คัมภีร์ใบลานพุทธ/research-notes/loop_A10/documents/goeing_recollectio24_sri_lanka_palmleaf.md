  <div class="entry-hero">
    <span class="card-num">#24 &middot; Colonial knowledge displacement</span>
    <h1>Sri Lanka Palm Leaf Manuscripts</h1>
    <p class="type-line">Buddhist and indigenous knowledge on palm leaf — two-phase displacement: Portuguese destruction, then British scholarly extraction</p>
    <div class="fact-grid">
      <div><div class="fact-label">Key Dates</div><div class="fact-value">1st c. BCE (earliest Pali MSS) → Portuguese destruction 16th c. → British extraction 18th–19th c. → EAP digitisation ongoing</div></div>
      <div><div class="fact-label">Crisis</div><div class="fact-value">Portuguese colonial destruction / British colonial extraction</div></div>
      <div><div class="fact-label">Origin</div><div class="fact-value">Buddhist monasteries and scholarly communities, Sri Lanka</div></div>
      <div><div class="fact-label">Current Location</div><div class="fact-value">National Museum Colombo, temple collections across Sri Lanka, John Rylands Manchester (70+ MSS), British Library, Royal Library Copenhagen, Bodleian Oxford</div></div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="timeline-section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Provenance Route</h2>
    <div class="timeline">
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">1st c. BCE</div>
        <div class="tl-text">The earliest recorded Pali manuscripts are produced in Sri Lanka. Palm-leaf writing — inscribed with a stylus on dried and smoke-treated palmyra or talipot leaves — becomes the primary medium for recording Buddhist scripture, medicine, literature, agriculture, astrology, mathematics, and law. Temple libraries serve as custodial centres where texts are copied when they wear out, maintaining a continuous manuscript tradition.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">16th c.</div>
        <div class="tl-text">Portuguese colonial invasions devastate Sri Lanka’s manuscript heritage. Temple libraries are destroyed as part of systematic campaigns against Buddhist institutions. The scale of loss is incalculable: palm-leaf manuscripts, fragile in tropical climate and dependent on institutional copying for survival, perish when the copying institutions are destroyed. Sinhalese kings initiate emergency copying efforts to preserve what remains.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">17th–18th c.</div>
        <div class="tl-text">Surviving manuscripts continue to be produced and preserved in temple collections, particularly in the Kandyan kingdom, which resists Portuguese and then Dutch colonial control. The manuscript tradition adapts but contracts.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">18th–19th c.</div>
        <div class="tl-text">British scholars begin acquiring palm-leaf manuscripts from Sri Lanka. Thomas William Rhys Davids (1843–1922), founder of the Pali Text Society, commissions unusually complete copies from Sri Lankan scholars — including some manuscripts that are rare even in Sri Lanka. Approximately seventy manuscripts reach the John Rylands Library in Manchester, donated by Rhys Davids in 1915.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">20th c.</div>
        <div class="tl-text">Additional manuscripts reach the British Library, Royal Library Copenhagen, and other European institutions through scholarly acquisition. Sir John Frederick Dickson (1835–91), a British colonial administrator who learned Pali, commissions Buddhist monks to produce manuscripts addressed to him — personalised diplomatic copies within the colonial relationship.</div>
      </div>
      <div class="timeline-event">
        <div class="tl-year">21st c.</div>
        <div class="tl-text">The British Library’s Endangered Archives Programme supports multiple projects to survey, digitise, and catalogue surviving palm-leaf collections in Sri Lanka — Tamil, Sanskrit, Sinhala, and even Malay manuscripts. The John Rylands Research Institute partners with Paramaththa and ICTB UK to digitise thirty-two Sinhalese manuscripts. The projects reveal that “most are already lost”: the surviving collections are a residue of a much larger tradition.</div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div class="prose-section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Historical Context</h2>
    <p>Sri Lanka’s palm-leaf manuscripts document a two-phase displacement that mirrors the European pattern with devastating precision. The first phase — Portuguese destruction of temple libraries in the sixteenth century — parallels the English Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–41), which destroyed the institutional framework of English manuscript culture. In both cases, a colonial or reforming power systematically dismantled religious institutions, and the manuscript collections housed within them were casualties of institutional destruction.</p>
    <p>The second phase — British scholarly extraction in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — parallels the Chalki slow-bleed model (Card #14): a gradual transfer of manuscripts from their communities of origin to European institutions through the asymmetric power dynamics of colonial scholarship. Rhys Davids and Dickson were not looters; they were scholars who valued what they acquired. But their acquisitions were enabled by colonial relationships that made Sri Lankan manuscripts available to European collectors in ways that would not have been possible between equal partners.</p>
    <p>The palm-leaf medium adds a material dimension absent from European cases. Palm leaves deteriorate in tropical climates; without regular institutional copying, texts are lost within centuries. When the Portuguese destroyed the copying institutions, they destroyed not just existing manuscripts but the mechanism of transmission. Unlike parchment or paper, which can survive for centuries in favourable conditions, palm leaf requires active custodianship. The destruction of institutions was therefore the destruction of the future as well as the present.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="prose-section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Material Evidence &amp; Provenance Marks</h2>
    <p>Palm-leaf manuscripts carry distinctive physical characteristics: incised lettering with applied ink, strung through central holes, often stored between carved wooden boards. Sinhalese, Burmese, and Cambodian script traditions can be identified palaeographically. Western cataloguing systems have been overlaid on manuscripts that originated within temple classification traditions. Some copies commissioned for European scholars carry bilingual elements — Pali text with Sinhala paraphrase, or dedicatory inscriptions to British officials — that document the colonial extraction process itself.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="prose-section">
    <h2 class="section-title">Role in Recollectio</h2>
    <p>Card #24 extends Recollectio furthest from its European core, testing whether the provenance-event model developed for parchment and paper manuscripts can accommodate a fundamentally different material medium with different preservation requirements and different institutional traditions. The answer matters: if Recollectio’s framework can model palm-leaf displacement, it can claim genuinely universal applicability.</p>
    <p>The EAP digitisation methodology — surveying, photographing, and cataloguing manuscripts in their current locations across Sri Lanka — connects directly to Recollectio’s partnership model with the British Library and Manchester. The Sri Lankan projects demonstrate that digital preservation can operate at scale in contexts where manuscripts remain in community custody, creating access without physical displacement.</p>
  </div>
