### F2. Key claims read in full text
- Definition: nissaya = "a type of translation in which each Pali word is followed by its relevant annotative interpretation"; found "in some of the earliest Buddhist literature found in Myanmar."
- Etymology: from Vinaya Mahāvagga sense of "guidance/reliance" (novice under preceptor ≥5 years); Skt niśraya = "requisite/support."
- Layered origin chain (per Tin Lwin 1961/2014): Pyu nissaya at Srikṣetra (Sanskrit passage interpretation, 7th c. excavation) → Mon nissaya (Pagan) → Sinhalese sannaya → Burmese nissaya. Oldest extant Burmese nissaya claimed = Khuddakasikkhā nissaya by Mahākassapa, reign Narapatisithu (1174-1211); but Ruiz-Falqués 2015: "no record of grammatical nissayas from the Pagan period"; popularization assumed 15th c.
- Walker 2020:678 cited: Pali-Burmese nissayas extant from late 13th c.; Sanskrit-Burmese bitexts listed in Thaungdwing governor's inscription of 1442.
- Okell 1965 characterized: "nissaya is a linguistic analysis with special emphasis on Pali grammar features in the Burmese language"; Pali morphology/syntax adapted into Burmese since late Pagan period.
- Sri Lanka parallel: Suttanipāta-sannaya (12th c.) earliest sannaya; McDaniel 2008:119 — Thailand/Laos genres nissaya, nāmasadda, vohāra.
- Genre scale (McDaniel 2008:131): nissaya/nāmasadda = close word-for-word glosses; vohāra cites 12-25 Pali words then extensive creative vernacular commentary; nissaya typically cites 4-10 words before glossing.
- Thai nissaya weaker than Burmese tradition: called gambhi-nisai (< gambhīra-nissaya "deep support"), e.g., Kaccāyana Gambhi-nisai; von Hinüber 1997 Handbook 100-1: Thai nissayas = vohāra or nāmasap (< nāma-śabda), "difference between vohāra and nissaya is not clear."
- Seven interpretive methods (Kāruṇika 2008:10): pāṭhānusārī, pāṭhasesa, saddattha, saṅketattha, paññatyattha, vohārattha, adhippāyattha.
- Chaṭṭhasaṅgīti official Tipitaka translation derives from old nissayas with minimal adjustment; added words (pāṭhasesa) appear in parentheses (Clark 2015:114).
- 19th c.: over 22 nissayas on Abhidhammattha-saṅgaha alone (History of the Abhidhamma 1965).
- Classroom layout link: ane'-de = "marking annotation in the margin" — students interlineate nissaya interpretations into the space around printed Pali textbooks; Fig. 3 photo caption: students "interlineating nissaya's interpretation in their printed textbook," Veluvan Monastery, Taunggyi.

## C. Hundius 1990 — colophons of Lan Na Pali manuscripts [VERIFIED-PAGE, OCR full text]

### F4. Verification and key facts
- Hundius, Harald. 1990. "The Colophons of Thirty Pāli Manuscripts from Northern Thailand." *Journal of the Pali Text Society* XIV: 1-173. Full OCR text downloaded from archive.org item jpts-xiv-1990. [VERIFIED-PAGE — hundius1990_jpts14_colophons_djvu.txt]
- Thirty manuscripts span "well over four centuries": earliest from turn of 15th-16th c.; studied via 1974 microfilms.
- 15th-16th c. learned Lan Na monks "were not only able to translate the huge corpus of Buddhist Scriptures from Pali into the vernacular language, Northern Thai"; script adapted for NT writing.
- "the canonical works were disseminated in bilingual (Pali-NT) versions called /vohaar/" (= vohāra) through the Lan Na Dhamma script (/tia muan/, tua mueang).
- Earliest dated Dhamma-script vernacular NT writing: AD 1465 (CS 827) on Buddha image base, Wat Chiang Man, Chiang Mai — "two short lines... preceded by two lines written in Pali" (Penth 1976:55ff). [Direct physical evidence of Pali+vernacular pairing]
- Colophons are in vernacular Northern Thai ("vernacular colophons of the thirty selected manuscripts"); colloquial NT prose or humorous verse; scribes admonish readers to be critical due to copyists' limited Pali knowledge.
- Physical practice: "engraving the letters on to the palm-leaf pages"; pagination system of palm-leaf folios described (sec. 1.4); bundles/fascicles (phuuk) with wooden covers and wedges.
- Von Hinüber 1988 cited re: Burmese subjugation of Lan Na affecting manuscript survival.

## E. Walker follow-ups — Khmer bitexts [VERIFIED-PAGE]

### F7. Walker 2020/21 Udaya 15 — epigraphic evidence for Cambodian bitexts
- Walker, Trent. "Carved Chants and Sermons on Stone: Epigraphic Evidence for Buddhist Literature in Middle Cambodia." *Udaya, Journal of Khmer Studies* No. 15 (2020, publ. 2021): 57-93. Open PDF at yosothor.org (archived). [VERIFIED-PAGE]
- IMA 32 (Angkor Wat inscription of 1688 CE) contains "a bitextual Pali-Khmer prayer... the earliest such datable example to surface in Cambodia"; style already mature → techniques developed between 12th and 16th c.
- K. 484 (late 12th c., Phimeanakas): Sanskrit-Khmer inscription using Khmer particles to mark Sanskrit case/number/mood (acc marked by nau/nūv) — "a hallmark technique of later Indic-vernacular bitexts", though not a true interlinear bitext.
- "Indic-vernacular bitexts were the dominant form of Buddhist writing across most of mainland Southeast Asia from the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries"; portions combined "in a phrase-by-phrase (interphrasal) or line-by-line (interlinear) format."
- Cambodia manuscript counts: ~20,000 incised palm-leaf mss survive (13,897 catalogued in four provinces per fn.5), almost all copied 1860s-1960s; virtually none before mid-18th c.; oldest known Cambodian ms = FEMC C.48 (Cittaganthidipani, Siamese-made, paleographically 1650-1750). Leporello (krāṃṅ) mss ≤1,000.
- fn.15: "at least 311 distinct Khmer texts and over 390 distinct Pali-Khmer bitexts transmitted on long-format palm-leaf manuscripts (sāstrā) in Cambodia."
- Oldest dated Lanna ms restated: CS 833 = 1471/72 CE (DLNTM 030104030_02).

### F8. Walker 2022 — three bitext sermon formats
- Walker, Trent. 2022. "Liquid Language: The Art of Bitextual Sermons in Middle Cambodia." *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 50.4 (Special Issue on The Idea of Text in Buddhism): 705-723. Springer; JSTOR stable/48808306. Full text paywalled; abstract + metadata fetched via r.jina.ai. [VERIFIED-PAGE abstract; body SNIPPET]
- Abstract: Theravada sermons in palm-leaf collections across South/SE Asia "are frequently bilingual"; three Pali-Khmer sermons (16th-19th c. Cambodia) each use a different format: "rearranged phrasal gloss, selective sentence gloss, and vernacular expansion."

### F10. How interlinear translation shaped physical layout (palm leaf)
- Siam/Cambodia: "Interlinear annotations in a special script for this purpose, known in Thai as khaam hvăt or t́vă kṣien, appear on certain Pali palm-leaf manuscripts" — mostly core-curriculum works (Kaccāyanavyākaraṇa, Dhammapadaṭṭhakathā, Maṅgalatthadīpanī, Sāratthasaṅgaha, Samantapāsādikā, Visuddhimagga, Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha); manuscripts thus carry three hands: scribe (carved letters inked), proofreader (ink-pen corrections + "proofreading complete" dān lèv2 / phdieṅ höy on cover leaf), annotator (interlinear glosses). [VERIFIED-PAGE — Walker JAOS archived md lines 35-39]
- Burma (RAS collection): nissaya olas catalogued with line counts — e.g., rasburmese15 "Fragment Pāli-Burmese nissaya. 110 olas...; 2 cord holes, 10 lines, 51 char."; rasburmese2: 126 olas, 8 lines; rasburmese60 Pali-Burmese nissaya fragments BS 1185?=1823. [SNIPPET via DDG; rasburmese2 previously VERIFIED in A06]
- V&A O39994 palm-leaf nissaya: "132 folios... two cord holes and bound with a single green cotton cord... lettering is in red on a yellow ground. Burmese round medium script is used for both the Pali and the Burmese texts... margins are 4 cm and undecorated." V&A O35191: red lacquered/gilded Pali-Burmese nissaya fragment, Konbaung, 1st half 19th c. [SNIPPET]
- Penn Museum 51-9-4 (Kislak): "Nissaya manuscript between 1775 and 1799" — "Pedagogical monastic work of Northern Thai commentary on a limited amount of Pali text"; "Written in 5 lines per leaf"; palm leaf, 2 central holes ~18 cm apart, red lacquered edges; 5.2 x 55 cm; Wat Mansikam; languages Thai; Pali "(Lanna)"; title supplied by Justin McDaniel (viewed 22 Dec 2015). [VERIFIED-PAGE — penn_colenda_nissaya_1775_1799.html]
- Burma classroom continuation: ane'-de margin/interline marking in printed books (Herngseng, sec. 6) shows the interlinear habit persisting after print transition.
