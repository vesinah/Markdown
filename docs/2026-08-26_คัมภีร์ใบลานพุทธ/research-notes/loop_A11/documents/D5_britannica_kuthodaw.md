# Kuthodaw Pagoda

pagoda complex, Mandalay, Myanmar

## Top Questions

What is the Kuthodaw Pagoda known for?
The Kuthodaw Pagoda is known for housing an extraordinary edition of the Pali canon, the body of Theravada Buddhist scripture. The text is inscribed across 729 marble tablets, each placed within an individual white stupa, arranged in rows surrounding a central gilded pagoda in Mandalay, Myanmar, forming a monumental religious complex.

When was the Kuthodaw Pagoda completed and by whom?
The Kuthodaw Pagoda was completed in 1868 by order of King Mindon, who ruled Myanmar from 1853 to 1878. It was commissioned as part of his broader effort to establish Mandalay as the country's new royal capital and to promote and preserve Theravada Buddhist teachings while serving as a monument of Myanmar's culture.

What significant relics are housed in the central stupa of the Kuthodaw Pagoda?
The central stupa of the Kuthodaw Pagoda houses various sacred relics, most notably tooth relics of the Buddha that were obtained from Sri Lanka. Such relics have long held religious importance in the Buddhist tradition of Myanmar and, more broadly, across the Buddhist world, where they are objects of devotion and pilgrimage.

How were the Kuthodaw Pagoda's marble tablets created?
The marble tablets were produced through a multistage process. The text was first reviewed by clerics and then copied by scribes onto marble slabs. Stonemasons chiseled the inscriptions by hand, averaging 10 lines per day. The marble was quarried from Sagyin Hill and transported by river to Mandalay for carving.

What happened to the original gold inlay used in the Kuthodaw Pagoda's marble tablet inscriptions?
The inscriptions on the marble tablets were originally filled with gold, making the carved letters more visible against the stone. However, after the British annexation of Mandalay in 1885, much of the gold was looted. Beginning in 1892, the inscriptions were instead filled with black ink to preserve their legibility.

## Main article

Kuthodaw Pagoda, pagoda in Mandalay, Myanmar, completed in 1868 by order of King Mindon (reigned 1853–78). It was commissioned as part of the establishment of Mandalay as the new royal capital. The complex is best known for housing an extraordinary edition of the Pali canon, the body of Theravada Buddhist scripture, inscribed across 729 marble tablets arranged within individual white stupas surrounding a central gilded pagoda.

The central stupa also houses various sacred relics, including tooth relics of the Buddha obtained from Sri Lanka.

Work on the marble inscriptions began in 1860. The text was first reviewed by three clerics and then copied by scribes onto marble for stonemasons to chisel. Most stonemasons engraved an average of 10 lines per day. The marble was quarried from Sagyin Hill, roughly 25 miles (40 km) north of Mandalay, cut into large blocks, floated down the Irrawaddy River, and transported to the capital. Each finished tablet stands 5 feet (1.5 meters) tall and is too heavy for any single person to carry. A 730th tablet records the history of the project and Mindon's reign. The inscriptions were originally inlaid with gold, which was looted following the British annexation of Myanmar in 1885 and replaced with black ink beginning in 1892.

The site has remained an important religious and cultural monument, and in 2013 the Kuthodaw Inscription Shrines were added to UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
