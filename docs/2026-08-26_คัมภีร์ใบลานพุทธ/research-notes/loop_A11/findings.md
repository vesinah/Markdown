## S1. British Library — "Kammavaca: Burmese Buddhist ordination manuscripts"
Author: San San May, Curator for Burmese | Published 17 Feb 2017 | URL: https://www.bl.uk/stories/blogs/posts/kammavaca-burmese-buddhist-ordination-manuscripts | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D1)

Key facts with exact quotes:
1. Definition: "*Kammavaca* is a Pali term describing an assemblage of passages from the Tipitaka – the Theravada Buddhist canon – that relate to ordination, the bestowing of robes, and other rituals of monastic life."
2. Materials: "*Kammavaca* manuscripts are written on a variety of materials, primarily on palm leaf but also on stiffened cloth, or gold, silver, metal or ivory sheets in the shape of palm leaf."
3. Script: "The Pali text is written in black lacquer in ornate Burmese characters known as 'tamarind-seed' script, also refered to as 'square' script, which differs from the usual round Burmese writing."
4. Robe-and-mother-of-pearl variant: "Some attractive and unusual *Kammavaca* may be made from discarded monastic robes thickly covered with black lacquer, with inlaid mother-of-pearl letters."
5. Ordination history: "In the 12th century, the Sihala Ordination was introduced into Burma by Chappaṭa who had studied the canon and commentaries in Sri Lanka." / 1476 mission sent to Sri Lanka; King Dhammaceti (1471–1492) built the Kalyani Sima at Pegu.
6. Five conditions for Upasampada: "Perfection of a person, Perfection of an assembly, Perfection of the Sima, Perfection of the motion, and Perfection of the Kammavaca."
7. Physical format: leaves "range in size from 50 to 60 cm in length, and from 10 to 15 cm in width"; "The leaves have two holes in them in which small bamboo sticks are usually inserted... bound between decorated binding boards"; "*Kammavaca* were usually wrapped in woven or silk wrappers, and secured with a woven ribbon and placed in a gilded box."
8. Donor inscription example: Or.12010A "records that the manuscript was a pious gift of lay devotee U Tha Hsan and his wife Ma Lun" — evidence of lay merit-commissioning.
9. Contents list of Or.12010A names nine official acts (Upasampada, Kathinadussadana, Ticivarena-avippavasa, Sima-sammuti, Therasammuti, Nama-sammuti, Vihara-kappa-bhumi-sammuti, Kuti-vatthu-sammuti, Nissaya-muti-sammuti).
10. BL shelf marks documented: Add.15289 (18th c., gilded/lacquered palm leaf), Or.12010H (18th c., ivory), Or.12010E (19th c., red palm leaf), Or.13896 (19th c., metal gilded and lacquered red), Or.12010A (19th c., lacquered cloth).

## S2. Far East Collections — "The loss of the Mandalay Palace Manuscripts in 1885"
URL: https://fareastcollections.com/the-loss-of-the-mandalay-palace-manuscripts-in-1885/ | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D2). Dealer blog; page cites Pali Text Society and a King's College London thesis; treat figures as needing corroboration for publication use.

Key facts:
1. November 1885 invasion context: British forces dethroned King Thibaw (1878–1885), exiled him to Ratnagiri, India; "widespread looting and fires swept through the royal archives."
2. U Tin (Pagan Wundauk U Tin) + palace servant U Lwin "rescued over forty bullock-cart loads of surviving documents", shipped to Chief Commissioner's office in Rangoon; recovery included "more than 21,000 parabaik".
3. James Colbeck (SPG missionary chaplain) persuaded General Prendergast to halt prize-agent sales; letter cited to Dr. Reinhold Rost of the India Office Library urging "a graceful act on the part of the army".
4. Fausbøll estimate: palace library originally held "around 1,150 manuscript volumes"; "over 800 of those works were missing".
5. Survivors split between India Office Library (London) and Bernard Free Library, Rangoon (now part of National Library of Myanmar).
6. Terminology: "the pay sa (palm leaf manuscripts used in monastic rituals)"; kammavaca in "thick, square 'tamarind-seed' script using a stiffened support... made from a variety of mediums such as discarded monastic robes, bone ash and lacquer."
7. Administrative palm leaf: strips called "cā-khyvan" used "to issue official royal appointments and court decrees."
8. Earliest documented gilt+lacquer decorated manuscript claimed to be NOT a kammavaca but a 1683 Pañcanipāta Anguttara-Aṭṭhakathā commentary (brought to light by J. A. Stewart 1934; analyzed by Noel F. Singer); borders mirror murals of Tilokaguru cave temple, Sagaing.
9. Magyi-zi origin: scholars Singer and U Nyunt Maung note "striking visual similarities between tamarind-seed lettering and the angular epigraphy found on 12th-to-14th-century Pagan stone stelae."
10. Lacquer recipe details: "Artisans mixed rice-husk ash into raw lacquer putty (thayo), bound stiffened folios using discarded monastic robes or royal garments (vat la), and occasionally blended the cremated ashes of revered monks or lay donors into the lacquer itself."

## S3. Musée Guimet — Kammavaca BG83700
URL: https://www.guimet.fr/en/our-collections/treasures-historic-library/kammavaca-burmese-manuscript | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D3)

Key facts:
1. Object: ivory kammavaca manuscript, late 17th–early 18th century, dimensions "85 cm x 8.5 m", inventory BG83700, acquired by purchase 2007.
2. "Manuscripts on ivory, that come later, appear towards the mid-18th century, when the crafting of that material developed in Burma." (Guimet dates its own piece earlier, late 17th–early 18th c.)
3. "Its text is in pâli... in square Burmese script known as 'Tamarind seeds' written on both sides with a thick black lacquer."
4. Binding/wrapping: margins "lacquered in red and gilded"; narrow wooden boards enclose sheets; ensemble wrapped in protective cloth called "robe" held by a rope or "belt".

## S4. SEACOM (BL author) — "A bar of pure gold": Shan Buddhist manuscripts
Author: Jana Igunma, British Library | 8 Dec 2025 | URL: https://seacomberlin.wordpress.com/2025/12/08/a-bar-of-pure-gold-shan-buddhist-manuscripts/ | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D4)

Key facts:
1. Shan gilt folding books look like gold bars deliberately: "the idea of pure gold rather refers in a figurative sense to the purity and the moral value of the sacred texts."
2. Shan practice: "selected texts, short extracts or translations from the Tipitaka were combined in one folding book (pap tup) for the purpose of teaching, giving sermons or chanting at ceremonies."
3. Commemorative gilt folding books are "known in Shan language as lik ho".
4. Cover tech: "Covers made from several layers of thick paper could be lacquered and gilded, with added high relief ornaments and coloured mirror-glass inlay"; rare royal examples had semi-precious stones inlaid.
5. BL Shan holdings documented: Or 15350 (Buddhadāna dīpaṇī, Chiang Mai 1913), Or 16079 (Saṅkhāra bhājanī, dated 1917, Doris Duke bequest provenance), Or 12040 (Buddhānūssati, dated 1885), Or 15353 (Nemi Jātaka, Chiang Mai 1914, 185 folds), Or 14572 (Anagatavamsa, dated 1894, over 2 kg).

## S5. Britannica — Kuthodaw Pagoda
Author: Ethan Teekah, ed.-checked | URL: https://www.britannica.com/place/Kuthodaw-Pagoda | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D5)

Key facts:
1. "inscribed across 729 marble tablets arranged within individual white stupas surrounding a central gilded pagoda"; pagoda completed 1868 under King Mindon (r. 1853–78).
2. "Work on the marble inscriptions began in 1860"; text "first reviewed by three clerics and then copied by scribes onto marble for stonemasons to chisel. Most stonemasons engraved an average of 10 lines per day."
3. Marble quarried from Sagyin Hill (~25 miles/40 km north of Mandalay), floated down Irrawaddy; each tablet ~5 ft (1.5 m) tall.
4. "A 730th tablet records the history of the project and Mindon's reign." — resolves the 729 vs 730 discrepancy found in Q2 snippets.
5. Gold inlay looted after 1885 annexation; "replaced with black ink beginning in 1892." In 2013 the Kuthodaw Inscription Shrines were added to UNESCO Memory of the World register.
6. Note: Britannica does not name the Fifth Council explicitly on this page; the Council framing appears in Q2 search snippets (havecamerawilltravel.com: "King Mindon convened the Fifth Buddhist Synod in 1871 at Kuthodaw Pagoda, where 2,400 monks recited the Pali Canon") — SNIPPET status only, corroborate in Phase B.

## S6. Vipassana Research Institute — Tipitaka Project
URL: https://www.vridhamma.org/Tipitaka-Propject | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D6)

Key facts:
1. "The most recent review, the Sixth Council, or Chatta Sangayana, was held in 1954 in Rangoon, Burma. Twenty five hundred learned bhikkhus and scholars from Burma, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, India and other countries participated."
2. Completion: "finishing its work on the full moon day of May 1956 (the 2,500th anniversary of the birth of the Buddha)".
3. VRI base text: "the authentic version as compiled in the Chattha Sangnyana (Sixth Council) in Burma during 1954-56 in the Burmese Script as the base".
4. Publication scale: "The entire Pali literature has been published in 140 volumes, each volume containing about 400 pages."
5. Digital: Chattha Sangayana CD-ROM viewable in seven scripts (Devanagari plus Roman, Sinhalese, Thai, Cambodian, Mongolian, Burmese), freely downloadable at www.tipitaka.org.
6. Tipitaka size: "more than 24 million characters in over forty printed volumes."
7. Page image credit: "Burmese-Pali Manuscript. Wellcome L0026547" — leads to Wellcome Collection imagery (Phase B lead).

## S7. UCLA MEAP — Documenting Colonial Era Monastic Collections in Former Principalities of the Shan State
Project lead: Francois Tainturier, Inya-Burma Institute | URL: https://meap.library.ucla.edu/projects/myanmar/ | Status: HARVESTED (full read, archived D7)

Key facts:
1. Survey of 74 monasteries across Taunggyi, Kengtung (Shan State, Myanmar) and Mae Hong Son (NW Thailand).
2. Wat Tong Si, Kengtung: "holds 14 large mulberry paper manuscripts, 49 small mulberry paper manuscripts, and 70 palm-leaf manuscripts."
3. Hamsi Monastery (Taunggyi area): 82 manuscripts, all palm-leaf; majority canonical texts and nissaya ("Pali textual sources written in Burmese alphabet"); wrapping textile cloths add value.
4. Linguistic diversity finding: manuscripts in Tai Long, Tai Khuen, Tai Yuan, Myanmar scripts plus "many manuscripts composed in Pa-O, Tai Nuea, and possibly other languages."
5. Chronology: collections span "from the mid-19th century (with very rare cases of early 19th-century palm-leaf manuscripts) to the early 1970s."
6. Shared custodianship: manuscripts also cared for by local "zare, laymen in charge of chanting texts on special occasions."
7. Risk context: "From the late 1980's to the early 1990's (and occasionally in the present), Southeast Asian manuscripts were often sold to tourists due to the material's historic desirability and their vulnerability to theft."
