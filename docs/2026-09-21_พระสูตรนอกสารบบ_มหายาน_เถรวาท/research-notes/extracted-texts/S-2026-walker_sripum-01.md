All three compositions are older than the manuscripts that record them; 
Nantho was composed in 1736, Supriti likely between the mid-­seventeenth 
and mid-­eighteenth centuries, and Mahachat possibly in 1482. Although only 
the first two manuscripts are definitively the products of Ayutthaya scribal 
culture, all three share a set of scribal features that were exclusively reserved 
for the kham luang variety of Indic-­Siamese bitexts prized by the royal courts 
of Ayutthaya (1431–1767) and early Bangkok (1782–present). Even the 
Mahachat manuscript from 1809 clearly follows much of the earlier paradigm 
laid down by Nantho and Supriti, reinforcing the notion that royal scribes of 
the early nineteenth century sought continuity with Ayutthaya-­era norms.

alternates between two kinds of compositional modes: exposition, where 
complete lines, phrases, or even longer passages in Indic are followed imme-

in Thai or other Southeast Asian languages; the latter is known by several 
terms, the most widespread being nissaya. Exposition and exegesis are 
techniques found in many, if not most, bitexts throughout the region. What 
is distinct about all three manuscripts is the clarity with which their para-
textual features accentuate not only the differences between Indic and ver-

The next text we consider is the Sanskrit-­Siamese Supriti. As was the case 
for Nantho, only a single manuscript survives. Unfortunately, however, this 
manuscript is fragmentary, and the beginning, end, and original covers are 
missing; no authorial or scribal colophon remains.11 There is therefore no trace 
of the date or author, much less the genre term kham luang. Even the title, 
composed of the Buddhist narrative category of jātaka combined with the name 
of the lead character, a king styled Suprītidhammarāja who gives away his three 
children to a brahman as an act of extreme generosity, is merely a modern 
reconstruction. The Sanskrit portions are not citations of a single preexisting 
source; some are borrowed from existing verse collections while others are the 
invention of the author. This is, by all accounts, an extraordinary work. 
Scholars of Thai literature, led by Niyada Laosunthon, argue that Supriti 
belongs in the kham luang genre by dint of its linguistic, metrical, and codi-
cological features.12 We likewise understand this manuscript as a fragment 
of a kham luang composition; we contend that the use of a distinct bitextual 
structure, undergirded by specific paratextual features, confirms this genre 
label. Paleographically speaking, the leporello dates from the late Ayutthaya 
period, perhaps around the same time as the Nantho manuscript; the Fine

Exposition and Exegesis in Kham Luang Bitexts
Indic-­vernacular bitexts are bilingual compositions, arranged in an inter-
phrasal or interlinear format, that interweave portions in Pali or Sanskrit 
with portions in a local vernacular language.20
In mainland Southeast Asia, such compositions were the mainstay of 
most Buddhist homiletic and scholastic genres between the sixteenth and 
nineteenth centuries.21 With regard to the paratextual dimensions of bitexts 
in manuscript form, scribal conventions in Burma, Cambodia, Lanna, and 
Laos were relatively conservative; the Indic and vernacular portions of bitexts 
in these contexts cannot be distinguished by an untrained eye, as they are 
almost always written in the same script and ink color, with only some

the Indic and Thai portions but also between two compositional modes: 
exposition and exegesis. 
Exposition is by far the simpler mode: a structure where a passage of Pali 
or Sanskrit, in its original wording and sequence, is followed directly by a 
vernacular translation. The length of the Indic-­language passage may vary, 
from as short as a brief phrase to as long as several intricate and complex 
sentences woven together into an extended paragraph. Most passages used 
in exposition fall between these two extremes. The most common way to 
divide Indic prose for the purposes of exposition is at the end of a complete 
sentence or clause with a real or implied verb. The final verb of a clause need 
not be finite; participle forms such as gerunds are common in clause-­final 
position. Indic prose sentences often consist of a string of many clauses that 
end in a participle before the sentence itself concludes with a finite verb. In 
exposition, a sentence can thus be broken into several parts, each ending 
with a final participle or finite verb. For Indic verse, which generally appears 
in stanzas of four lines, the ordinary style for exposition entails dividing the 
text into discrete passages after each line. Whether division is made after a 
clause of prose or a line of verse, what follows is a vernacular translation of 
the preceding portion in Pali or Sanskrit.
For the three Indic-­Siamese bitexts discussed here, the vernacular portion 
is always in verse, regardless of whether the preceding Indic passage was in 
verse or prose. The most common verse format is the rai (rā̀y) meter, an old 
and relatively flexible structure in which the final syllable of the preceding 
line rhymes with a selected syllable in following line. There are various forms 
of rai: the rai boran (rā̀y pōrāṇ) variant predominates in Supriti, while rai yao 
(rā̀y yāv) is preferred in Nantho; Mahachat uses a mixture of different rai 
styles.22 The exposition mode allows for other, more complex verse styles,

compositional modes for Indic-­vernacular bitexts in Southeast Asia. The 
three kham luang texts are exclusively composed of these two modes. Though 
expository passages are more frequent and therefore take up a greater 
percentage of each composition overall, the less-­frequent exegetical passages 
are always the longest and most elaborate. Part of the literary expansion of 
the exegetical parts is due to the addition of many new Pali or Sanskrit 
words and phrases. But the use of an interlinked and impressively extended 
verse of rai for each exegetical passage plays a key role as well. These exegeti-

including beginnings, endings, and other major transitions. The overall 
structure of each of the three kham luang works is determined by the 
sequence of different forms of exposition and exegesis throughout the 
composition. As we will show in the following section, the paratextual 
features of the manuscripts themselves serve to highlight and reinforce these 
aspects of bitextual structure. They make immediately clear what portions 
are in each language and mode.
The Nantho Manuscript: Color, Script, Spacing,  
and Punctuation
The codex unicus of Nantho offers the most straightforward example of how 
the bitextual structure can be emphasized through specific scribal features.

For an uninitiated reader, the most striking visual feature of the manuscript 
is its use of three colors of ink: black, red, and gold. The lattermost is 
restricted to the following: the opening page, including a Sanskrit-­language 
aspiration to buddhahood; a Pali invocation to the Buddha; the citation of 
the single Pali root verse that inspired the portion of the ṭīkā commentary 
on which Nantho is based; and the royal names and titles of the author in 
both Pali and Thai, which appear in the first exegetical passage, the closing 
exegetical passage, and part of a versified colophon at the very end.31 Gold is 
thus reserved for the portions of the text with the highest status: those 
connected to the Buddha and to the royal author. Text in black and red ink, 
however, appears on almost every page of the manuscript. Gold portions 
notwithstanding, the linguistic distribution of the text follows an ironclad 
rule: passages in Pali are in red ink, those in Siamese are in black. Hence a 
user of the manuscript is immediately reminded that the work they are 
reading or reciting is structured as an Indic-­vernacular bitext. 
Such blatant division of language by color is rare in bitextual manuscripts 
from Southeast Asia. While multiple colors of ink appear in interlinear 
annotations, the only interphrasal examples that divide languages by hue in 
this way date from the late Ayutthaya period, including Nantho, Supriti, and 
the oldest manuscript of Rachowat chadok (Rājōvād jātak), copied in 1725.32 
Rubrication for emphasis, including of certain punctuation marks, appears 
in some palm-­leaf manuscripts from the Ayutthaya period as well, but the 
practice was not as widespread as in India, Nepal, or Tibet.33 By placing the 
entirety of their Indic passages in red, the scribes who crafted the Nantho, 
Supriti, and 1725 Rachowat chadok manuscripts were emphasizing the works’ 
status as bitexts.

key question is why. Our contention is that these numerals aid the reader in 
understanding the bitextual structure. The numeral 1 appears only before a 
section of line-­by-­line verse exposition. Moreover, it appears just once at the 
onset of the expository passage in question; no matter how many stanzas of

The 1809 “Nakhòn” Manuscript: Musical Notation  
and the Logic of the Cadences
Early Bangkok manuscripts of Mahachat, particularly those of the 
“Thotsaphòn,” “Kuman,” and “Nakhòn” chapters, maintain almost all of the 
distinct scribal features of Supriti. The only exceptions are the loss of color 
distinctions between Indic and Siamese portions (both languages are written 
in yellow-­gold ink on black paper) and the lack of the rare tha marker. The 
remaining paratexts that highlight the bitextual structure remain in place: 
Khòm script for Pali paired with Thai script for Siamese; strict conventions 
on spacing and punctuation; and the 1, 2, 4, and long markers, following the 
exact same usage as in Supriti. The royal scribes of the early nineteenth 
century, as part of their broader efforts to restore Ayutthaya literature, 
managed to maintain most of the key scribal practices for bitexts from a 
prior age. But many Mahachat manuscripts, including early examples of the 
“Thotsaphòn” and “Kuman” chapters and the 1809 “Nakhòn” we focus on 
here, add one additional paratextual feature that ties the bitextual structure 
together in revelatory ways: musical notations or cantillation marks, typically 
in white, that prescribe chanting melodies in apparent detail.

Conclusion
The manuscripts of the three kham luang bitexts studied here reveal increasing 
layers of paratextual complexity. The Nantho manuscript uses color, script, 
spacing, and the fòng man punctuation mark to highlight the differences 
between the Pali and Siamese portions as well as between the expository and 
exegetical passages. Supriti and Mahachat each have a similar compositional 
structure, composed of distinct chanting sections delimited by the long 
marker. The Supriti manuscript, like Nantho, uses color, script, spacing, and 
punctuation to make the Sanskrit and Siamese portions stand apart from 
one another. The additional deployment of the markers 1, 2, 4, long and tha 
clarify which portions are exposition (those following 1, long, and tha) and 
which are exegesis (those following 2 and 4, comprised of a full-­ or half-­verse 
citation plus its analysis). In addition, the contrasts between long (before

Except for the musical notation, the paratextual conventions that guided 
the scribes’ creation of these manuscripts between the mid-­eighteenth and 
early nineteenth centuries were already in place at the end of the Ayutthaya 
period. Bangkok-­era scribes of Mahachat, presumably recognizing the impor-
tance of its bitextual structures, maintained these conventions as best they 
could; the 1809 “Nakhòn” manuscript reveals that such conventions were 
rigorously followed even before Rama II’s restoration of Mahachat in 1814. 
Siamese scholars of the period did not necessarily use specific terms to 
describe the distinctions between exposition and exegesis. But those who 
copied and edited these kham luang in the late Ayutthaya and early Bangkok 
eras were clearly cognizant of the intricate structures behind each bitext and 
the importance of clarifying these structures through visual and sonic means. 
The paratextual features analyzed in this article are therefore key to appreciat-
ing both the labor of the scribes and the bitexts they transmitted. The study 
of classical Siamese literature, including Indic-­vernacular compositions, has 
been divorced for too long from the manuscripts themselves. What the scribes 
left us is far more than the words of the poets: they also equipped us with 
the tools to read them.
