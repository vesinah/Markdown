Encountering weikza in Buddhist Burma
The Biographical Account of a Ritual Specialist as an Echo to
“Tantricking”
Bénédicte Brac de la Perrière
CNRS-CASE (Centre Asie du Sud-Est)
abstract
In this ethnographic study, I examine how Medaw, a Buddhist Burmese ritual
specialist, claims karmic relationships to naga and describes her life trajectory as encounters
with beings known as weikza. These beings pertain to a Burmese field of practice which
can be examined in the light of Lorea and Singh’s conceptualization of “tantricking.” With
this purpose, I analyze the stories Medaw recounted during the so-called Burmese political
transition (2011–2021) as a biographical account. Particularly, I trace how, in the course
of her practice as a ritual specialist, she built her relationship with weikza beings as a
unique one, qualifying her distinctiveness in contrast to monasticism and to other ritual
specialists. In so doing, weikza emerge as beings whose specific agency and ontology—more
readily workable with the brand of urban Buddhism that has developed in contemporary
Burma—give access to followers and politically well-connected clients eager to tap into
their sources of potency. This speaks to the interstitial location of the weikza field of
practice in the overall Buddhist religious field in Burma.

to all. However, the fact that knowledge would not be accessible directly and would only be
transmitted through specific spiritual lineages or through inspiration by invisible masters,
means that the weikza path could be considered as esoteric from a sociological point of view,

Some scholars have also discussed the family resemblances observed in weikza practices

which the “Tantric Buddhism” label has been readily applied (de Bernon 1998; Crosby 2000,

powerful means to obtain embodied knowledge at the margins of the Buddhist center of power.
This happened during the same span of time and as an alternative to the development of
“insight meditation” (wipathana, P. vipassanâ), into which people started to engage as a form
of mind-based mysticism, making Kate Crosby ask: “To what extent, then, are the features that
have come to be seen as unorthodox [the weikza practices] part of a repertoire of practices
that pervaded the region only to inhabit the margins with changing ideas of what constitutes
knowledge?” (2014, xxiii). Indeed, the contrast between wipathana and weikza practices in
Burma appears to mirror the dialogical rhetoric of the Western “scientific” and the Eastern
“magical” (Crosby 2014, xxiv), which has since been questioned by the critics of Orientalism.

We actually have earlier traces of prototypical weikza practice in the presence of Burmese
[4]
figures, such as the medieval Ari, remembered as a sect of heterodox monks in historiography,
or the red-dressed flying wizards (zawgyi) represented in many “esoteric” manuscripts dealing
with the disciplines they mastered. Notwithstanding Duroiselle’s early mention of the historical
Ari monks as pertaining to Tantric Buddhism in his very title (1915), the weikza field does
not seem to qualify immediately as Tantric studies, unlike the case studies gathered in Lorea
and Singh’s edited book (2023). At the least, these practices do not seem to have surfaced in
the academic field of Tantric studies. This may be due to the lack of inclusion, at large, of
Burmese studies in global academia, despite the recent increase of interest for the weikza field
(apart from Brac de la Perrière, Rozenberg, and Turner 2014; Brac de la Perrière 2014; see
also, among the main references, Foxeus 2011; Rozenberg 2015; Patton 2018). If “Tantric” is
obviously not an emic category in the Burmese context, not more than in many Tibetan and
South Asian contexts, according to Lorea and Singh, it does not follow that weikza practices
may not be considered as a case that could be illuminated by bringing it along with other
evolving cases dealt with in the Tantric studies.
My aim with this paper is to study the current field of practice that the Burmese identify
[5]
as the weikza path (weikza lan), which seems to have crystallized as such starting at the
beginning of nineteenth century. I will do so, building on the frame envisioned by the editors
of The Ethnography of Tantra (Lorea and Singh 2023), by focusing on one ethnographic
entanglement. Rather than analyzing the weikza path as a Tantric readymade tradition, I will
look at contemporary processes that allow the continuous differentiation of this interstitial
field in Burmese Buddhism. These processes, I argue, are similar to “tantricking,” a Lorea and
Singh’s concept that they describe as “an ever changing and complex array of things people
do” (2023, 9), in order to “disambiguate the problem of self-definition” (2023, 10). I will use it
to express the idea of the weikza path as an endless formative process of ritual empowerment.
It could be paralleled to the older formative processes of Tantrism highlighted by Ronald
Davidson (2002), who describes them as taking shape in early medieval India in reaction to
new feudal conditions, in a period specialists call the Tantric Age, as highly developed ritual
esoteric systems aiming at the acquisition of supernatural powers through which phenomenal
existence may be manipulated. But, so far, if these processes may be paralleled, no lineage of
any sort can be traced between Indian Tantrism and the weikza path.
Some fundamental features of the Burmese weikza field of practice need to be outlined at
[6]
this point and tentatively situated in the recent work of the polythetic definition of Tantra
as a “plural yet single tradition” that was synthetized by Lorea and Singh (2023, 17–22).
Weikza from the Pâli vijja means “knowledge,” as already stated. In our context, it refers
more precisely to the disciplines, or the crafts as formulated by Foxeus (2011), in which the
practitioners of the path may specialize in. This specialization occurs while engaging in strict
religious observances until practitioners become virtuosi, which may manifest through the
acquisition of supernatural powers (iddhi/theiddhi). As formulated by Lorea and Singh about
the Tantric way, it “provides both a path for individual enlightenment and an empowerment
for the alleviation of worldly suffering through the activities of Tantric healers” (ibid., 16).
Ordinary Burmese talk of the kind of perfected beings thus produced as weikza, which is an
abbreviated form of weikzadho, the “bearers” of the weikza disciplines. These “bearers” may
become the object of specific cults: the main historical weikza worshiped today in Burma are
Bomingaung, who disappeared in 1952, and Boboaung, who lived during the beginning of the
nineteenth century.

Disciplines practiced by the weikza path practitioners include alchemy, medicine, magic
[7]
squares (in), powerful rituals (yantra), numerology, and astrology,5 which all pertain to
mundane knowledge (lawki pinnya). These disciplines used to be part of monastic teaching
curriculums until they were banned during the nineteenth-century reforms of the Sangha
(Dhammasami 2004). The weikza field emerged through this process of exclusion from the
monastic education because of the very “mundanity” of these disciplines. To engage in the
weikza path, practitioners must familiarize themselves with one or more of these disciplines,
rigorously observe the five to eight moral precepts (thila) of “lay” Buddhists, and practice
meditation on a daily basis, usually calm abiding meditation (thamatha) but also, nowadays,
insight meditation (wipathana).6
This engagement is a trying endeavor which often implies a complete change of mindset,
[8]
lifestyle and morals, which may be undertaken under the guidance of a master (hsaya) or
not. Weikza beings can then serve as instructors, or inspiring beings, so that one esoteric
form of knowledge transmission is in order and that it projects the new practitioner in a new
“spiritual kinship” (Lorea and Singh 2023, 17). One may note that ritualized initiation is not
necessarily required, except for the healers who practice exorcism in a congregation, as is the
case in the Manawseittopad organization (Brac de la Perrière 2017b). The usual engagement
of practitioners in the weikza path is marked by vows of observance taken in front of a Buddha
image, as signaled by Schober who equates these vows to “a different kind of ordination” (2014,
38),7 challenging the monastic ordination in the Burmese Sangha. While this point, among
others, certainly distinguishes the weikza from the monastic practice, I would consider it as an
absence of ordination rather than equate it to “a different kind of ordination,” or even as an
absence of ritualized initiation, signaling the weikza field as a Buddhist lay field of practice.8
Although some monks do engage in the weikza path, it places them in a somewhat awkward
position with regard to the monastic rule (Vinaya), and does not preclude the mundane status
of the associated practices: exorcism, healing, or astrology.9
Through this intensive practice and complete dedication to the path, practitioners may
[9]
become virtuosi and acquire all sorts of powers (iddhi/theiddhi): concrete powers such as
long life, ubiquity, invincibility or healing; and more intangible powers such as the ability
to master dat, a kind of cosmic energy. Séances of inspiration by recognized weikza, such as
Bomingaung or Boboaung, are held by leaders of their cults called medaw or bodaw, their agents
in this world. The rituals they are thus instructed to perform are said “to ride energy” (dat si),
while chasing nefarious beings from a suffering body would be “to strike [with] the energy”
(dat yaik). By learning to master this cosmic energy through intensive meditation exercises,
practitioners may eventually exit out of the cycle of rebirth—thus becoming a htwetyapauk,
“one who got out,” so to speak. They are then supposed to retreat to a mysterious place called
the Mahamyaing jungle, which may be located in the Himalayas or in some remote location in
Burma, from where they remain available to help fellow Buddhists still struggling to overcome

Weikza thus denotes, all at once, the disciplines practiced along the path, the “bearers” of
[10]
these disciplines, the perfected beings who are the object of cults from ordinary practitioners,
and advanced practitioners themselves. That is, it functions as an indicial signifier of the
field as a whole. Weikza lan is a lay Buddhist domain that contrasts with the monastic field
particularly in the following ways: it is not subject to ordination; it avoids religious donations;
and it claims instead to rescue Buddhist people through their various powers. In particular,
weikza profess to defend Buddha’s teachings within the Burmese dispensation (thathana/P.
sâsana).
However, the weikza banner gathers all sorts of practitioners, specialists of various kinds
[11]
whose connection to Buddhist soteriological ends may be more or less explicit and who may
operate in variously structured settings. It stands for a very heterogeneous field encompassing
astrologers whose authority as consultants may or may not depend on a lineage of learning
(Candier and Ferguson 2022); the multifarious cults to recognized weikza such as Bomingaung
and Boboaung, whose leaders, medaw and bodaw, have developed a particular connection to
those beings and may thus build a following of devotees for whom they act as ritual specialists;
and healers or exorcists (payyawga hsaya/ma), who are organized in congregations in which
membership may be regulated by initiation processes and whose healing powers originate
in their founding weikza to whom they address a specific cult (Brac de la Perrière 2017b;
Rozenberg 2014). This list serves only to give an idea of the diversity of the field, which lacks
any overarching hierarchical structure.
To consider how the weikza field may be paralleled to features characterizing Tantric
[12]
traditions, as synthetized by Lorea and Singh (2023, 17–22), we can first highlight the plural
ways in which a practitioner may seek enlightenment. Secondly, we can note the fact that
ritual empowerment serves to rescue Buddhist fellows and sustain the Buddhist dispensation.
Weikza practitioners cultivate mundane disciplines besides Buddhist virtues to access liberation
and act upon the world through a system of associations. In this way, they relate to previous
perfected beings (weikzadho) and develop a particular attitude towards the cosmos “whereby
the body and the universe reflect each other in an all-embracing net of correspondences that
tie the microcosm and the macrocosm” (2023, 18). Healing, confronting death, “departing
from this world while alive” (ashin htwek), oracular possession as well as negative possession,
in the context of exorcism, are all common features of the weikza field that allow to speak at
least of family resemblances with Tantric traditions.
The weikza field is indeed highly heterogeneous, thriving in an interstitial space, in between
[13]
the mainstream societal delineations of Buddhism, at the juncture of the monastic institution
and state power. It is mainly a lay field of ritual specialists cultivating all sorts of powers in
ways that, at times, foster politics of control by governmental authorities. To say the least,
official attitudes towards this field are equivocal: the powers that weikza practitioners claim to
harness thanks to their connections with their “superhuman” or “metapersonal” counterparts
and their mastery of cosmic forces (dat) are both a source of suspicion and envy among those

in Burma, which will become clear from the biography of the medaw I will present here.
For now, a reminder of such a case, which surfaced in 2004, will suffice to make the
[14]
point. Khin Nyunt was then the almighty Prime Minister and celebrated patron of most of the
spectacular state rituals. When he was suddenly dismissed, a famous ritual specialist from
the weikza path, known as Bodaw Than Hla, was put under house arrest along with him. The
entire ritual career of this specialist had depended on his association with Khin Nyunt’s pagoda
refurbishing program betson, as it was then called. Indeed, the kind of potency required for
such undertakings as founding or renovating a Buddhist sanctuary is key to mundane political
power. The pair formed by Khin Nyunt and Than Hla is one of the most well-known recent
examples of the mutually constitutive link between political and ritual powers in contemporary
Burma—thus demonstrating how a powerful political figure may align himself closely with a
potent ritual specialist from the weikza path, as a partner of sorts, and how one’s fall inevitably
brings down the other.11
Biography as an Ethnographical Entanglement
I will now discuss the trajectory of a medaw, a woman weikza ritual specialist whose powers
[15]
come from her devotion to Bomingaung. In my view, our evolving exchanges came to constitute
over time a particularly productive ethnographic entanglement. I met her while visiting
Bomingaung’s main place of cult, Mount Popa—a volcanic formation located in the heart of
the Burmese central plain—on the date of the weikza’s “way-out” anniversary celebration
(thwekbwe), in September 2011. I will refer to her as “Medaw” to preserve her anonymity.
Medaw means “noble mother” and is used in contrast to the masculine counterpart bodaw,
“noble great-father,” to address weikza path ritual specialists. Furthermore, medaw is also a
term used to address other feminine invisible beings, or metapersons, which speaks of the
ontological continuity between these beings and ritual specialists of their cults.

[16]
the celebration of Min Mahagiri, the chief of the 37 tutelary spirits (nat), a celebration that
was previously held there in December and where the Burmese pantheon’s spirit mediums
used to gather (Brac de la Perrière, Rozenberg, and Turner 2014). The newly popular event,

distinct of the monastic robes. The atmosphere was frenzied, as the village of Mt Popa was

claiming intimate connections to the weikza. On the day before the start of the event, it was

environment. In this context, to meet a medaw at this festival was to experience the presence
of the perfected being through her connection to him: and for her, welcoming a newcomer in
her entourage meant testifying to this connection by recounting how she came to establish
it—or, rather, how she was chosen by the weikza to act as his agent in this world.
I was there with Laylay, my longtime friend and companion in field research trips, sharing
[17]
both the hardships of travelling to remote places and a curiosity for the weirdest ritual
specialists who hosted us in their encampments. A broker by profession, originating from the

company, she had always used her brilliant social abilities to the benefit of my various inquiries
into the Burmese ritual world, while personally keeping some distance from them. Over time
and in accordance to the evolution of the political situation in Burma, she became increasingly
involved as a pro-democracy activist, using her engagement with the ritual specialists to
inquire on the local people’s conditions—though she remained skeptical about their mundane
actions.
Medaw, a rather elegant woman in her fifties, was seated on the staircase leading to the
[18]
top of the volcanic chimney on which stood the Buddhist sanctuary visited by the pilgrims.
It was a prime location—both to be seen and to observe the festival. As soon as we arrived
there and inquired about the leader of the group of devotees seated there, we were directed
to her. In response to our queries about her presence at this site, she began, in this public
context, to tell us about her life—specifically how she came to interact almost freely with the
weikza. In other words, in this new ritual event, she offered a sketch of her biography as a
‘spiritual career.’ Laylay and I discovered together the novelty of the role Medaw had invented
for herself, all the more captivated by the fact that she immediately revealed herself to be
both deeply engaged in the weikza cosmic world and in Burmese political and social dynamics.
As a genre, biography (attuppatti) is linked in Burma to the Buddhist biographical imagi-
[19]
nation (Schober 1997), which makes of karma—that is the retribution to the previous lives’
actions—the main explanatory principle. However, knowledge of past lives depends on one’s
spiritual attainments, which cannot be self-asserted during one’s lifetime, rendering auto-
biography an almost unworkable genre in this context (Brac de la Perrière 2022a). On the
one hand, Medaw’s oral account of her spiritual career as an explanation of her presence
at Bomingaung’s anniversary does not precisely fit in the biographical genre (attuppatti), al-
though her claim of karmic links with perfected beings already gestures towards self-assertive
spiritual achievements. On the other hand, this biographical genre could fall under Bourdieu’s
well-known critique of the biographical method, which questions the notion of life prede-
termination ([1986] 1994). While Bourdieu’s critique primarily concerns the biographer’s
intention, namely the imposition of preconceived meanings into a life, it has been mitigated

loaded with the energy (dat) mobilized in the specialist’s rituals. In this way, shrines are an
immediate testimony of the practitioner’s ritual efficiency. The front room is thus transfigured
into a potent room (dat hkan) where consultants are received by the specialist, who sits under
the shrines and holds séances for them, there.
By observing Medaw’s shrine room and asking about its evolving contents, when she was
[22]
alone and free to talk, Laylay and I gradually learned more about her life, the circumstances
that led her to the weikza path and the nature of her ritual undertakings. Over the years, her
ritual activities became increasingly tied to her high-ranking connections within the official
administration, and her conversations with Laylay grew more and more political, with both
women assessing the other’s position through commentaries of Medaw’s rituals. Indeed, if
meeting Medaw at Mt Popa had been a way to experience the presence of Bomingaung through
her connection to him, then entering Medaw’s shrine room was entering her spiritual world,
and, indirectly, coming to understand her position within the political world as well.

Encountering weikza and naga as Ritual Expertise
In 2011, at Mt Popa, Medaw claimed a karmic link with four spiritual female dragons, called
[23]
nagama, beings able to make themselves visible to their affiliates under the guise of beautiful
young women. These figures served as the “treasure guardians” (thaik) of four main Buddhist
sanctuaries in Burma, those of Kyaikkemi, Botataung, Shwezayan and Popa.15 As in other
places in Southeast Asia, the devotion to naga has known a significant development in Burma
since the nineties, particularly among young urbanites.16 As a way of introduction, Medaw
told us how she came to encounter her naga “sisters” when she visited their sites as a pilgrim.
She recounted numerous anecdotes of how she saw them appearing as beautiful young ladies
at different times. To “see” them meant to her that she had met them and shared their life
in a previous life, which constitutes a karmic link. It also suggests the intention to see these
usually invisible beings by going as a pilgrim to their sites, akin to practices of visualization.
Later on, we would understand that these sightings account for the specific ritual roles she
fulfills in preeminent religious sites such as the naga medaw Mya Nan Nwe temple next to the
stupa of Botataung, in Yangon, which has known tremendous ritual development since the
beginning of the 2000s (Brac de la Perrière 2022b).
Medaw also told us about the three episodes that urged her to engage in the weikza path and
[24]
thus determined her spiritual career. Before she started any practice, as she was visiting the
Shwedagon pagoda, the main Buddhist sanctuary in Yangon, she saw a statue of Bomingaung
wink at her among the numerous paraphernalia exposed by sellers of religious artefacts. She
bought the statue, which still serves as the main representation of this weikza in her potent
room. Later, while she was still living in the Shan states, she once was advised in her dreams
that thieves had intruded in the house. Thirdly, while she was travelling without any money
left to main religious sites, a kind of lonely wandering travel (gambari kheyi) that marks
the engagement in the weikza path, someone offered to pay for her return ticket. One can
understand these three tiny incidents as incitements to take part in the path in three ways: the
‘election’ by Bomingaung, the ability to see and hear beings that other people don’t see and
hear, and the protection one benefits from the weikza. These elements are very common in the
practitioners’ descriptions of their engagement in the path.
Medaw was the daughter of a military man and had married an army man older than she
[25]
was, from whom she got three children. When I met her, they were living in a populous
ward in Yangon (Kyaungmyaung), since her husband’s military career had known a serious
backdrop following the 1988 political crisis. I met a number of weikza specialists whose lives
had similarly been disrupted after the events of 1988, and who had then turned to the weikza
path because they had been deprived of any access to previous power circles. Most often, they
were already practitioners of one kind of esoteric practice, as the link between political power

technique of inspired possession, in séances of cosmic energy mastering (dat si), which she
holds at home in her “room of cosmic energies” (dat hkan).
Indeed, when at home, she holds a consultation formally free of charge, for devotee-clients
[26]
coming to her to get advice on all sorts of matters. However, consultants invariably put a
standardized amount of money in a silver bowl left for that purpose at the end of the exchange.
This amount of money is not considered as a payment, nor as a religious donation, but rather as
an offering to pay respect to the weikza (puzaw bwe), as is the norm for this kind of specialist.17
Medaw responds to the consultants’ demands by “hearing the voice of Bomingaung,” a
[27]
séance of oracular possession (dat si), so to say. While sitting on a cushion under her shrines,
she enters into a state of possession that contrasts, in Burma, with danced possession (nat ka)
which is the mark of the tutelary spirits and of spirit mediums (natkadaw). Comparatively,
signs of the change of state allowing her to hear the advice of the weikza are discrete, and may
include sitting with crossed legs, lightning a cigar and slightly dwindling the head, changes
in her voice, moving from informal conversation with her devotee-clients to voicing the
weikza’s recommendations amidst the smoke of the cigar. Despite the minute change of state,
the consultants immediately notice it and listen while joining their hands as a mark of their
profound respect to the weikza. After having transmitted the weikza’s message, Medaw recovers,
and seems not to know what happened. She then asks her clients about what has been said
and eventually comments it. She says she helps and heals people through her practice, as
exorcists do, but rather than the violent exorcism, she uses a kind of distinctive possession by
the weikza, a possession more inclined to inspiration—that is oracular possession—than to
possession dances.
There are gender implications in Medaw’s positioning compared to that of her family lineages
[28]
and to spirit possession: exorcism—that is, expulsing spiritual agencies from a patient’s body
by making use of some of a weikza’s protective power—is a masculine role in Burma, performed
with authority and force (Brac de la Perrière, Rozenberg, and Turner 2014; Brac de la Perrière
2018). On the other hand, caring for people through a weikza’s agency, such as that of
Bomingaung, is neither a feminine nor a masculine role. In case the technique used is a kind
of possession, such as in Medaw’s case, the possibility that a perfected being may enter a
mere human body is a matter of debate, all the more so if it is a woman’s body. However,
it is rather common to see women performing this inspiration-like possession. By contrast,
spirit possession as it is institutionalized in the 37 Lords cult and identifiable through dances
appears as a formally feminine role in Burma, as spirit mediums are understood to be “female
spouses of male spirits” (natkadaw). Although there are a lot of variations around this scheme,
such as the involvement of men in the higher ranked ritual roles, or the increasing number of
transgender spirit mediums, being possessed by a spiritual agency is ideally a feminine role
(Brac de la Perrière, Rozenberg, and Turner 2014). Finally, the phrase designating the seances

Accumulating Cosmic Energy (dat) and Capitalizing on Political
Power
During the years we followed Medaw’s activities, from 2011 to around 2019, Laylay and I
[30]
came across one executive member of the military proxy party and some ministers or high
officials of the Yangon government, among her devotee-clients. Laylay would immediately
signal them to me, as these people, who were holding rather high governmental positions,
were able to grant authorizations to Medaw’s ambitious ritual undertakings and to finance
them. Medaw envisioned and talked about rituals such as pagodas foundations or large homage
rituals at Buddhist sites known for their potency, and she spoke about this ritual activity as
thathana pyu—that is, to contribute to the “material dispensation” of Buddhism. Indeed, the
construction of Buddhist sanctuaries requires spiritual protection and specific knowledge that
pertains to the weikza field (see Tosa Keiko 2014).
For instance, Medaw proudly told us about how she managed, during the first transitional
[31]
government (Thein Sein’s government, 2011–2016), to build a pagoda at the start of the new
express road connecting Yangon to Mandalay through Naypyidaw. This pagoda was built
because the death toll by car accidents was considered too high on this road, and something
had to be done for the wandering souls of these people whose death was considered untimely
and violent. She planned this pagoda at the demand of the minister in charge of the road’s
construction and on the advice of Bomingaung and another weikza, to take care of these
victim’s souls produced by violent death through tightening them in the built structure of the
sanctuary. This kind of pagoda is called, paradoxically, akywot lot hpeya, a “releasing” pagoda
(from kywot- to severe links and lot- to free), meaning that the dead souls are thus released
of their endless roaming around. During the years 2015 and 2016, Medaw was constantly
travelling to the site to supervise the construction and organize consecration rituals.
However, the change of government following the 2015 democratic electoral victory took
[32]
her by surprise, as she was against the victory of Aung San Suu Kyi, the longtime leader
of opposition to the Burmese juntas. Sometime before, she had told us that she had got a
message from Bomingaung, according to which “a woman could not govern the country, or the
religion would be destroyed” and she had performed powerful rituals (yadaya) to impede this.
She had also asked Laylay to tell the democrats not to perform any yadaya to win elections.
“That should not be done!” she said. While Medaw assured us that she did not involve herself
in politics and only obeyed the weikza’s instructions, Laylay answered that she did politics
purposely, for the people’s sake, and that she had devoted herself to that mission, using the
expression “to donate ones’ body” (hkanda hlu) and that she had even experienced jail. At this
point, Medaw got into oracular possession by Bomingaung, who recommended some rituals to
Laylay for her personal protection.
Nevertheless, the democrats won the November 2015 general elections and Aung San Suu
[33]
Kyi eventually took the lead of the new government. Medaw soon adjusted to the new situation
and planned the foundation of another pagoda at the other end of the road, to the south
of Mandalay, to take care of the wandering souls of the other way, so that she would have
founded, she told me, one pagoda under each successive government. Bomingaung had come
to tell her to do her work without worrying anymore about politics. She just had to find new
sponsors for her undertakings, which she eventually found among high executive officers of
a military-linked bank. She then spent more and more time at this new place and met new
supporting followers around Mandalay.

Her spiritual career was encapsulated in her “room of energy” (dat hkan), where ritual
[34]
artefacts accumulated according to her ritual undertakings. At every visit, I noticed new
elements brought back from her ritual journeys, accompanying pilgrims to Bodhgaya or
Ayuttaya, or new installations to impede the political situation, on which she would readily
comment. This is how a poster of the previous royal couple of Thailand appeared on the wall
after a trip there, or a turban was suddenly topping the main statue of Bomingaung when

headdress “because the turban was a symbol of political power.” This was explicitly done to
help the party then in power, the Union State and Development Party (a proxy of the military)
to keep their political supremacy.
For those belonging to the adverse political party, such as Laylay, it was definitely a yadaya,
[35]
that is a ritual used to stay in power against the people’s will in what most people think is a
non-ethical way. Both women, Medaw and Laylay, were perfectly aware of their respective
position, particularly during the 2015 electoral period when one was performing what looked
like yadaya for the military proxy party to stay in power, and the other was engaging in activism
to have the democrats win, to the point of being incarcerated. While she often commented
on the political situation, Medaw denied her implication in politics: she claimed only to be
working for the dispensation of Buddhism, for the Burmese people. Discussions between the
two of them could be civil, as well as lively, each one seemingly interested in what the other
knew about the situation from their respective expertise, weikza rituals or political activism.
Observing them and the way they were actually making their worlds communicate through
the weikza was an amazing instance of the entanglement of spiritual and political worlds in
ritual life.
Concluding Comments
Laylay inquired about specific situations through ritual specialists, while I inquired about ritual
[36]
specialists through her political involvement. Such were the particulars of the ethnographic
entanglement presented here. This was how the political connections of the ritual specialist
and the way Medaw managed to carve out a distinctive ritual role in the weikza field, out of
her individualist practice, have been uncovered.
The record of Medaw’s ritual activities, which we discovered through this entanglement,
[37]
says a lot about the importance of studying the agents and networks mobilized by a ritual
specialist. To tell her story in the shape of a spiritual career has in turn allowed us to get
access to the social world she is engaged in and to understand how she has overcome changes
in political allegiances through her weikza affiliations. In all her stories, Medaw underlined the
agency of her spiritual partner, Bomingaung, in such a way that her undertakings appeared as
those of the weikza.
Moreover, the ritual role which Medaw has been consciously exposing to us is a distinctively
[38]
weikza one in more than one way: it is contrasted with the monastic role by not being submitted
to ordination and not formally asking for religious donation; it is different from the healer’s
role by not making use of exorcism; and it differs from spirit possession of the Burmese spirit
mediums by avoiding dance possessions and rather involving a kind of oracular possession.
Medaw also embodies the particular knowledge of the perfected being she is inspired by,
Bomingaung, who instructs her on the potent rituals she has to perform. In that way, she

articulates her ritual practice to the contemporary field of power. This knowledge available to
her through oracular possession may be said to locate her practice in some kind of esoteric

Medaw’s case study demonstrates that weikza path specialists who seek some sort of en-
[39]
lightenment are actually very much connected to this world, in which they are “tantricking”
through the agency of their metapersonal partners. While working for their ritual empow-
erment, they inscribe themselves in an interstitial space entangled in between institutional
Buddhism, Buddhist theravadin practices and an otherwise diversified ritual field. They tap
on spiritual potency and cosmic energy (dat) to manipulate mundane situations. In this way,
they render sensible weikzas’ distinctive potency and contribute to the continuous localization
of Buddhism in the Burmese world.
