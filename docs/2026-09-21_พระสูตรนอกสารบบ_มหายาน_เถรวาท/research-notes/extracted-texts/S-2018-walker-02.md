Siamese Manuscripts in Cambodian Collections

Trent Walker

The vast majority of traditional manuscripts that survive today in
Cambodia were copied within its modern borders in the late nineteenth
through mid-twentieth centuries. These manuscripts, including the bulk of the
present catalogue, comprise texts in Khmer, in Pali, and in a mix of the two.
Most were sponsored and copied by Khmer monks or laypeople. Not all
manuscripts in Cambodia were produced locally, however. There are also a
significant number of what we might call ‘Siamese’ manuscripts, particularly
in the libraries curated by the FEMC in Phnom Penh. These documents are
Siamese in the sense that they were fashioned by scribes in what is now
Central Thailand, largely in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. They
include texts composed in 1) Siamese (Central Thai), in 2) Pali, or 3) in a
bilingual Pali-Siamese format, and were brought to Cambodia, mostly during
the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, though a few may have been
imported earlier.

Siamese

The National Library of Cambodia includes an extraordinary
collection of folded-paper books or leporellos (Thai samut khaay;; Khmer
kramn) from Siam. Its greatest strength lies in Siamese epic literature in verse,
including a number of verse novels for which Khmer parallels and sometimes
even translations exist in the pralom lok ท น ห นิ ท or sastra Ipèñ genre (FEMC
classification code: II.1).° All told, the collection includes 76 Siamese folding
books, 72 of which are volumes from 28 different verse novels (FEMC
d.904—d.931). For many of the titles, however, only one or just a handful of
volumes are present. For instance, in the case of Inau (FEMC d.904), only 19
out of at least 42 volumes remain.° Nevertheless, this collection is one of the
largest gatherings of nineteenth-century Siamese literary manuscripts outside
of Thailand. The provenance of the collection is not known; although
Siamese texts and manuscripts played a significant role in the literary,
theatrical, and court cultures of Cambodia, particularly in the eighteenth
through early twentieth centuries, scant evidence survives with regard to how
these specific objects were used in Cambodia prior to their accession by the
National Library.

In addition to these verse novels, there are a handful of other
Siamese leporellos in the National Library collection. These include a legal
code (FEMC d.935); an herbal (FEMC d.933); a manual for countering bad
omens (utpdda) and averting the malicious influence of the planets
(nabagroh, FEMC d.932); a primer for students learning to read and write
Thai (a separate leporello, but also catalogued as FEMC d.932); and a
collection of poems connected to royal elephant rituals (FEMC d.934). This
last text is particularly significant for several reasons. One, it is among the
oldest dated Siamese leporellos in Cambodia, bearing a copy date of CS 1176,
equivalent to January 27, 1815 AD. Two, the first text it contains is partly in
a form of Khmer used in Siam during the Ayutthaya period. The poem, gam
chand" tusti sänvey, is attributed to Khun Deb Krahvi in late sixteenth
century.’ Though there is no evidence to suggest that this manuscript played
an important role in Cambodian letters, it is nevertheless part of the Khmer
literary heritage that extends beyond Cambodia’s modern borders.

Siamese texts printed on palm-leaves or thick paper in the likeness of
inscribed manuscripts (FEMC ๐ .23.111.2., 6 ๐ .24.111.1: ๑ ๐.25.111.2: ๑๐.26.111.6:

sermons were produced in Thailand throughout the twentieth century and
continue to be used today. In Cambodia, their closest equivalent are the
printed kramn, van, and satra produced by Jay Sen, a Khmer layman from
Vietnam who has manufactured and sold such objects throughout Cambodia
for the past few decades. A complete collection of such printed ‘manuscripts’
exists, albeit uncatalogued, in the Preah Vanarat Ken Vong Library; a second
complete set is held by the University of California, Berkeley.

Beyond the National Library, there are also two Siamese leporellos
in the collection of the National Museum. The first, FEMC d.586, is written
in an unsteady, late nineteenth-century hand in both ink and pencil, and
contains a number common didactic and narrative texts in Thai verse, some
attributed to Sunthorn Phu (1786-1855). The second, FEMC d.587, dates
from a similar period but is written in a much finer hand, with Siamese
portions in Thai script and Pali passages in Khom script. The text offers a
doctrinal exposition of celibacy (brahmacary") based on various Buddhist
sources.

Pali

The Pali manuscripts in the five major manuscript libraries in Phnom Penh
contain an exceptionally diverse range of texts, far greater than that
encountered in the provinces. Part of the reason for this is that these libraries
contain much of the former holdings of the pre-1975 Buddhist Institute and
the Royal Library before it. During the French Protectorate, George Cœdès
and others brought many Pali palm-leaf manuscripts from Siam to Cambodia,
both through commissioning Cambodian copies of Pali works transmitted in
Siam and through the direct importation of manuscripts. Many of these
manuscripts had likely never circulated in Cambodia prior to the early
twentieth century. In addition, learned Cambodian monks, particularly in the
eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, brought many Pali works back to
Cambodia upon completing their studies in Bangkok. Some of these
manuscripts also survive in Cambodian collections today.

For Pali works copied by or for Cambodians from Siamese
exemplars during the Protectorate, it is difficult to label these manuscripts as
solely ‘Siamese’, for in the twentieth century these manuscripts played a
significant role in the development of Khmer Buddhist modernism, including
the translation of the Tipitaka and other important ritual, narrative, and
exegetical works in Pali. However, there are also many manuscripts that were
clearly produced in Siam—and likely for Siamese use—that were
nonetheless brought to Cambodia, whether by monks or by colonial officials.

An especially important group of such manuscripts appears in the
collection of the Preah Vanarat Ken Vong Library. This collection contains
626 titles (and many more fascicles) of Pali manuscripts, namely those with

For example, manuscripts with code numbers FEMC A.1-52 are
almost exclusively those produced by scribes working for the Buddhist
Institute in Phnom Penh, particularly during the 1940s. Most of FEMC
A/A*.53-78 were also produced in Cambodia, but a few were certainly
brought from Siam. FEMC A.61, for instance, is a bilingual Pali-Siamese
manuscript, to be discussed in the following section. FEMC A.68, on the
other hand, is a 29-fascicle manuscript in monolingual Pali. The sixth fascicle
provides a name in Thai script, nen ti, presumably referring to the Siamese
novice monk Ti who served as the scribe; other fascicles give the scribe’s
name in Khom Thai script instead. On the cover of a few of the fascicles,
however, are more extensive colophons in Siamese in Khom Thai script that
illuminate the history of the manuscript. The one on fascicle 19, for instance,
reads as follows:

dan brah sangaraja méa% kambhijadipvati ma rien dhamm nai
[kru]n st ayuddayä san vai sämrap° brah sassana

The sañgharäja of mueang Kambujadhipati, who came to study the
Dhamma in Krun Sri Ayudhyä, sponsored [this manuscript] for the
sake of the dispensation [of the Buddha].

The date for this manuscript is uncertain. Fascicle 4 includes a
partially effaced set of numbers in ink that appears to read 2282 [BE], i.e.
1745 AD, but this reading is highly conjectural. The use of ‘Krun Sri
Ayudhya’ also suggests a date in the late Ayutthaya era, which ended in 1767,
though this term was used in the Thonburi and Rattanakosin periods as well.
More convincing is the rounded Khom script of the Pali text itself, which
corresponds paleographically to manuscripts of the mid- to late eighteenth
century in Siam. If this dating is correct, then FEMC A.68 is among the
oldest manuscripts in Cambodia.

Perhaps even more importantly, the colophon and the names of the
Thai scribes included in FEMC A.68 confirm that Cambodian monks, well
before the arrival of the French, travelled to Siam to study and commission
manuscripts to be brought back to their homeland. We do not know the
identity or specific rank of the sañgharäja in question, though we know from
other sources that Cambodian monks with the title of sangharaja studied
Siamese and Pali texts from Ayutthaya in the eighteenth century.’

In addition to FEMC A.68, a few other manuscripts in the FEMC
A/A*.1-78 range were also likely produced in Siam. These include FEM
A.69, 70, and 72, as well as FEMC A*.73. Several of these are quite old as
well and feature the variant of Khom script popular in the late Ayutthaya and
early Rattanakosin periods. By the same token, manuscripts with codes in the
FEMC B/B*.1-51 and FEMC C/C*.1-119 ranges are almost entirely imports
from Siam. In fact, only a handful of manuscripts in this range feature Khmer
colophons or other features that indicate they were produced in Cambodia.
These include FEMC B.1, B.12, B.18, B.28, B.33, B.39, C.14, C.80, and
C*.106, many of which include dated colophons in Khmer ranging from
1903 to 1923 AD. The remaining 160 or so manuscripts in these ranges were
largely produced in Siam in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Moreover, most of manuscripts in this group were proofread in Siam
before being brought to Cambodia. Evidence for this appears in the Siamese
phrases, written in Khom Thai script, that appear on the cover leaves,
including dän° lév° (‘proofread’ [past tense], found on around 70 manuscripts)
and dan’ lèv° täm° japap’ or dan’ lèv° tè japäp° (‘proofread against the parent
manuscript [chpdp]’, FEMC C.26, 35, 70, 94, 96; FEMC B.23). The

Pali text. Occasionally the cover leaf will also indicate the number or location

Bilingual Pali-Siamese

As suggested above, not all of the Pali manuscripts catalogued as
‘Pali’ by the FEMC are only in that language. There are a number of
manuscripts, particularly in the Preah Vanarat Ken Vong Library, that
include both Siamese and Pali portions. There are two main types of bilingual
Pali-Siamese manuscripts in the collection. The first are word-by-word or,
more accurately, phrase-by-phrase translations of Pali texts into Siamese. In
these manuscripts, the Siamese and Pali passages are roughly equivalent, and
both are inscribed directly onto the palm leaves and are arranged on the same
line in alternating fashion. The second are annotated versions of monolingual
Pali texts. In these cases, by contrast, only the Pali text is inscribed. The
Siamese and Pali annotations are applied later in black ink, and appear in
between the lines or on the margins.

Phrase-by-phrase translations from Pali into Khmer, Lanna, Lao,
Mon, or Siamese employ three main techniques. One, they adopt a set of
particles, largely unnecessary in ordinary vernacular prose, to mark Pali
grammatical features such as case, tense, and number. Two, they

of the vernacular language. Three, they gloss each Pali word or phrase with
Pali and/or vernacular equivalents, with the vernacular meanings expanded or
contracted as necessary.” Such phrase-by-phrase translations are known as
samray or prè lot prayog in Khmer, as krdai in Mon, as nisäy (cf. Pali
nissaya) or nämasäbd" in Lanna and Lao, and as Plé yak sabd" or Plè toy
byäñjanah in modern Thai.

In addition to phrase-by-phrase Pali-Siamese translations, the other
form of bilingual Pali-Siamese manuscripts present in Cambodian collections
are annotated versions of Pali texts. Unlike the previous form, which are
inscribed in alternating in Khom Pali and Khom Thai script on the same line,
the only inscribed part of the annotated manuscripts is the main Pali text in
Khom Pali script. When each of these manuscripts was first produced, there
were no annotations present, only the Pali text. The next step in the process
was a proofreading stage, as discussed above, in which the manuscript was
checked against a reference text and errors were corrected in black ink, again
in Khom Pali script. Occasionally a yellow paste was also used to blot out
extra letters or other mistakes. Only then could the process of annotation
begin.

A single annotator typically includes notes in both a vernacular

to unpack, gloss, and clarify the text. Drawing on the three techniques of
inserting grammatical particles, rearranging the syntax, and adding glosses as
witnessed in bilingual Pali-Siamese texts, the annotators adopt a highly
compressed version of the Plé yak sabd" style. They accomplish this through
using abbreviations for grammatical particles, adopting numbers and letters
as codes for syntactical rearrangement, and including glosses only for
selected terms and phrases. The ingenuity of this system provide a precise
glimpse of Siamese and Cambodian monastic education in Southeast Asia
from the eighteenth through early twentieth centuries.

proficient in Siamese, it is not inconceivable that students of Pali in
Cambodia would refer to the Siamese annotations as well as adding Khmer
