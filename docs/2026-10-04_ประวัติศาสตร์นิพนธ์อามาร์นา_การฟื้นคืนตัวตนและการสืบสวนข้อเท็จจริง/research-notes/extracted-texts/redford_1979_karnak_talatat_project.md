# The Akhenaten Temple Project and Karnak Excavations
By Donald B. Redford

Scholarly study of the reign of Akhenaten, pharaoh of Egypt (ca. 1375-1357 B.C.), has focused with justification on the last twelve years of his rule, when he abandoned Thebes to found a new capital at Tell el-Amarna. Yet it was in Thebes, ancient capital of the Egyptian Empire, that the young Amenhotep IV spent the first five years of his reign. Here he initiated the revolutionary religious and artistic program that culminated in the worship of the Aten to the exclusion of the old pantheon, especially the state god Amun.

Until recently, our knowledge of Akhenaten's Theban monuments was frustratingly limited. Early in the 19th century, travelers and archaeologists noted thousands of decorated sandstone blocks of uniform size embedded in later constructions throughout the great Temple of Amun at Karnak. These stones, known in archaeological parlance as talatat (from the Arabic word for "three," referring to their length of three spans or about 52 cm), were the building units of Akhenaten's demolished temples.

<!-- Page 55 -->
When Horemheb and the subsequent kings of the 19th and 20th Dynasties dismantled Akhenaten's shrines, they used these talatat by the tens of thousands as core filling inside their own monumental pylons (especially the Second, Ninth, and Tenth Pylons at Karnak) and in the foundations of great hypostyle halls. The talatat had standard dimensions: approximately 52 cm long, 26 cm wide, and 20 cm high. Because a single workman could easily lift and carry a stone of this size (weighing roughly 50 to 55 kilograms), Amenhotep IV was able to erect massive complexes in a remarkably short time.

The Akhenaten Temple Project (ATP) was initiated in 1966 under the visionary leadership of Ray Winfield Smith, under the auspices of the University Museum of the University of Pennsylvania. The enormous challenge facing the project was nothing less than reassembling an architectural jigsaw puzzle composed of tens of thousands of loose, scattered blocks. Over 40,000 decorated talatat had been recovered from Karnak, dispersed in government storehouses, museum basements, and open-air magazines.

<!-- Page 56 -->
To cope with this vast mass of data, Smith pioneered the application of computer technology to archaeological research. Every decorated talatat was photographed at a uniform scale of 1:10 on 35 mm black-and-white film. A comprehensive descriptive code was devised to record every visual feature on each stone: architectural elements, religious iconography, representations of the king, queen, royal daughters, courtiers, priests, offerings, and hieroglyphic inscriptions.

These descriptive profiles were transferred onto punch cards and processed by computer systems in Cairo. The computer could search the entire corpus in seconds, identifying potential matches based on joining relief lines, continuous color bands, matching scale of figures, and complementary inscriptions.

<!-- Page 57 -->
As the fragments were assembled on photographic boards, four distinct temple structures built by Akhenaten at East Karnak began to emerge from oblivion:
1. The Gem-pa-Aten ("The Aten is Found in the House of the Aten"), the principal sun temple with its vast pillared colonnade and open-air offering tables.
2. The Hwt-benben ("Mansion of the Benben"), a sun shrine deeply connected with the solar cult.
3. The Rud-menu ("Firm of Monuments of the Aten Forever").
4. The Teni-menu ("Exalted of Monuments of the Aten Forever").

One of the most astonishing discoveries made during the reconstruction of the Hwt-benben was that this sanctuary was dedicated exclusively to Queen Nefertiti. In the reliefs from this building, Nefertiti and her daughters (Meritaten and Meketaten) officiate at the daily solar liturgy completely alone, with no representation of Akhenaten whatsoever! Nefertiti is shown offering incense, pouring libations, and standing before the altars heaped with flowers, fruit, and meat offerings beneath the protective, life-giving rays of the Aten.

<!-- Page 58 -->
This extraordinary prominence of Nefertiti in the Theban phase of the reign proved that her exceptional religious status was not an afterthought developed at Amarna, but an integral component of the Atenist theology from the very inception of the religious movement. In the talatat reliefs, Nefertiti appears nearly twice as often as Akhenaten himself in certain architectural contexts.

Moreover, the ATP reconstructions revealed scenes of Akhenaten's Heb-Sed (Thirty-Year Jubilee Festival), celebrated in his fourth or fifth regnal year at Karnak, long before the traditional thirty-year milestone. The talatat show the king in the traditional sed-festival cloak, accompanied by the royal family, celebrating the renewal of his divine kingship in the presence of the radiant solar disc.

<!-- Page 59 -->
The subsequent excavation of East Karnak by our team confirmed the ground plan of the Gem-pa-Aten. What had once been regarded as a complete and successful obliteration of the Amarna heresy by Horemheb has paradoxically turned into an unparalleled archaeological repository. By enclosing the talatat inside dark, dry pylon walls, the destroyers preserved the brilliant paint, fine incised reliefs, and delicate hieroglyphs from the weathering effects of the sun, rain, and human vandalism, leaving for modern science the means to resurrect Akhenaten's lost monuments.
