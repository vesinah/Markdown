# The Amarna Succession
By James P. Allen

The transition of power following the death of Akhenaten in his seventeenth regnal year has been characterized by John Baines as "a maze with few markers and many pitfalls." The key to navigating this chronological and epigraphic labyrinth lies in the precise distinction between two royal figures who shared the prenomen Ankhkheperure: Ankhkheperure Smenkhkare Djeserkheperu and Ankhkheperure Neferneferuaten.

For decades, scholars treated these two names as variant designations of a single male pharaoh, Smenkhkare, who was presumed to be a younger brother or son of Akhenaten, married to Princess Meritaten. However, rigorous epigraphic analysis initiated by Rolf Krauss (1978) and refined by Marc Gabolde (1998) and William J. Murnane has established beyond reasonable doubt that two distinct rulers held power between Akhenaten and Tutankhamun: one male (Smenkhkare) and one female (Neferneferuaten).

## Epigraphic Evidence for a Female King
The decisive epigraphic breakthrough came with the recognition of grammatical gender markers in the royal titulary of Ankhkheperure. In Egyptian hieroglyphic grammar, the suffix -t marks the feminine gender. On numerous monuments, jar sealings, and reused funerary objects from KV62, the epithets associated with Ankhkheperure contain explicit feminine markers:
1. *Ꜣḫ.t-n-hjs* ("Effective for her husband"), an epithet that can only be borne by a female ruler married to a male king.
2. *Mrit-Nfr-ḫpr.w-RꜤ* or *Mrit-wꜤ-n-RꜤ* ("Beloved of Waenre/Akhenaten"), where the participle *mrit* carries the feminine ending .t.
3. The prenomen itself is frequently expanded with the feminine epithet *S-nfr-nfr.w-Jtn* or written with the divine determinative of a goddess.

<!-- Page 11 -->
In contrast, monuments naming Ankhkheperure Smenkhkare Djeserkheperu never exhibit feminine grammatical gender markers. Furthermore, the epithet *Djeserkheperu* ("Holy of Forms") is exclusive to Smenkhkare, whereas the epithet *Akhet-en-hyes* / *Merit-Neferkheperure* is exclusive to Neferneferuaten.

This dichotomy demonstrates the existence of two distinct political entities:
- King A: Ankhkheperure Smenkhkare Djeserkheperu (Male ruler).
- King B: Ankhkheperure Neferneferuaten (Female ruler).

## The Two Candidates for King Neferneferuaten
If King Neferneferuaten was a woman, who was she? Two primary candidates have dominated modern scholarly discourse:
1. Princess Meritaten: The eldest daughter of Akhenaten and Nefertiti. She was elevated to the rank of Great Royal Wife following the disappearance of Kiya and the elevation of her mother. Rolf Krauss and Aidan Dodson have argued that Meritaten was crowned as king under the name Neferneferuaten following the death of Smenkhkare.
2. Queen Nefertiti: Akhenaten's chief consort, who bore the extended name Neferneferuaten-Nefertiti since regnal Year 5. Marc Gabolde and Dimitri Laboury argue that Nefertiti was elevated to co-regent during Akhenaten's lifetime and ruled as sole pharaoh after his death.

<!-- Page 13 -->
The case for Nefertiti is epigraphically compelling. Nefertiti had borne the specific name *Nfr-nfr.w-Jtn* for over a decade. When a ruler named King Neferneferuaten appears, the most parsimonious explanation is that the queen simply retained her well-known personal name upon assuming the royal kilt and nemes headdress.

Moreover, the graffito discovered in the Theban tomb of Pere (TT 139) records a "Regnal Year 3, third month of Inundation, day 10" under the King of Upper and Lower Egypt, Lord of the Two Lands, Ankhkheperure-mery-Neferkheperure, Son of Ra, Neferneferuaten-mery-Waenre. This inscription proves that the female pharaoh ruled for at least three full years, presiding over a temple of Amun in Thebes and initiating the initial reconciliation with the traditional priesthood before Tutankhamun's reign.

## The Sequence of Succession
Based on the synthesis of epigraphic, archaeological, and chronological markers, the following sequence emerges:
1. Akhenaten (Years 1 to 17): The sole initiator of Atenism.
2. Smenkhkare (Short co-regency or ephemeral reign ca. Years 15-16 of Akhenaten).
3. Neferneferuaten / Nefertiti (Co-regent in Year 16-17, followed by an independent reign of approximately 3 years, ca. 1336-1334 B.C.).
4. Tutankhaten / Tutankhamun (Accession at approximately age 9, ca. 1334 B.C., overseen by Vizier Ay and General Horemheb).
5. Ay (ca. 1325-1321 B.C.).
6. Horemheb (ca. 1321-1292 B.C.), who erased the memory of all four preceding Amarna rulers and dated his accession directly to the death of Amenhotep III.

This reconstruction harmonizes the epigraphic data of the Karnak talatat, the Amarna Boundary Stelae, the Dayr al-Barsha graffito, and the contents of KV62, resolving the historical puzzle of the Amarna succession.
