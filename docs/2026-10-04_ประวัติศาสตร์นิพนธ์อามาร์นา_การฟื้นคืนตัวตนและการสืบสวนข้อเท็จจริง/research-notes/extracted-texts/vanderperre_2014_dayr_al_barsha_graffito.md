# The Year 16 Graffito of Akhenaten in Dayr Abū Ḥinnis
By Athena Van der Perre

## Introduction
The chronology and political history of the final years of the Amarna Period (regnal years 12 to 17 of Akhenaten and the subsequent succession) have long constituted one of the most enigmatic chapters in ancient Egyptian historiography. For decades, the apparent disappearance of Queen Nefertiti from the monument record after regnal Year 12 led Egyptologists to hypothesize either her premature death, perhaps during the epidemic that swept the Near East, or her political downfall and disgrace.

In 2012, during an epigraphic survey conducted by the Dayr al-Barsha Project of the KU Leuven under the direction of Harco Willems, an exceptional building graffito written in black ink hieratic was discovered in an underground limestone quarry at Dayr Abū Ḥinnis (Quarry 320), situated just north of Tell el-Amarna on the east bank of the Nile.

<!-- Page 68 -->
## The Epigraphic Text of the Graffito
The graffito is located on a pillar inside Quarry 320. It consists of three lines of hieratic script, meticulously penned by an ancient quarry scribe, recording the extraction of stone blocks for a major building project in the capital city of Akhetaten.

Line 1: Regnal Year 16, third month of Akhet, day 15 (ḥꜢt-sp 16 Ꜣbd 3 Ꜣḫt sw 15). Under the Majesty of the King of Upper and Lower Egypt, Living in Truth, Lord of the Two Lands, Neferkheperure Waenre, Son of Ra, Living in Truth, Lord of Crowns, Akhenaten, Great in his Lifetime.

Line 2: Great Royal Wife, his beloved, Lady of the Two Lands, Neferneferuaten Nefertiti, living forever and eternity (ḥmt nsw wrt mrt.f nbt tꜢwy Nfr-nfrw-Jtn Nfrt-jytj Ꜥnḫ.tj ḏt r nḥḥ).

Line 3: Extracting stone for the works of the Great Mansion of the Aten in Akhetaten (ḥwt-Ꜣṯ n Jtn m Ꜣḫt-Jtn).

## Historical Implications of the Discovery
The historical impact of this brief administrative inscription is profound. Prior to this discovery, the latest unequivocally dated attestation of Nefertiti was found in the tomb of Meryre II at Amarna (Tomb 2), where she is depicted alongside Akhenaten receiving foreign tribute during the famous "Durbar" of regnal Year 12, second month of Peret, day 8.

The total absence of securely dated monuments mentioning Nefertiti after Year 12 had formed the cornerstone of two rival schools of thought:
1. The Disgrace Theory: Advanced early by J.D.S. Pendlebury and Cyril Aldred, which proposed that Nefertiti fell into ideological disagreement with Akhenaten over religious concessions to Amun and was exiled to the Northern Palace. This theory was largely abandoned when it was proven in the 1980s that the defaced names in the Northern Palace belonged to secondary queen Kiya, not Nefertiti.
2. The Plague/Death Theory: Promoted by Aidan Dodson, Rolf Krauss, and Nicholas Reeves, arguing that Nefertiti died shortly after Year 12, possibly succumbing to the virulent epidemic attested across Syria and Egypt, necessitating the elevation of royal daughters (Meritaten and Ankhesenpaaten) to official ceremonial roles.

<!-- Page 70 -->
The Dayr Abū Ḥinnis graffito conclusively disproves both hypotheses. In regnal Year 16, Month 3 of Akhet—a mere year to fourteen months before Akhenaten's final Year 17 and death—Nefertiti was alive, healthy, and still actively bearing her full official titles as Great Royal Wife, his beloved, Lady of the Two Lands.

## Nefertiti's Transformation into Pharaoh
The verification that Nefertiti survived into Year 16 provides the crucial missing epigraphic bridge for the hypothesis, championed by Marc Gabolde, Dimitri Laboury, and James P. Allen, that Nefertiti did not die, but underwent a monumental political metamorphosis: she was elevated to the status of co-regent and ultimately succeeded Akhenaten as female king under the coronation name Ankhkheperure Neferneferuaten.

As Gabolde has argued, when Akhenaten sought a reliable co-ruler to secure his theological revolution in the face of domestic and international instability, his most loyal, experienced, and religiously elevated partner was none other than Nefertiti. Her royal nomen as queen, Neferneferuaten ("Beautiful are the Beauties of the Aten"), was seamlessly adopted as her royal prenomen/nomen upon her ascension to the throne.

<!-- Page 71 -->
Furthermore, the discovery explains why the burial equipment of Neferneferuaten was repurposed for Tutankhamun's tomb (KV62). When the female pharaoh died, her successor oversaw her burial, and unused or superseded items carrying the female royal titulary (with feminine grammatical endings such as mryt-wꜤ-n-rꜤ) were subsequently adapted for the burial of Tutankhaten.

The Year 16 graffito from Dayr Abū Ḥinnis stands as one of the most transformative epigraphic discoveries in Amarna studies of the 21st century, fundamentally reshaping our understanding of the succession, the female pharaoh, and the twilight of the Amarna revolution.
